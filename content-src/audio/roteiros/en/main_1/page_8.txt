## [01] André Breton  (805 car., ~0.8 min)

André Breton, the founder of Surrealism, saw cinema as a way of liberating the subconscious. As his collaborator Philippe Soupault wrote: "We believed that cinema would present extraordinary possibilities for expressing, transfiguring and realizing dreams."

In nineteen twenty-four, Breton wrote the Surrealist Manifesto and, that same year, filmmaker René Clair (eighteen ninety-eight to nineteen eighty-one) directed Entr'acte, from nineteen twenty-four, a blend of slapstick comedy and dream logic. Made to be shown during the intermission of Francis Picabia's ballet Relâche, then playing at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées in Paris, the film features cameos by Picabia and the artists Man Ray (eighteen ninety to nineteen seventy-six) and Marcel Duchamp (eighteen eighty-seven to nineteen sixty-eight).

## [02] Learn more: The Surrealist Manifesto  (872 car., ~0.9 min)

Learn more: The Surrealist Manifesto.

Published in nineteen twenty-four by André Breton, the Surrealist Manifesto brought the world a new way of looking at art.

Following Dadaism (a movement that advocated opposition to any kind of balance), Surrealism imposed so-called psychic automatism, "in its pure state, by which one proposes to express - verbally, by means of the written word, or in any other manner - the actual functioning of thought; dictated by thought, in the absence of any control exercised by reason, exempt from any aesthetic or moral concern."

According to Breton, he and the writer Soupault gave the name Surrealism to the new mode of expression they had at their disposal, in homage to Guillaume Apollinaire.

In this manifesto, the Surrealist principles are declared, such as freedom from logic and the embrace of a higher reality, called the "marvelous."

## [03] Germaine Dulac  (883 car., ~0.9 min)

Picabia conceived the film together with Clair and intended it as an attack on Breton, yet Entr'acte is considered a Surrealist film. It opens with a superimposition of images and relies on skillful editing to create surprising effects: a ballerina turns into a bearded man and a hearse travels along the tracks of a roller coaster.

Although director Germaine Dulac (eighteen eighty-two to nineteen forty-two) was never part of the movement, she too was interested in the unconscious and used Antonin Artaud's Surrealist screenplay for La coquille et le clergyman (The Seashell and the Clergyman, nineteen twenty-eight), a fragmented, lyrical narrative centered on the erotic hallucinations of a clergyman. However, Artaud felt that Dulac's fluid camerawork and optical effects were incompatible with Surrealism, and he staged a loud protest with other artists at the film's premiere.

## [04] Un chien andalou  (2301 car., ~2.3 min)

The Seashell and the Clergyman was overshadowed by the hugely acclaimed Un chien andalou, from nineteen twenty-nine, directed by Luis [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]] (nineteen hundred to nineteen eighty-three), who also wrote the screenplay together with Salvador Dalí. The film helped the two of them become accepted as Surrealists, and [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]]'s second film, L'Âge d'Or, from nineteen thirty, was also faithful to the movement.

The film caused a scandal and there was a riot at its premiere, after which it was banned for almost fifty years. [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]] broke with the Surrealists in nineteen thirty-two but continued to make films in that vein throughout his career. Although he later went into exile from his native Spain, in nineteen sixty he accepted an invitation from Francisco Franco's government to make a film.

[CITAÇÃO] Surrealism is based on the belief in the superior reality of certain forms of previously neglected associations, in the omnipotence of dream (...).

said André Breton.

The director showed he had lost none of his subversive verve, and the film Viridiana, from nineteen sixty-one, - with its parody of Leonardo da Vinci's painting The Last Supper, featuring a band of rowdy beggars in place of the apostles - was banned in the country until Franco's death.

Surrealism gradually made its way into commercial cinema. In nineteen forty-five, Dalí was hired to create a dream sequence for Alfred Hitchcock's (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty) Freudian thriller Spellbound, from nineteen forty-five. The following year, he collaborated with Walt Disney (nineteen oh-one to nineteen sixty-six) on an animated short, Destino, from two thousand three, which was shelved until two thousand three, when it was finally completed by the studio's animators.

The influence of Surrealist cinema is significant, and its dreamlike imagery, non sequiturs, use of Freudian symbolism and bizarre superimpositions are present in the films of directors such as Federico Fellini (nineteen twenty to nineteen ninety-three), Kenneth Anger, from nineteen twenty-seven, and David Lynch, from nineteen forty-six.

In the summer of nineteen twenty-nine, a Parisian audience gathered for the screening of a new film. Backstage, the director waited with a bucket full of stones to throw at the spectators in case the picture was poorly received.

## [05] Luis Buñuel  (1555 car., ~1.6 min)

On screen, a razor is honed on a leather strop. A man with a cigarette dangling from his mouth tests the blade on his thumb, drawing blood. A young woman stares at an invisible audience, expressionless. The man's hand parts her eyelids. A full Moon hangs in the sky and a pointed cloud passes across it. The razor slices the eyeball.

In his autobiography, Luis [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]] describes how he and the artist Salvador Dalí worked on Un Chien Andalou: "Our only rule was very simple: no idea or image that might lend itself to a rational explanation of any kind would be accepted. We had to open all doors to the irrational and keep only those images that surprised us, without trying to explain why." The film offers the illusion of a narrative: it begins with the simplest of all invocations of a story, "Once upon a time... ", and this intertitle imposes an arbitrary and meaningless chronology.

[CITAÇÃO] Surrealism is destructive, but it destroys only what it considers to be shackles limiting our vision.

said Salvador Dalí.

The film is a stream of subversive images: a man in partial drag, wearing a bonnet and a short skirt over a suit, rides a bicycle through the streets of Paris; an enigmatic young woman pokes at a severed hand lying in the street; ants crawl out of a hole in the palm of a living person's hand; to reach the object of his desire, a man has to pull harnesses tied to two priests and two grand pianos, each with a dead donkey on top. Un Chien Andalou may not have been the first Surrealist film, but it is a landmark for the movement that inspired it.
