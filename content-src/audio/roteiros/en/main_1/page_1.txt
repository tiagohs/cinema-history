## [01] Pathé Frères  (962 car., ~1.0 min)

Cinema at once created and fed an appetite for spectacle, offering the chance to recreate the past, reimagine the present and envision the future. The pioneers of this art were not to be found in Hollywood, which at the time did not even exist as a center of film production, but in Europe. Among the most notable were the French filmmakers Georges [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] (eighteen sixty-one to nineteen thirty-eight), Charles [[pron: Pathé|Pa-tay]] (eighteen sixty-three to nineteen fifty-seven) and Ferdinand Zecca (eighteen sixty-four to nineteen forty-seven).

The latter directed the one-minute short film A la conquête de l'air (Conquering the Skies, nineteen oh-one), in which he appears flying over the Parisian neighborhood of Belleville in a bizarre flying machine. The film was made for the production company [[pron: Pathé|Pa-tay]] Frères, founded in eighteen ninety-six by Charles [[pron: Pathé|Pa-tay]] and his brother Émile. In a nod to their origins as restaurant owners, they used a rooster, Le Coq, as their logo.

## [02] Roll film  (722 car., ~0.7 min)

We can mention other names that were unknown until then: Thomas Edison (eighteen forty-seven to nineteen thirty-one), W.K.L Dickson (eighteen fifty-four to nineteen thirty-two), Louis Le Prince (eighteen forty-one to eighteen ninety) and Francis Doublier (eighteen seventy-eight to nineteen forty-eight). All of them contributed in some way to the creation of the so-called seventh art, and for that reason we cannot say it was created by a single person, nor does it have a precise date of birth. In eighteen eighty-four, George Eastman invented roll film. In the same decade, Thomas Edison and his assistant W. K. L. Dickson invented the kinetoscope. Many other inventions emerged in parallel or built upon earlier ones.

## [03] December 28, 1895  (1006 car., ~1.0 min)

But one date went down in history: December twenty-eighth, eighteen ninety-five, widely regarded by historians as the birth date of cinema. That night, at the Grand Café in Paris, they showed a short program of their documentary films to a paying audience; soon after came the now-famous L'Arrivée d'un train en gare de La Ciotat (The Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat, eighteen ninety-six).

After attending the first public screenings of the [[pron: Lumière|Loo-mee-air]] brothers' films in eighteen ninety-five, theater producer and magician Georges [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] quickly recognized cinema's potential as a vehicle for illusion and fantasy. [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]]'s signature was his love of illusionism, blending the fantastic with the macabre. The silent film L'Homme à la tête de caoutchouc (The Man with the Rubber Head, nineteen oh-one) features [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] as a scientist who attaches a rubber tube to his own severed head to inflate it with a bellows. The experiment is then repeated by one of his assistants, who inflates the head until it explodes.

## [04] Beginning of the Indian film industry  (1523 car., ~1.5 min)

In Le Voyage dans la Lune (A Trip to the Moon, nineteen oh-two), the image of the rocket landing in the eye of the Moon is a typical example of [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]]'s blend of the eccentric and the visceral. Yet despite all the fascinating cinematic innovations this filmmaker developed and explored, his films never managed to break free entirely from their theatrical origins.

In India, pioneering director D. G. Phalke (eighteen seventy to nineteen forty-four), enchanted by one of the first film versions of the life of Christ, learned the rudiments of filmmaking in order to make Raja Harishchandra, from nineteen thirteen. This elaborate period drama, based on Hindu mythology, marks the beginning of the Indian film industry.

Italy's film pioneers were drawn to the mythical and the spectacular. Dante's work proved a great source of inspiration, and L'Inferno (Dante's Inferno, nineteen eleven) is considered the country's first feature film.

In the United States, The Great Train Robbery, from nineteen oh-three, directed by Edwin S. Porter (eighteen seventy to nineteen forty-one), is considered the first American film with a narrative. Porter's innovative shooting and editing techniques, his location shots and the Western format made it extremely thrilling for audiences of the time. As technology advanced, directors were able to make longer films, sometimes turning to literature for inspiration.

## [05] Serial films  (814 car., ~0.8 min)

As technology advanced, the length of films increased, making possible the emergence of serial films. One of the most successful examples of the format is the Fantômas series (nineteen thirteen to nineteen fourteen), divided into five parts and based on one of the most popular characters in French crime fiction, played by René Navarre (eighteen seventy-seven to nineteen sixty-eight). Directed by Louis Feuillade (eighteen seventy-three to nineteen twenty-six), each part ran about an hour and always ended on a cliffhanger. The prolific French director also made Les Vampires (The Vampires, nineteen fifteen). The suspense techniques developed by Feuillade would later influence masters such as Fritz Lang (eighteen ninety to nineteen seventy-six) and Alfred Hitchcock (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty).

## [06] Learn more: Serial Films  (2048 car., ~2.0 min)

Learn more: Serial Films.

Between nineteen oh-eight and nineteen twenty, cinema developed through so-called serial films, or serials, which became the main attraction of movie houses. This innovation, however, was not an invention of cinema, since at the time, especially in France, biweekly installments of crime and adventure stories were popular and had spread throughout Europe. It was in France, therefore, that the serial was born, with Nick Carter in le roi des détectives, in nineteen oh-eight, based on those installments and directed by Victorin Jasset, an imaginative director who often improvised the script during shooting. Following the series' success, several imitations were made in Europe, such as Raffles and Sherlock Holmes in Denmark, and Nick Carter in Germany.

The most famous serial of the era, however, was Fantômas, by Louis Feuillade, adapted from the serialized novel by Pierre Souvestre and Marcel Allain.

Serial films reached the United States around nineteen twelve, with What Happened to Mary, from nineteen twelve, a co-production of Edison Studios Company and the magazine The Ladies World, the first American serial. While serials were playing in theaters, they were usually accompanied by the same story published in chapters in newspapers.

Serials proliferated, producing their masterpieces, such as The Million Dollar Mystery, from nineteen fourteen, produced by the Thanhouser Film Corporation, which earned ten times its cost, and The Perils of Pauline, a [[pron: Pathé|Pa-tay]] / Hearst production starring Pearl White, who soon became an idol of young audiences.

Serials became very popular among children and young people in the first half of the twentieth century, and film screenings, including in Brazil, were generally preceded by an episode of a serial until the sixties, as they were known back then. They were later acquired by TV channels and shown in weekly or daily episodes. Most were Westerns, but there was a wide range of genres: dramas, science fiction, espionage, jungle adventures, as well as book adaptations.

## [07] The first film with some underwater sequences  (1570 car., ~1.6 min)

The opportunity to make longer films, the rise of the Hollywood studios and bigger budgets encouraged filmmakers to produce literary adaptations such as twenty thousand Leagues Under the Sea, from nineteen sixteen, directed by Stuart Paton (eighteen eighty-three to nineteen forty-four) and based on the eighteen sixty-nine novel by Jules Verne, whose book The Mysterious Island, from eighteen seventy-four, was also adapted for the screen. Paton joined forces with two other British immigrants in the United States, the brothers John and George Williamson, to create the first film with some underwater sequences.

The appetite for spectacle continued to be explored in epics such as The Ten Commandments, from nineteen twenty-three, and Ben-Hur: A Tale of the Christ, from nineteen twenty-five, forming what would later be known as the sword-and-sandal genre. The latter proved a troubled and expensive production for Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, which had just completed its merger. To make the chariot race more exciting, the studio offered a one hundred dollars prize to the winner, and the terrible crash that resulted remained in the film's final cut. The spirit of pioneering director Georges [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] can be glimpsed in these nineteen twenties epics and, later, in the special effects created by Ray Harryhausen in the nineteen fifties and nineteen sixties. Filmmaking techniques grew more complex and narrative structures increasingly sophisticated, yet cinema's creative foundation, reinventing and reinterpreting reality, has remained a constant since its earliest days.
