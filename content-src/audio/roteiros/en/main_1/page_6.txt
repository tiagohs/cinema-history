## [00] Opening  (331 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero one: From eighteen ninety-five to nineteen twenty-nine. Chapter six: Silent Comedy.

One of cinema's first and most popular genres, comedy has been part of film from the very beginning. Names like Charlie Chaplin, Harold Lloyd and Buster Keaton are forever etched in history, influencing future comedians on the big screen.

## [01] Keystone  (1752 car., ~1.8 min)

Since the earliest days of the seventh art, a number of films, such as L'Arroseur arrosé (The Sprinkler Sprinkled, eighteen ninety-five), directed by Louis [[pron: Lumière|Loo-mee-air]] (eighteen sixty-four to nineteen forty-eight), in which a gardener suffers a series of mishaps with his garden hose, played with single-scene visual gags.

These early experiments were a great success; however, several actors who were hugely popular during the silent era are now forgotten or overshadowed by both Charlie Chaplin (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen seventy-seven) and Buster Keaton (eighteen ninety-five to nineteen sixty-six). The daredevil performances of Harold Lloyd (eighteen ninety-three to nineteen seventy-one) in Safety Last!, from nineteen twenty-three, and of Raymond Griffith (eighteen ninety-five to nineteen fifty-seven) in Hands Up!, from nineteen twenty-six, are typical examples of inventive comic routines, and they also serve to remind us how cultural icons can fade from public memory.

During the silent film era, it was in Europe that comedy began to develop the logic and form it would take on in the nineteen twenties, with comic actors such as André Deed (eighteen seventy-nine to nineteen forty) and, above all, the hugely popular Max Linder (eighteen eighty-three to nineteen twenty-five) making the first forays into the genre.

However, it was Mack Sennett's Keystone studios that developed the traits that would define silent comedy and began producing films of this kind on an industrial scale. The Keystone Kops series proved to be one of audiences' favorites after the studio was founded in nineteen twelve.

Learn more: A Classic That Came at a Price.

Keaton made several films that can be counted among the best (and funniest) in the entire history of cinema, but none of them is a stronger candidate for first place than the timeless masterpiece The General, from nineteen twenty-six. This is not merely due to the constant stream of great gags, nor to the way they arise entirely from the situations and the character instead of existing in isolation from the plot. What makes this film so extraordinary is the fact that it is superlative in every respect: humor, suspense, historical reconstruction, character study, visual beauty and technical precision.

Much of this pleasure comes from the story itself, inspired by a book about the real exploits of a group of Northern soldiers who, during the Civil War, disguised themselves as Southerners to steal a train, which they drove north to rejoin their Union comrades until they were captured and executed. Since he was making a comedy, Keaton left out the executions and shifted the heroic perspective to that of a Southerner, Johnny Gray, an engineer who, stoically if not absurdly, decides to pursue the Union spies single-handedly when they steal his locomotive - "The General" - and, aboard it, Annabelle Lee (Marion Mack), the other love of his life.

The film was a box-office flop at the time of its release, with audiences more interested in Flesh and the Devil, from nineteen twenty-six, starring Greta Garbo (nineteen oh-five to nineteen ninety).

Nor did it impress the critics, who saw little humor in the engineer's antics. Keaton was devastated by this reception, as he believed the film was his masterpiece. Today, however, The General appears on numerous "best films" lists and, in nineteen eighty-nine, it was selected by the Library of Congress for the first batch of American films to be preserved in the United States National Film Registry.

## [03] Laurel and Hardy  (2037 car., ~2.0 min)

Chaplin, for his part, went beyond the simple slapstick style of the Keystone studios and became the biggest comedy star of his day, earning, in nineteen fifteen, a weekly salary of one thousand two hundred fifty dollars (the equivalent today of more than sixteen thousand dollars). The control over his own material that his fame allowed him was essential to Chaplin's success. In this way, he moved further and further away from the studio's sloppy productions and knockabout comedies (an approach he had never liked) and refined his comic alter ego, a ragged but good-hearted tramp with his bowler hat, cane and baggy clothes.

Directed by Chaplin himself, The Gold Rush, from nineteen twenty-five, was one of the biggest box-office hits of the silent era and, like many of his films, including The Kid, from nineteen twenty-one, it explored themes close to his heart - especially the effects of poverty, something that harks back to the director's own humble origins.

Keaton, a former acrobat and child vaudeville performer, differed from Chaplin in that his comedy usually came from the contrast between his eternally deadpan face and the incredible athletic feats his characters had to perform to escape danger. With his careful, effortless execution of increasingly complex sequences, as dangerous as they were spectacular, Keaton brought physical comedy close to poetry.

The arrival of sound radically altered the careers of many silent comedy actors. Chaplin held out against dialogue films until nineteen forty, with The Great Dictator, from nineteen forty, preferring to use sound effects in films such as Modern Times, from nineteen thirty-six.

Keaton struggled to adapt to sound comedy, and actors such as Harold Lloyd and Harry Langdon (eighteen eighty-four to nineteen forty-four) lost much of their verve and comic charm. Stan Laurel and Oliver Hardy, the duo known as Laurel and Hardy, having started working at the end of the silent era, were able to adapt to the change and ushered in the next generation of comic geniuses.

## [04] The Gold Rush  (1632 car., ~1.6 min)

The Gold Rush reinforces Charles Chaplin's belief that tragedy and comedy are never very far apart. This unlikely double inspiration came to him after seeing some slides about the hardships suffered by prospectors in the Klondike gold rush of eighteen ninety-six to eighteen ninety-eight, and reading a book about the tragedy of the Donner Party of eighteen forty-six, in which a group of emigrants trapped by snow in the Sierra Nevada was forced to eat their own shoes and the corpses of their dead companions.

From this grim, barren theme, Chaplin created high comedy. The familiar Tramp becomes a prospector, joining the mass of brave, optimistic men facing the dangers of cold, hunger, loneliness and the bears that might show up now and then.

The film was, in every respect, the most elaborate project of Chaplin's career. The crew spent weeks shooting on location at the Truckee glaciers, in the snow-battered Sierra Nevada region. There, Chaplin re-created the historic image of prospectors struggling to cross the Chilkoot Pass. Some six hundred extras, many of them drifters and hobos from Sacramento, were brought in by train to climb the seven hundred-meter pass through the mountain snow.

For the main shot, the crew returned to the studio in Hollywood, where a highly convincing miniature mountain range was built from wood, chicken wire, burlap, plaster, salt and flour. In addition, the studio technicians created exquisite models to produce the special effects Chaplin demanded, such as the prospectors' cabin, which is blown by a storm to the edge of a precipice in one of cinema's longest sequences of comic suspense.

To silent film purists, Chaplin's decision to re-release The Gold Rush in a shortened sound version (complete with plot changes) in nineteen forty-two was sacrilege. The narration was provided by the director-star himself.

Audiences must have been surprised by his melodious, cultivated tone of voice, and the narration lends a curious detachment to the story. Chaplin narrates his own character's adventures in the third person, creating the impression that he is observing, from on high, the characters he had created nearly twenty years earlier.

In nineteen twenty-nine, the director had declared that talkies were "spoiling the oldest art in the world, the art of pantomime. They are ruining the great beauty of silence." However, the sound version of The Gold Rush was a big hit in the nineteen forties with audiences who had not known Chaplin at his peak in the silent era.

## [06] The dance of the rolls  (1224 car., ~1.2 min)

The film is full of comic scenes that have become classics. The historical horror of the starving nineteenth-century pioneers inspired the sequence in which Charlie and his partner Big Jim (Mack Swain) are trapped, starving, in the snow. In the eyes of the delirious Big Jim, his friend intermittently turns into a roast chicken - a triumph both for the cameraman, who had to make the complex trick work using the camera alone, and for Chaplin, who takes on, as if by magic, the characteristics of a bird.

The lonely prospector's dream of hosting a New Year's dinner for the beautiful dance-hall girl (Georgia Hale (nineteen oh-five to nineteen eighty-five), who replaced sixteen-year-old Lita Grey (nineteen oh-eight to nineteen ninety-five) when the latter became pregnant and married Chaplin) provides the opportunity for another famous Chaplin routine: the dance of the rolls. The gag was not new to cinema, but Chaplin gives a unique personality to the dancing legs made of forks and bread rolls.

The Gold Rush is considered one of Chaplin's most perfect achievements. Although his taste for his own work changed over time, late in life he declared several times that this was the film he would like to be remembered for.
