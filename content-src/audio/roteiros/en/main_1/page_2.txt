## [00] Opening  (383 car., ~0.4 min)

Part zero one: From eighteen ninety-five to nineteen twenty-nine. Chapter two: The First Epics.

Epics are known for their huge budgets, grand sets, enormous casts, sweeping scope and, at times, their special effects. In the early days of cinema, rudimentary technology limited what filmmakers could produce, but as it developed, directors were free to let their imaginations run wild.

## [01] Enrico Guazzoni  (1042 car., ~1.0 min)

The origins of the epic film lie in Europe, and more precisely in Italy, where filmmakers drew inspiration from their country's classical past to make visually extravagant films of operatic proportions. Enrico Guazzoni (eighteen seventy-six to nineteen forty-nine) used five thousand extras in his nine-reel drama Quo Vadis?, from nineteen twelve, set in Ancient Rome. Giovanni Pastrone (eighteen eighty-three to nineteen fifty-nine) shot Cabiria, from nineteen fourteen, in multiple locations.

The film runs three hours, with a cast of hundreds and spectacular sets. Pastrone and his Spanish cinematographer Segundo de Chomón were the first to use a dolly-on-tracks system so the camera could move, which allowed them to create uninterrupted tracking shots (shots in which the camera moves closer to the subject). De Chomón was a master of special effects, collaborating on Napoléon (Napoleon, nineteen twenty-seven), directed by Abel Gance (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen eighty-one), an epic that explored a variety of filming techniques.

## [02] Cecil B. DeMille  (1265 car., ~1.3 min)

Italian sword-and-sandal epics were a major influence on Hollywood production and inspired Cecil B. DeMille (eighteen eighty-one to nineteen fifty-nine) to make his first version of The Ten Commandments, from nineteen twenty-three, a one hundred thirty-six-minute spectacle dramatizing the biblical story of Moses. DeMille put his flair to work again in nineteen twenty-seven, when he directed the spectacular biblical epic The King of Kings, from nineteen twenty-seven.

When World War One broke out in nineteen fourteen, the development of the European film industry was interrupted, but American filmmakers kept breaking down the barriers of the seventh art. D. W. Griffith (eighteen seventy-five to nineteen forty-eight) directed a saga about the American Civil War, The Birth of a Nation, from nineteen fifteen, a groundbreaking work in both its technical and narrative style.

At once one of the most revered and most reviled films ever made, D. W. Griffith's The Birth of a Nation matters for the very same reasons that inspire these two opposing reactions. Rarely has a film earned such praise and such contempt, which in many ways increases its value not only in the annals of cinema, but also as a fundamental historical artifact (some would call it a relic).

## [03] Worst racial stereotypes  (2287 car., ~2.3 min)

Although the film is based on Thomas Dixon's explicitly racist play The Clansman: An Historical Romance of the Ku Klux Klan, accounts claim that Griffith was indifferent to the racist content of its central theme. The degree of the director's complicity in conveying its infamous message has been debated for nearly a century. There is no controversy, however, about the film's technical and artistic merits: Griffith was, as usual, more interested in the possibilities of the medium than in the message, and in that realm he set the standards of modern Hollywood.

The Birth of a Nation was, quite clearly, the first historical epic ever made, proving that, even in the silent film era, audiences were willing to sit through a story more than three hours long. With its countless artistic innovations, Griffith essentially created contemporary film language, and although some elements of The Birth of a Nation may seem dated, virtually every film owes it a debt in some way. Griffith introduced the use of dramatic close-ups, tracking shots and other significant camera movements; parallel action, alternating sequences and other editing techniques; and even the first orchestrated score. It is a pity that all these innovative elements were tied to a story of such dubious merit.

The first half of the film begins before the Civil War, explaining the introduction of slavery in the United States before the action gets underway. Two families are introduced: the Stonemans from the North and the Camerons from the South. The story is told through these families and, often, through their servants, embodying the worst racial stereotypes. As the nation is torn apart by war, the slaves and abolitionist sympathizers are portrayed as the destructive force behind it all.

The film's racism worsens in the second half, which takes place during Reconstruction and depicts the rise of the Ku Klux Klan, whose members are presented as the film's supposed heroes. That Griffith introduced a love story in the middle of this re-creation of a race war is extremely audacious: a choice that is at once thrilling and disturbing.

## [04] National Association for the Advancement of Colored People  (703 car., ~0.7 min)

The Birth of a Nation is undoubtedly powerful propaganda, even though its political message is stomach-turning. The film seems to say that only the puritanical Ku Klux Klan can preserve the unity of the nation. Not surprisingly, even in its own time the film was met with outrage. It drew protests from the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP), sparked demonstrations and later forced Griffith himself to respond to the criticism with his even more ambitious Intolerance: Love's Struggle Throughout the Ages, from nineteen sixteen. Still, the fact that The Birth of a Nation is respected and studied to this day, despite its subject matter, reveals its enduring importance.
