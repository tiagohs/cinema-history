## [00] Opening  (414 car., ~0.4 min)

Part zero one: From eighteen ninety-five to nineteen twenty-nine. Chapter five: Fantasy and Supernatural Cinema.

Early in cinema's evolution, some pioneers saw the potential of the seventh art to portray the mysterious, the terrifying, the fantastic and the supernatural. Filmmakers such as F. W. Murnau, Georges [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] and Benjamin Christensen brought to the screen masterpieces that influenced future generations.

## [01] Georges Méliès  (1007 car., ~1.0 min)

French filmmaker Georges [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] (eighteen sixty-one to nineteen thirty-eight) was the first to explore this territory, seizing on the possibilities of slow motion, fast motion, superimposition, stop-motion animation, dissolves and all the other ingenious tricks cinema could play on audiences who were amazed, bewildered or terrified. As early as eighteen ninety-six, while Louis [[pron: Lumière|Loo-mee-air]] (eighteen sixty-four to nineteen forty-eight), somber prose compared with [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]]'s mischievous poetry, was showing his thrilling L'arrivée d'un train à La Ciotat (The Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat, eighteen ninety-five), [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] was dazzling his patrons with Le manoir du diable, from eighteen ninety-six.

It is telling that the [[pron: Lumière|Loo-mee-air]] brothers started out as industrial chemists, whereas [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] was a magician by profession. Yet, however imaginative they were, there was always something farcical about the French director's films, and it is hard to imagine many viewers were actually frightened or disturbed by them.

## [02] Frankenstein  (967 car., ~1.0 min)

Over the course of the nineteen tens, many of the classic fantasy creatures made their screen debut: Frankenstein in nineteen ten, The Werewolf in nineteen thirteen, among others. Vampires, however, had to wait until the following decade, with Nosferatu: A Symphony of Horror, from nineteen twenty-two, directed by F. W. Murnau (eighteen eighty-eight to nineteen thirty-one).

Witchcraft was another theme that fed the bubbling creativity of fantasy cinema. Danish director Benjamin Christensen (eighteen seventy-nine to nineteen fifty-nine) emigrated to Hollywood, but not before making his masterpiece: Häxan, from nineteen twenty-two, with its surreal blend of documentary and extraordinary fictional sequences. The film was a visceral attack on religious fanaticism, though it also found room for sardonic humor, especially when the director himself appears as Satan, playing the role stark naked. Most of Christensen's Hollywood films have unfortunately been lost.

## [03] Abel Gance  (2475 car., ~2.5 min)

Madmen, and especially mad scientists, were also guaranteed presences in early fantasy films, and they still are today. Abel Gance (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen eighty-one) made La folie du docteur Tube (The Madness of Dr. Tube, nineteen fifteen), about a scientist who discovers how to change the appearance of people and objects, giving the director a cue to use distorting lenses freely.

The fantastic tales of Edgar Allan Poe were a popular source of adaptations, one of the first being Le système du docteur Goudron et du professeur Plume (Dr. Goudron's System, nineteen thirteen), based on the short story The System of Doctor Tarr and Professor Fether, in which a visitor to an insane asylum has trouble telling the guards from the patients. The asylum would later reappear as the setting of Kurutta ippeji (A Page of Madness, nineteen twenty-six), a strikingly avant-garde drama by Japanese director Kinugasa Teinosuke (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen eighty-two) about a former sailor who works as a janitor at an asylum to be near his insane wife.

With Das Cabinet des Dr. Caligari (The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari, nineteen twenty), fantasy cinema took off, especially in postwar Germany. The film's distorted, Expressionist sets, which rejected any pretense of realism, the fragmented narrative, the hyper-stylized performances and the sense of paranoid insecurity came together to express the atmosphere of a defeated, traumatized nation, lacking confidence in its own identity and seeing enemies both within and without. The painted shadows of this film and of its silent-era successors seemed to anticipate, as Siegfried Kracauer and other critics pointed out, the shadows that would deepen in the German collective psyche in the years to come.

It is no surprise that Death, whether in the form of the Grim Reaper himself or of his various demonic incarnations, dominated German cinema during its most creative period. Released the same year Nosferatu appeared, Der müde Tod (Destiny, nineteen twenty-one), by Fritz Lang, shows Death offering a young woman, whose fiancé he has taken, three chances to bring him back. But the stories Death shows her, set in the Baghdad of the caliphs, Renaissance Venice and a fairy-tale China, only prove the impossibility of saving a doomed life, and in the end the bride agrees to die so she can be reunited with her beloved.

## [04] Lon Chaney  (1585 car., ~1.6 min)

Throughout the rich range of fantasy films from the German silent era there is a sense that dark forces are taking over, that things are coming unhinged and sliding inexorably toward a fatal abyss, once again making the comparison with the nightmare that would soon engulf the country inevitable. Not that German cinema held a monopoly on the fantastic during that period. Hollywood produced a series of films starring the chameleonic Lon Chaney, who established himself as one of the most powerful actors of his time. In one of his most memorable performances, Chaney played the disfigured Phantom in The Phantom of the Opera, from nineteen twenty-five.

Although these Hollywood fantasies featured extraordinary morbid elements, they were essentially grotesque stories set in realistic environments. They lack the distressing sense of an entire world out of joint that was so strong in German cinema of the period. A rare exception was The Man Who Laughs, from nineteen twenty-eight, directed by Paul Leni and starring Conrad Veidt, who plays Gwynplaine, the son of a decadent English nobleman who had offended King James Two. On the monarch's orders, Gwynplaine is disfigured, left with a permanent gash of a grin on his face.

German Expressionism is one of the most recognized styles of silent film, although it is sometimes hard to define. The Expressionist movement in the arts first emerged in poetry and painting in the early twentieth century, also influencing theater, architecture and, naturally, cinema, especially after World War One (nineteen fourteen to nineteen eighteen).

## [05] Horror film  (1768 car., ~1.8 min)

What Expressionism sought was a subjective representation of the world, one capable of revealing the anguish of human existence through distorted images far removed from reality, evoking nightmares. These concepts originated, in part, in German Romanticism.

With its use of shadows and contrasts, stories tackling unsettling themes, and exaggerated sets, makeup and costumes, German silent film of the nineteen twenties laid the foundations for two major genres of modern cinema: the horror film and film noir.

[CITAÇÃO] I believe that the films of the future will use more and more of these 'camera angles' or, as I prefer to call them, these 'dramatic angles'. They help cinematic thinking

said F.W. Murnau.

Like Expressionist paintings, the films of this period sought to address the personal and subjective experiences of human beings. In cinema, this trait is most particularly associated with unrealistic sets, steep angles and striking shadows that heighten the film's atmosphere.

The Italian term chiaroscuro (light-dark) is often used to describe this kind of high-contrast cinematography between light and darkness, but German critic Lotte H. Eisner, author of The Haunted Screen, from nineteen fifty-two, preferred an expression from her own language: helldunkel, which she defined as "a kind of twilight of the German soul, expressing itself in gloomy and enigmatic interiors or in hazy and insubstantial landscapes."

To make up for the lack of big budgets, the early Expressionist films used unrealistic sets, with designs painted on the walls and floors to represent lights, shadows and objects. The stories and narratives generally dealt with madness, insanity, betrayal and other existential themes brought to the surface by the traumatic experiences of World War One.

## [06] Bram Stoker  (1843 car., ~1.8 min)

This dark, melancholy school of filmmaking was eventually carried over to the United States later on, when the Nazis seized power and many German filmmakers were forced to emigrate to Hollywood. These artists realized that the American studios were willing to welcome them, and several German directors and camera operators found success in the country, producing Hollywood films that left their mark on cinema as a whole. According to theorist Siegfried Kracauer, author of the book From Caligari to Hitler, from nineteen forty-seven, German cinema of the Golden Age, that is, the silent films made before Hitler's takeover of the country and the subsequent rise of the Third Reich, already showed signs that Nazi Germany was inevitable.

Among the most emblematic films of German Expressionism, The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari certainly deserves special mention. The film is famous for its harrowing atmosphere and macabre story, in which the character Francis tries to solve a series of murders he suspects are the work of a hypnotist, Dr. Caligari, and his somnambulist accomplice, Cesare. Many have interpreted the feature as an allegory of World War One, in which Cesare represented the innocent soldiers driven to crime by the manipulation of an authoritarian and abusive government (represented by Caligari).

Another highly representative film of German Expressionism was Nosferatu: A Symphony of Horror, by F.W. Murnau. Although it used many real locations, which was uncommon in films of the time, the film builds despair and anguish in an escalating, frightening way. Murnau turns the familiar into something strange, giving Expressionist touches to real-life landscapes, which gradually carries the viewer from reality into nightmare. The vampire, who symbolizes the inevitability of death, is a loose adaptation of Bram Stoker's Dracula.

Henrik Galeen's screenplay for Nosferatu: A Symphony of Horror largely follows the plot of Bram Stoker's classic vampire novel Dracula, from eighteen ninety-seven, although it simplifies the story and eliminates or sidelines a number of characters.

Jonathan Harker, the young hero, becomes Hutter; Professor Van Helsing, Dracula's powerful antagonist, is reduced to the useless Professor Bulwer. The theme of female sacrifice in the film (Ellen, Hutter's wife, gives herself to the vampire in order to destroy him) is not in the novel. The setting also changes: Whitby becomes the fictional Baltic port of "Wisborg." And Dracula himself becomes "Count Orlok," supposedly to disguise the fact that the filmmakers had used Stoker's plot without permission.

If that was the reason, it didn't work: Stoker's family won a copyright infringement lawsuit, and a court order required that all copies of the film be destroyed. Prana studios (a Sanskrit word meaning "life force") went bankrupt, making Nosferatu its first and only release.

## [08] Metropolis  (698 car., ~0.7 min)

Metropolis, from nineteen twenty-seven, by Fritz Lang, is also considered one of the most famous films of Expressionism and of silent film as a whole. The feature blends Gothic elements with a futuristic aesthetic that still inspires the science fiction genre today. With its extreme vision of a society driven by class struggle, with the rich living at the brightly lit top of the city and the poor below, in the shadows, the film reveals the economic inequalities of Weimar Republic Germany, blending personal anguish with social critique. The crowd of workers itself is an essential part of the film's visual aesthetic, moving in a symmetrical, mechanical way, like the giant machine they serve.
