Part zero one: From eighteen ninety-five to nineteen twenty-nine. Chapter seven: The "Golden Age" of Silent Film.

During this time, investment doubled. Major studios emerged, pouring ever more money into film production, and great directors made memorable films.

## [01] F. W. Murnau  (1849 car., ~1.9 min)

By the mid-nineteen twenties, cinema, deprived of speech, had already developed a form of visual storytelling as subtle and expressive as words. Although written intertitles stood in for dialogue, it was the images that truly drove the narrative and captured emotions. Free from the demands of sound recording, the camera, which F. W. Murnau (eighteen eighty-eight to nineteen thirty-one) called "the director's paintbrush," achieved a flexibility unsurpassed to this day.

Unable to speak, actors relied on gestures or facial expressions, achieving the same subtlety and depth of feeling they would later attain with the more naturalistic style brought by sound cinema.

[CITAÇÃO] Nothing in the world can be compared to the human face. It is a land one can never tire of exploring. There is no greater experience in a studio than to witness the expression of a sensitive face under the mysterious power of inspiration. To see it animated from inside, and turning into poetry.

said Carl Theodor Dreyer.

Silence, therefore, was not a limitation. For many aesthetes and film theorists, the uniqueness of film lay precisely in its ability to tell a story through images alone. This quest for a pure cinema found followers in countries as far away as Japan, where the Expressionist Kurutta ippêji (A Page of Madness, nineteen twenty-six), directed by Teinosuke Kinugasa (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen eighty-two), used only images to tell the disturbing story of events that take place inside an asylum.

Few films went as far as eliminating intertitles completely, but the greatest glory of the final years of silent film undoubtedly lies in its visual refinement. Murnau's Faust, from nineteen twenty-six, despite its literary origins in the legend of the man who sells his soul to the Devil, is especially notable for its magnificent re-creation of medieval Europe.

## [02] Die Nibelungen: Siegfried  (1151 car., ~1.1 min)

This was one of the most extravagant productions of Universum Film AG (UFA), the largest German studio of the time, which also made the sumptuous Die Nibelungen: Siegfried, from nineteen twenty-four, and Die Nibelungen: Kriemhilds Rache (Die Nibelungen: Kriemhild's Revenge, nineteen twenty-four), by Fritz Lang (eighteen ninety to nineteen seventy-six), as well as Metropolis, from nineteen twenty-seven, the same director's science fiction epic.

Metropolis, despite its flimsy plot, remains to this day one of the most visually striking evocations of the future ever seen on screen. French cinema aspired to the epic many times, but never as much as in the work of Abel Gance (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen eighty-one), whose film Napoléon (Napoleon, nineteen twenty-seven) took the aesthetic refinement of silent film to the extreme. Danish director Carl Theodor Dreyer (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen sixty-eight) dramatized French history in La passion de Jeanne d'Arc (The Passion of Joan of Arc, nineteen twenty-eight), but avoided spectacle as much as possible by focusing obstinately on the emotional registers conveyed by the human face.

## [03] Learn more: The Supreme Work of Master Carl Theodor Dreyer  (1933 car., ~1.9 min)

Learn more: The Supreme Work of Master Carl Theodor Dreyer.

The austere Danish master Carl Theodor Dreyer made The Passion of Joan of Arc more than eighty years ago, yet few would deny that his meticulous production still represents a standard of excellence for any character study in cinema.

The film presents the martyrdom of this blend of warrior and maiden, who carries the weight of a nation on her back and does so through an unshakable faith made evident in the expressive power of the protagonist's eyes. This is largely due to the anguished, ethereal performance of the enigmatic Maria Falconetti (eighteen ninety-two to nineteen forty-six), a stage actress Dreyer discovered in Paris. (This was not her film debut, however - and, perhaps discouraged by Dreyer's methods, she never made another film.) Falconetti had her suffering, which is palpable on screen, perversely refined, being forced - legend has it - to kneel on stones and to watch the dailies exhaustively until she knew every feature of her face in detail.

Setting the film in the city of Rouen in fourteen thirty-one and drawing on the manuscripts of Joan's trial, Dreyer works from a palette of raw emotions: Falconetti's expressions proclaim, loud and clear, her character's present agony, past glories and future fears. Often, by framing Joan's face in close-up and at a slightly skewed angle, he sets her sorrow against the monstrous faces of the judges and theologians gathered to force her confession, creating a claustrophobic, desperate atmosphere out of a simple camera movement.

As in later films, among them Vredens dag (Day of Wrath, nineteen forty-three) and Ordet, from nineteen fifty-five, Dreyer addresses questions of faith and spirituality by portraying characters who supposedly have reached a state of grace but are judged by a society of skeptics and violent fanatics. The film remains a fascinating anomaly in the history of European cinema.

## [04] Sunrise: A Song of Two Humans  (1222 car., ~1.2 min)

Indeed, what was most extraordinary about the final period of silent film was its ability to combine visual grandeur with intimacy. This also applies to Hollywood, whose greatest masterpieces were directed by European émigrés. Murnau came from Germany to work for Fox and, in the romantic melodramas Sunrise: A Song of Two Humans, from nineteen twenty-seven, and City Girl, from nineteen thirty, combined stylistic beauty with a delicate attention to human feelings. The Swede Victor Sjöström (eighteen seventy-nine to nineteen sixty), known for his studies of rural Scandinavian life, chose an equally remote setting for one of the last great American silent films, The Wind, from nineteen twenty-eight, a powerful melodrama shot on location in the Mojave Desert.

The excellence of silent film in the late nineteen twenties helps explain why many saw the arrival of sound as a step backward. Much of Lonesome, from nineteen twenty-eight, directed by Paul Fejos (eighteen ninety-seven to nineteen sixty-three), another portrait of urban life in the United States made by a European émigré, displays all the refinement of the twilight of that era of cinema, but in it the mood is ruined by terribly banal dialogue sequences.

## [05] Bronenosets Potyomkin  (1312 car., ~1.3 min)

Sound opened up new possibilities for cinema and, by the nineteen thirties, the new medium had already secured its place in the United States and Western Europe. However, it would still take years for filmmakers to have the technical means to combine sound with the visual fluency of the last silent films. This is borne out by the exceptional work of the few mature film industries that made this kind of film after nineteen thirty.

Until the middle of that decade, Soviet cinema continued to produce great silent films, such as the classic Bronenosets Potyomkin (Battleship Potemkin, nineteen twenty-five), by Sergei M. [[pron: Eisenstein|Eye-zen-stine]] (eighteen ninety-eight to nineteen forty-eight), one of the most influential directors in the history of cinema. In Japan, where talkies were rare until nineteen thirty-five, filmmakers such as Mikio Naruse (nineteen oh-five to nineteen sixty-nine) and Hiroshi Shimizu (nineteen oh-three to nineteen sixty-six) blended local traditions with the flamboyant style of the Western masters of the late silent era. In films such as Rakudai Wa Shita Keredo (I Flunked, But..., nineteen thirty-two), Yasujiro Ozu (nineteen oh-three to nineteen sixty-three) showed that silent film was capable of a realism as authentic as that achieved with sound in the Neorealist films of postwar Italy.
