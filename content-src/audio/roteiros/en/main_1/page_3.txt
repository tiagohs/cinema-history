## [00] Opening  (297 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero one: From eighteen ninety-five to nineteen twenty-nine. Chapter three: Heroines and Women Directors.

The rise of the term femme fatale; the popularization of the term "It Girl"; and Alice Guy-Blaché, one of cinema's most important pioneers, even though history forgot her for a long time.

## [01] It Girl  (1306 car., ~1.3 min)

The virgins played with passion by Lillian Gish and with humor by Mary Pickford during the silent era were countered by two comparatively short-lived manifestations of the sexualized woman. In the mid-nineteen tens came the vamp, the voracious upper-class man-eater, iconically portrayed by Theda Bara in A Fool There Was, from nineteen fifteen. In the nineteen twenties, the vamp gave way to the flapper, a hedonistic but not at all malicious figure, most successfully embodied by Colleen Moore and Clara Bow, the It Girl.

Virgins and vamps hark back to Victorian melodrama and were fantasies rooted in idealized and demonized notions of femininity. Having arrived after World War One, the flapper was one facet of the new emancipated woman, who worked, voted, smoked, drank, danced to jazz and had sex. The other facet was the sophisticated wife, played with great daring by Gloria Swanson (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty-three) and her extravagant outfits in six bedroom comedies by Cecil B. DeMille (eighteen eighty-one to nineteen fifty-nine). The sexy working girl and the liberated wife merged into one, giving rise to the sharp-tongued modern woman of the screwball comedy, or madcap comedy, of the nineteen thirties, a subgenre that can be considered the forerunner of the romantic comedy.

## [02] Cleopatra  (1180 car., ~1.2 min)

Just as the femme fatale of nineteen forties film noir was a product of male anxieties during a period of national instability, the vamp was a sociological construct. Unlike the femme fatale, however, she would not later be redeemed by feminist scholars. She emerged as a celluloid phantom, drawn from Sir Philip Burne-Jones's painting The Vampire, from the poem of the same name by his cousin Rudyard Kipling and from the novel Dracula by Bram Stoker, all from eighteen ninety-seven. Kipling's poem inspired the Broadway play A Fool There Was, a nineteen oh-nine hit directed by Porter Emerson Browne and adapted for the screen in nineteen fifteen.

Bara explored her vampish traits in a series of seductive characters, her eyes painted black, most recognizably in Cleopatra, from nineteen seventeen. However, Bara was not American cinema's first vampire. Alice Hollister preceded her in The Vampire, from nineteen thirteen, in which vaudeville dancers Alice Eis and Bert French re-created their "Vampire Dance," which had been a stage hit in nineteen oh-nine. As a supernatural woman who sucks the life force out of a lawyer, Bara became the most sensual of all the femme fatales.

## [03] Learn more: From the Page to the Screen  (962 car., ~1.0 min)

Learn more: From the Page to the Screen.

Theda Bara was neither the first nor the last star to have her name changed by the Hollywood studios. Florence Lawrence, "The Biograph Girl," considered the "first movie star," who in nineteen ten also became the first actress to have her real name in the credits, was actually Florence Annie Bridgwood.

Many other stars were given, or chose, though this was rarer, stage names. Sometimes it was a matter of euphony: Julius Ulman became Douglas Fairbanks, while Frances Gumm metamorphosed into Judy Garland. Some names carried unfortunate associations: Hedwig Kiesler's surname was considered too similar to keister (slang for "rear end"), and so she became Hedy Lamarr.

Only when the power of the old studios began to wane were actors like Jack Lemmon able to refuse to be renamed. More recently, stars such as Arnold Schwarzenegger and Renée Zellweger have built extraordinary careers while proudly keeping their surnames.

## [04] F. Scott Fitzgerald  (1269 car., ~1.3 min)

In nineteen twenty, F. Scott Fitzgerald symbolically ushered in the Roaring Twenties with This Side of Paradise, his sensational debut novel, and Olive Thomas, who had originated the "baby vamp " in Upstairs and Down, from nineteen nineteen, played a schoolgirl pretending to be a jazz girl in The Flapper, the first time the term was used in the United States. Fitzgerald claimed that Colleen Moore was the quintessential flapper for her performance in Flaming Youth, from nineteen twenty-three.

Moore became a box-office phenomenon, though she was more spirited than seductive. The same could not be said of Bow in films such as It, from nineteen twenty-seven, in which she shone as a department store salesgirl set on landing her boss. Screenwriter Elinor Glyn even makes an appearance in the film to explain the meaning of "it," a euphemism for the character's sex appeal.

Alice Guy Blaché had been hired as a secretary at the Comptoir Général de Photographie, which would be acquired the following year by Léon [[pron: Gaumont|Go-mon]]. Under the [[pron: Gaumont|Go-mon]] name, the world's first film company was born, and the oldest still in operation. Despite the change at the company, the young woman, then in her twenties, kept working as a secretary, but she would not stay in the job for long.

## [05] La Sortie de l’usine Lumière à Lyon  (1333 car., ~1.3 min)

Together with the [[pron: Gaumont|Go-mon]] team, Alice Guy was invited to witness the magic of the first cinematograph developed by the [[pron: Lumière|Loo-mee-air]] brothers. The device, revolutionary for its time, worked as both camera and projector. As she watched the scenes of La Sortie de l’usine [[pron: Lumière|Loo-mee-air]] à Lyon (Workers Leaving the [[pron: Lumière|Loo-mee-air]] Factory, eighteen ninety-five), she foresaw the potential of the new technology. The daughter of a bookseller, Alice had always been used to reading and had even done some theater for a while. Her familiarity with storytelling led her to look at cinema in a new way. She decided to turn it into a vehicle for telling stories.

And so, in eighteen ninety-six, Alice released the world's first non-fiction film. La Fée aux choux (The Cabbage Fairy, eighteen ninety-six), just one minute long, was written, produced and directed by her.

Although the [[pron: Lumière|Loo-mee-air]] brothers made a short scene titled L’Arroseur arrosé (The Sprinkler Sprinkled, eighteen ninety-five) in eighteen ninety-five, they had no inkling of cinema's full potential and saw it more as a tool for recording than as a way of telling stories. In contrast, Alice Guy's first film features sets, cuts, special effects and a narrative, however brief. It is based on an old French legend according to which baby boys are born from cabbages, while girls are born from roses.

## [06] The Lost Garden: The Life and Cinema of Alice Guy-Blaché  (1872 car., ~1.9 min)

Alice herself remade the production twice, releasing new versions in nineteen hundred and nineteen oh-two. Of the nineteen hundred film, a fragment kept by the Svenska Filminstitutet, the Swedish Film Institute, was recovered.

According to her granddaughter Adrienne Blaché-Channing in The Lost Garden: The Life and Cinema of Alice Guy-Blaché, from nineteen ninety-five, Alice's first commercial film sold eighty copies, a success for its time. Its strong reception led the young woman to be quickly promoted to head of film production at [[pron: Gaumont|Go-mon]]. Quite a position for a woman in the nineteenth century.

By ushering in a new era of cinema, in which filming was no longer restricted to depicting reality, she could not have been more deserving of the role. From that moment on, the creators' imagination was the only limit for the Seventh Art. That same year, Georges [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] would release his first film. He became famous; Alice was nearly forgotten by history.

[CITAÇÃO] There is nothing connected with the making of a film that a woman cannot do as easily as a man, being fully capable of mastering every technical detail of this art.

said Alice Guy Blaché.

The greatest success of her career came the same year, when Alice released La Vie du Christ (The Birth, the Life and the Death of Christ, nineteen oh-six), a thirty-four-minute short film that explores film language as never before. With special effects, scene cuts and deep characters, she laid the first foundation upon which the blockbusters of the future would be built. Also in nineteen oh-six, the director released the film Les résultats du féminisme (The Consequences of Feminism, nineteen oh-six), which shows men performing activities typically associated with women, while the women enjoy themselves at the bar and harass their partners. In less than seven minutes, the comedy uses laughter to poke at the status quo.

## [07] Herbert Blaché  (709 car., ~0.7 min)

On a business trip, the director met her colleague Herbert Blaché, whom she married, and was removed from her post at [[pron: Gaumont|Go-mon]], he, of course, kept his job. In nineteen oh-seven, her husband was sent to the United States as the company's production manager. Determined to start a new life in America, they packed their bags. Once in the United States, Alice founded her own company, Solax, in nineteen ten. The first productions were a success, and by nineteen twelve she was the only woman in the country earning more than twenty-five thousand dollars a year. With that success, she built her own studio in Fort Lee for one hundred thousand dollars, the equivalent of a three million dollars investment today.

## [08] Part 7  (575 car., ~0.6 min)

The director is believed to have worked on more than a thousand film productions, although only one hundred thirty of them have been found to date. Over time, many of her films were credited to men, while others bore only the name of the production company. Her work began to be rediscovered in the eighties, after the posthumous publication of her autobiography, written in the late forties. In the book, Alice lists the films she produced, in the hope of one day receiving due credit for her work and claiming a place that had always been hers: that of a pioneer of cinema.
