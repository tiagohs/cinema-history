## [01] Spike Lee  (986 car., ~1.0 min)

"Black film may be to the nineties what the car phone was to the eighties: every studio executive has to have one," wrote The New York Times magazine in the summer of nineteen ninety-one. It is a comment that says a lot about a cultural moment and its transience. The article was titled They've Gotta Have Us, a reference to the film by Spike Lee (born in nineteen fifty-seven) released in nineteen eighty-six, She's Gotta Have It, from nineteen eighty-six.

The group portrait on the cover brought together an impressive collection of young Black filmmakers, what was labeled "the class of 'ninety-one." Lee was at the top, of course. At the time, he was in the midst of a creative burst that would continue at a pace of practically one film a year: Do the Right Thing, from nineteen eighty-nine, Mo' Better Blues, from nineteen ninety, Jungle Fever, from nineteen ninety-one, Malcolm X, from nineteen ninety-two, Crooklyn, from nineteen ninety-four, Clockers, from nineteen ninety-five.

## [02] Learn more: Boyz n the Hood: An Honest Portrait of a Society (1)  (2165 car., ~2.2 min)

Learn more: Boyz n the Hood: An Honest Portrait of a Society.

Thanks to forerunners in the early nineteen seventies such as Melvin Van Peebles (born in nineteen thirty-two) and Gordon Parks (nineteen twelve to two thousand six), a number of African American filmmakers emerged in commercial cinema. Among them, Robert Townsend and Spike Lee deserve special mention, each producing brilliant comic dramas about the condition of Black people in the United States. But it was the autobiographical debut of John Singleton, Boyz n the Hood, in nineteen ninety-one, that introduced critics to a so-called cinematic voice of Black Americans. For his achievement, Singleton received two Oscar nominations, and his film was a surprise hit that earned Columbia Pictures an impressive profit.

Singleton's drama, which begins in the wake of the nineteen eighty-four Los Angeles Olympics, tells the story of three ten-year-old boys: Tre (Desi Arnez Hines Two), Doughboy (Baha Jackson) and Ricky (Donovan McCrary). Raised by separated parents, they live in a world torn apart by gang violence, police brutality and economic hardship. When Tre's mother, Reva (Angela Bassett), drops him off at the door of his father, Jason "Furious" Styles (Laurence Fishburne), he gets a drastic lesson in manhood and becomes a teenager determined to get ahead in life.

The film jumps ahead seven years. Tre (played from then on by Cuba Gooding Jr.) is a high school senior who works part-time, wants to attend a historically Black college and is dating a young woman named Brandi (Nia Long). Doughboy (Ice Cube) is a layabout gang member who takes advantage of the patience of his mother, Mrs. Baker (Tyra Ferrell), while his brother Ricky (Morris Chestnut) trains, hoping for a college football scholarship. Even while trying to get out of that neighborhood, Tre remains tied to his friends from the block. When thugs from a rival gang threaten the trio, Ricky ends up dead, murdered by a gang. After that, Tre heeds his father's wise teachings and manages to avoid the mistakes of his tyrannized world. In the end, he escapes the trap of poverty and violence and goes off to college with Brandi.

## [03] Learn more: Boyz n the Hood: An Honest Portrait of a Society (2)  (996 car., ~1.0 min)

Preachy, but wisely orchestrated as a moving portrait of intraracial violence in America's inner cities, Boyz n the Hood is a fable with a hard-to-forget moral, aimed at the very youth it depicts on screen. After a troubling dedication reminding viewers how many young Black men are murdered by other young Black men, the film launches into a realistic, profanity-laden and moving coming-of-age story.

Also notable as rapper Ice Cube's screen debut, Singleton's film displays full awareness of Hollywood conventions in its storytelling and shows genuine affection for the influence of hip-hop. Structured in three very distinct acts and with a clear leaning toward broad moralizing, the film has a professional polish. But it is also suffused with hypnotic beats and raps by several successful artists who contributed to the soundtrack, making Boyz n the Hood a career-making success and the origin of a wave of dramas centered on Black neighborhoods that flooded screens in the nineteen nineties.

## [04] Ernest Dickerson  (2019 car., ~2.0 min)

There was also Lee's friend Ernest Dickerson (born in nineteen fifty-one), cinematographer of Do the Right Thing, who was now directing his own Harlem tale, Juice, from nineteen ninety-two. There was Mario Van Peebles (born in nineteen fifty-seven), creator of the gangster thriller New Jack City, from nineteen ninety-one. There were the brothers Warrington Hudlin (born in nineteen fifty-two) and Reginald Hudlin (born in nineteen sixty-one), creators of the hit teen comedy House Party, from nineteen ninety. There were Charles Lane (born in nineteen fifty-three) and twenty-year-old Matty Rich (born in nineteen seventy-one), whose debut films won awards at Cannes and Sundance, respectively.

And the rising star of the moment was John Singleton (nineteen sixty-eight to twenty nineteen), whose autobiographical Los Angeles drama Boyz n the Hood, from nineteen ninety-one, earned him a three-year deal with the studio and would make him the first African American (and the youngest person) to be nominated for the Best Director Oscar. "The current crop is breaking new ground with its stories and politics," the article observed. "For the studios, these are tales from a new world, presented with an often harrowing, if sometimes hyperbolic, realism." The filmmakers themselves seemed sensibly wary of being lumped into a trend. "If one Black filmmaker messes up," said Lee, "they make the rest of us feel it."

Fast-forward twenty-five years or so and we find ourselves in a situation where, for the second year in a row, no African American actor received an Oscar nomination; where they still complain about the lack of representation in film and television, when they are not taking to the streets to remind the country that their lives matter. ## [05] Ice Cube  (1169 car., ~1.2 min)

One thing is certain: looking back, the nineties were a golden age. The number of films directed by and starring Black artists that were successful at the time is astonishing. Hip-hop, and white audiences' interest in it, fueled the movement. Rappers and musicians made a seamless transition to film: Ice Cube (born in nineteen sixty-nine), Ice-T (born in nineteen fifty-eight), Tupac Shakur (nineteen seventy-one to nineteen ninety-six), Queen Latifah (born in nineteen seventy). It was not just youth "hood dramas," however; there was quality work across the whole spectrum. Serious thrillers such as Devil in a Blue Dress, from nineteen ninety-five, and Deep Cover, from nineteen ninety-two, appeared alongside "art" films from the "LA Rebellion" group. This movement of young film students produced influential works such as To Sleep with Anger, from nineteen ninety, by Charles Burnett (born in nineteen forty-four), and Daughters of the Dust, from nineteen ninety-one, by Julie Dash (born in nineteen sixty-two), released in nineteen ninety-one, the first feature film directed by an African American woman to receive theatrical distribution in the United States.

## [06] Learn more: L.A. Rebellion  (1106 car., ~1.1 min)

Learn more: L.A. Rebellion.

Between the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties, the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) launched a policy of including young film students from diasporic and immigrant backgrounds in the US. It was in this context that an extraordinary body of films emerged, expressing the vigor, singularity, persistence and resonance of a new Black cinema, acclaimed by international critics and claimed by new generations of filmmakers. Rediscovered and restored this decade, the films by African American filmmakers who came out of these classes, driven by a project of historical autonomy and artistic emancipation, were brought together under the label L.A. Rebellion, which came to designate the work of names who would become celebrated, such as Charles Burnett (winner of an honorary Oscar in twenty eighteen) and Julie Dash (the first Black woman to direct a commercially released feature film in the US), as well as others who are less well known (but equally remarkable), such as Zeinabu irene Davis, Alile Sharon Larkin, Haile Gerima, Larry Clark and Billy Woodberry.

## [07] Will Smith  (2116 car., ~2.1 min)

On television, too, African American content seemed to be transforming. Will Smith (born in nineteen sixty-eight) figuratively entered Hollywood with The Fresh Prince of Bel-Air, while Jamie Foxx (born in nineteen sixty-seven) began working on the Wayans brothers' sketch show In Living Color. If you couldn't choose between Friends or Seinfeld, you could find Black-led series such as Moesha, A Different World, Family Matters, Kenan and Kel, Martin, Living Single, Sister, Sister and Malcolm and Eddie.

For the first time, mainstream film and TV audiences were getting honest, intelligent portrayals of modern African American life and of US history from a Black perspective, the kind of story only Black filmmakers could tell. The revolution was being televised, dramatized, publicized and praised. It is worth remembering that just three years earlier, in nineteen eighty-seven, Robert Townsend (born in nineteen fifty-seven) had made the cult satire Hollywood Shuffle, from nineteen eighty-seven, which detailed the futile struggle of an aspiring Black actor, and it seems to belong to a completely different era. "The only roles they'll let us play are a slave, a butler or some street thug," another participant at the audition tells Townsend's character. The white producers say they are looking for an "Eddie Murphy type" and ask him to "be more Black." In his daydreams, Townsend fantasizes about playing a detective in film noir, a Shakespearean king, a superhero, even a Black Rambo. Instead, he lands the role of a stereotypical street hustler, which only conjures up visions of protesters picketing his house, accusing him of selling out.

But by the mid-nineties, Townsend's wildest dreams had come true. Now you could be a Black Shakespearean, as Denzel Washington (born in nineteen fifty-four) was in Much Ado About Nothing, from nineteen ninety-three, or a Black superhero, as Wesley Snipes (born in nineteen sixty-two) was in Blade, from nineteen ninety-eight, and not exactly a Rambo, but an action movie hero, as Will Smith (born in nineteen sixty-eight) was in Bad Boys, from nineteen ninety-five.

## [08] Angela Bassett  (1718 car., ~1.7 min)

The fruit of that creative flowering of the nineties was a generation of African American artists who gained real Hollywood power: the power to take on leading roles, to win awards and, ultimately, to get films greenlit. Most of them were men, which tells its own story, and most of them still wield that power today: Washington, Smith, Foxx, Samuel L. Jackson. But it was also the time when Angela Bassett (born in nineteen fifty-eight) became the first Black woman in more than a decade to receive a Best Actress Oscar nomination, for the Tina Turner biopic What's Love Got to Do with It, from nineteen ninety-three, and Whitney Houston (nineteen sixty-three to twenty twelve) powered hits such as The Bodyguard, from nineteen ninety-two, and (alongside Bassett) Waiting to Exhale, from nineteen ninety-five.

[CITAÇÃO] How do you think the crack rock gets into the country? We don't own any planes. We don't own no ships.

said Jason "Furious" Styles (Laurence Fishburne) in Boyz n the Hood.

But did this success come at a cost? The actors were getting the roles, but their films were not necessarily "Black" stories. More often than not, they became token Black faces in regular Hollywood movies: exceptions that proved the rule, you might say. Meanwhile, the filmmakers who had helped put them there seemed to have been left behind. A big part of the problem, says Julie Dash, was that the rise of Black cinema coincided with Hollywood's takeover of independent film as a whole. "It became a commodity that could be co-opted by the industry. We moved forward and, as soon as the curators of culture recognized that we were actively making films our own way, suddenly the thing stopped. The funding dried up for almost everyone."

## [09] 25th Hour  (719 car., ~0.7 min)

Directors such as Singleton, Dickerson and Van Peebles struggled to regain momentum after becoming Hollywood properties. Lee's career took the shape of an ongoing battle. Clark points out that he had a terrible time making Malcolm X, for example. "He had to fight off competition from other studios to make it. Norman Jewison wanted to make it. His studio, Warner Bros., wouldn't increase the budget, so he had to campaign for people like Prince, Michael Jackson and Oprah Winfrey to fund it." By the end of the decade, his audience had dwindled, even if the quality of his output had not. Films such as Crooklyn and Bamboozled flopped at the box office, and he ended up making Twenty-fifth Hour, from two thousand two.

## [10] Learn more: The Landmark of a New Wave in Black Cinema (1)  (1950 car., ~1.9 min)

Learn more: The Landmark of a New Wave in Black Cinema.

Living now in a global pop culture so thoroughly sculpted by the language, music, aesthetics and politics of hip-hop, it is sometimes hard to remember that there was a time when the force of that youth movement had not yet been tapped. At the same time, considering the artistic shallowness and materialism that have come to define contemporary hip-hop, it is sometimes equally hard to remember the time when the movement and the music that drove it were radical, subversive and proudly revolutionary. Spike Lee's social protest film brilliantly captures that time when hip-hop and rap were urgent and conscious: perhaps even more impressively, it manages to concentrate the issues of racism, racial pride, class struggle and the joys and frictions of urban life into a snapshot of paradoxically saturated colors. Incendiary and insightful. This is a film born of hip-hop when hip-hop had something to say.

The film opens to the furious music of veteran hip-hop icon Public Enemy. With a cast of vibrant characters whose stories intersect over the course of the day and are used to illuminate larger sociopolitical issues, the film centers on Mookie (Lee), a pizza delivery man who circulates throughout the neighborhood making his deliveries. We meet his demanding, shrill-voiced girlfriend (Rosie Perez), bickering but likable elders (Ruby Dee and Ossie Davis), and neighborhood youths, represented by Radio Raheem (Bill Nunn) and Buggin' Out (Giancarlo Esposito), among others. We also meet the Italian owner of the pizzeria, Sal (Danny Aiello), whose ambivalence about his Black clientele is reflected in the differing attitudes of his racist son Pino (John Turturro) and the more color-blind kid, Vito (Richard Edson). Rounding out the ensemble is a hilarious, profane Greek chorus of elderly and middle-aged Black men whose critical commentary on the day's events offers comic counterpoint.

## [11] Learn more: The Landmark of a New Wave in Black Cinema (2)  (1150 car., ~1.1 min)

As the summer day wears on and tempers flare, trivial disagreements take on great significance. Sexual and racial tensions that lie just beneath the surface of everyday encounters explode, and the resulting fierce violence is a clear metaphor for the state of American race relations at the end of the twentieth century. Controversial and often misread by its detractors (many of whom said the film was an incitement to riot), Do the Right Thing is an unabashedly angry film. It is also full of images of love and camaraderie among Black people that remain so rare in American cinema that they have become radical. Shot in vivid colors that seem to leap off the screen, full of slightly tilted camera angles that suggest the free style of comic books, and edited to reflect the energetic drive of the most frenetic rap. Do the Right Thing became one of the most influential films in recent film history, not only for its stylistic flourishes but also for giving filmmakers permission to draw on life experiences that until then had been ignored or distorted both in Hollywood and in so-called independent films. It dares you not to take it seriously.
