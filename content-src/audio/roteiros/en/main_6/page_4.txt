## [01] Daniel Day-Lewis  (1554 car., ~1.6 min)

In nineteen eighty-nine, film production in Britain was at a low ebb. Audience numbers were starting to grow, but few British films enjoyed commercial success. Yet the talent of British actors remained in evidence, and Daniel Day-Lewis (born in nineteen fifty-seven) greeted the new decade by winning the Oscar for Best Actor for his performance as Christy Brown in My Left Foot: The Story of Christy Brown, from nineteen eighty-nine.

Fifteen British and Irish actors received Oscar nominations for Best Actor or Actress in the nineteen nineties, and their success kept Anglo-Irish cinema in the spotlight. The decade saw the passing of one of Britain's most innovative directors, Derek Jarman (nineteen forty-two to nineteen ninety-four), but new talents burst onto the scene, such as Danny Boyle (born in nineteen fifty-six), with the wry Shallow Grave, from nineteen ninety-four, and Trainspotting, from nineteen ninety-six. Guy Ritchie (born in nineteen sixty-eight) reinvigorated the gangster film with his black comedy about cockney criminals, Lock, Stock and Two Smoking Barrels, from nineteen ninety-eight.

Meanwhile, Mike Leigh (born in nineteen forty-three) consolidated his position as a major director with three internationally acclaimed films - Life Is Sweet, from nineteen ninety, Naked, from nineteen ninety-three, and Secrets and Lies, from nineteen ninety-six. The last of these charted new territory by examining racial barriers and stereotypes, reflecting a contemporary Britain trying to integrate an increasingly multiethnic identity.

## [02] Neil Jordan  (1268 car., ~1.3 min)

The British said goodbye to Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher in nineteen ninety, but Anglo-Irish films continued to reflect her philosophy. Neil Jordan (born in nineteen fifty), with The Crying Game, from nineteen ninety-two, and Jim Sheridan (born in nineteen forty-nine), with In the Name of the Father, from nineteen ninety-three, explored the effects of the Irish question and gave the conflict a human face.

Sheridan's film was based on the true story of the Guildford Four, who were wrongly accused of taking part in an IRA pub bombing. Daniel Day-Lewis plays one of the accused, Gerry Conlon, and the story centers on his relationship with his father, Giuseppe (Pete Postlethwaite), who was also imprisoned, and with his lawyer, Gareth Peirce (Emma Thompson). The lead actors earned Oscar nominations for their performances - the rebellious son, the humble father and the fighting lawyer made the film deeply moving. Directed by Ken Loach (born in nineteen thirty-six), Land and Freedom, from nineteen ninety-five, explores similar themes of identity and idealism against the backdrop of the Spanish Civil War.

## [03] Howards End  (1423 car., ~1.4 min)

Although period dramas such as Howards End, from nineteen ninety-two, and Shakespeare in Love, from nineteen ninety-eight, showed that they were still Britain's strong suit, the most gratifying development of the decade was the resurgence in popularity of homegrown comedy. The romantic comedy Four Weddings and a Funeral, from nineteen ninety-four, directed by Mike Newell (born in nineteen forty-two), was a huge hit and launched the collaboration between screenwriter Richard Curtis (born in nineteen fifty-six) and actor Hugh Grant (born in nineteen sixty), which produced another hit five years later, Notting Hill, from nineteen ninety-nine.

Grant's knack for portraying the bumbling but attractive middle-class bachelor struck a chord with audiences around the world, even if this English stereotype did not always seem familiar to local viewers. The success of Four Weddings and a Funeral paved the way for more authentic British productions such as Brassed Off, from nineteen ninety-six, and The Full Monty, from nineteen ninety-seven, which showed working-class people who might be down but did not feel defeated.

The resistance of the brass band members in Brassed Off to the closure of their rehearsal space, and of the unemployed men in The Full Monty who become strippers, appealed to international audiences, showing that Anglo-Irish filmmakers did not need to dilute their own culture to suit American tastes.
