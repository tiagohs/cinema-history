## [01] Part 1  (844 car., ~0.8 min)

The Iranian Revolution, which overthrew the Shah, Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi, and installed Ayatollah Khomeini as the country's supreme leader in nineteen seventy-nine, was followed by a year of violent demonstrations. Because they were associated with the old order, film activities came to a halt. The new order brought a wave of anger against the media, which were seen as a tool of the overthrown regime. By the time the Revolution was over, one hundred eighty movie theaters had been destroyed. Over the following years, very few films were made. In nineteen eighty-three, the government created the Farabi Cinema Foundation, which oversaw film production and played a crucial role in ensuring that films were made within the values cherished by Islamic culture. By the end of that year, twenty-two feature films had already been produced.

## [02] Mohsen Makhmalbaf  (1239 car., ~1.2 min)

The most important filmmaker to emerge after the Revolution was Mohsen Makhmalbaf (born in nineteen fifty-seven). A militant Muslim in the nineteen seventies, he was imprisoned for four years under the Shah. After the Iranian Revolution, he devoted himself to cinema, having already established his reputation as a writer. Dastforoosh (The Peddler, nineteen eighty-nine), made up of episodes exploring the plight of Iran's urban poor, brought international attention to Iranian cinema. Mohsen's work became increasingly liberal and even critical of the state.

Time of Love, from nineteen ninety, was banned in his home country, but, like several of his colleagues, Mohsen came to be recognized around the world as a great director for his productions: Gabbeh, from nineteen ninety-six, Nun va Goldoon (A Moment of Innocence, nineteen ninety-six) and Sokout (The Silence, nineteen ninety-eight) display a lyrical visual style that captivated foreign audiences. His often rapturous approach was joined by a more urgent polemic in the powerful Safar e Ghandehar (Kandahar, two thousand one), about Afghan society, a film that gained even greater relevance after the September eleventh, two thousand one, attacks on the Twin Towers in New York.

## [03] Abbas Kiarostami  (2488 car., ~2.5 min)

No filmmaker has been as closely identified internationally with Iranian cinema as Abbas Kiarostami (born in nineteen forty). His most acclaimed work of the post-Revolution years was the Koker trilogy: Khane-ye doust kodjast? (Where Is the Friend's House?, nineteen eighty-seven), Life, and Nothing More..., from nineteen ninety-two, and Zire darakhatan zeyton (Through the Olive Trees, nineteen ninety-four), in which a blend of fiction and documentary erases the boundaries between the story being told and the process of its creation. The spare style of these films was refined in Ta'm e guilass (Taste of Cherry, nineteen ninety-seven), winner of the Palme d'Or at Cannes, about a middle-aged man (Homayoun Ershadi) determined to kill himself, and Bad ma ra khahad bord (The Wind Will Carry Us, nineteen ninety-nine), which tells the story of a clash between two worlds when an engineer from the city (Behzad Dorani) travels to a remote Kurdish village to attend the vigil of a dying woman.

The role of women became more important after the Revolution. Among the prominent female filmmakers who emerged in this period are Marzieh (born in nineteen sixty-nine), wife of Mohsen Makhmalbaf, and his two daughters, Samira (born in nineteen eighty) and Hana (born in nineteen eighty-eight). Marzieh wrote and directed her first film in two thousand, Roozi ke zan shodam (The Day I Became a Woman, two thousand), produced by her husband. It portrays three Iranian women at different stages of life: the first is a child, Hava (Fatemeh Cherag Akhar), who is declared "a woman" on her ninth birthday and is consequently required to wear a chador and forbidden to talk to boys, including her best friend; the second is a rebellious young woman, Ahoo (Shabnam Toloui), whose husband threatens her with divorce because she wants to take part in a bicycle race; and the last is a wealthy widow, Hoora (Azizeh Sedighi), who finally manages to break free from society's shackles. Marzieh then made Sag-haye velgard (Stray Dogs, two thousand four), a reflection on European and Hollywood cinema, and, together with her husband, directed Mardi Ke Ba Barf Amad (The Man Who Came with the Snow, two thousand nine), a drama set in the former Soviet Union.

## [04] Sib  (2369 car., ~2.4 min)

Samira won international acclaim for her debut film, Sib (The Apple, nineteen ninety-eight), based on the true story of two sisters, Massoumeh and Zahra Naderi, who were kept locked up by their parents for years and then released into the world. Some critics felt the film harked back to the first native Iranian production, Khaneh siah ast (The House Is Black, nineteen sixty-three), a short documentary about a leper colony directed by the controversial poet Forugh Farrokhzad (nineteen thirty-five to nineteen sixty-seven), featuring a lyrical narration by Forugh herself. The narrative structure of The Apple is more complex than that of The House Is Black, mirroring the style of Kiarostami's earlier Nema-ye Nazdik (Close-Up, nineteen ninety), in which Mohsen Makhmalbaf plays himself in a film about the case, also based on real events, of a man who tried to pass himself off as the filmmaker. Samira then directed the bold and moving Takhté siah (Blackboards, two thousand), which follows the journey of a group of itinerant Kurdish teachers during the Iran-Iraq War. Panj é asr (At Five in the Afternoon, two thousand three) won the Jury Prize at the Cannes Film Festival and follows young Nogreh (Agheleh Rezaie) in her struggle to get an education in contemporary Afghanistan. In another self-referential cinematic twist, the making of At Five in the Afternoon in Kabul after the fall of the Taliban regime became the subject of the documentary Joy of Madness, from two thousand four, directed by Samira's sister, Hana, when she was only fourteen years old.

One of the youngest directors in world cinema, Hana has since adopted a minimalist style similar to her sister's, making Buda as Sharm Foru Rikht (Buddha Collapsed Out of Shame, two thousand seven), about the life of Bakhtay (Nikbakht Noruz), a five-year-old Afghan girl who lives in a cave beneath the ruins of the Buddhas of Bamiyan, destroyed by the Taliban in two thousand one. Her Ruzhaye Sabz (Green Days, two thousand nine) was initially meant to be a drama about the Iranian elections of the time, but it became a semi-documentary when violence broke out after the disputed result, incorporating anonymous amateur videos.

## [05] Rakhshan Bani Etemad  (532 car., ~0.5 min)

Iran's most prominent female director is Rakhshan Bani Etemad (born in nineteen fifty-four). A graduate of the College of Dramatic Arts at the University of Tehran, she worked in television before directing a series of short documentaries. Etemad's first three features, Kharej az mahdudeh (Off-Limits, nineteen eighty-six), Zard-e ghanari (Canary Yellow, nineteen eighty-eight) and Pul-e khareji (Foreign Currency, nineteen eighty-nine), are close to her documentary work in their concern for the lives of society's less fortunate.
