Part zero six: From nineteen ninety to two thousand nine. Chapter ten: Horror: A New Beginning.

In the nineteen nineties and two thousands, directors sought a new purpose for horror, experimenting with new ways to scare and shock. That experimentation gave rise to subgenres such as found footage, splatter and J-horror.

## [01] George A. Romero  (885 car., ~0.9 min)

Since nineteen ninety, the horror film has been caught between the old and the new. On one hand, familiar conventions such as ghost stories and maniac-killer movies remain as dominant as ever; on the other, new ideas and new technologies are influencing the genre.

Legendary directors such as George A. Romero (born in nineteen forty) and John Carpenter (born in nineteen forty-eight) struggled to make effective films in a more savvy era, with Romero revisiting zombies in Diary of the Dead, from two thousand seven, and Survival of the Dead, from two thousand nine, and Carpenter turning to B movies and overseeing remakes of his most memorable works. But after a string of throwaway films such as Vampire in Brooklyn, from nineteen ninety-five, Wes Craven (born in nineteen thirty-nine) came back with one of the most influential films of the genre: Scream, from nineteen ninety-six.

## [02] Learn more: Saw and Splatter Films (1)  (2434 car., ~2.4 min)

Learn more: Saw and Splatter Films.

Our story begins with a man waking up in a bathtub. In the dark, he wakes in a panic, stretching out his arms to find something to hold on to. He hears a voice in front of him, and suddenly the lights come on. The man finds himself in a decrepit bathroom, chained to the wall like the man across from him, with a corpse on the floor between them. So begins the journey into one of the biggest horror franchises, Saw. Since its U.S. release on October twenty-ninth, two thousand four, the franchise has gained a loyal fan base. For many horror fans, Saw is what The Evil Dead, from nineteen eighty-one, A Nightmare on Elm Street, from nineteen eighty-four, or Friday the thirteenth, from nineteen eighty, was to horror in the nineteen eighties. What drew so many fans was the gore, the torture and the imaginative "games" that the villain Jigsaw would devise. Saw is a film that began with a small idea, and while the sequels reshaped that original idea by adding new elements, the original remains the high point of the saga. By combining unique story, visual and violent ideas on a small budget, Saw would become one of the most important horror films of the new century.

Saw is the brainchild of James Wan (born in nineteen seventy-seven) and Leigh Whannell (born in nineteen seventy-seven). The two friends shared a love of cinema and wanted to write and finance their own film. One of the original inspirations for Saw actually came from The Blair Witch Project, from nineteen ninety-nine; both Wan and Whannell realized that a film didn't need much money to be truly scary. The two saved enough to put together a short version of the project and eventually found distribution backing through Lionsgate. Driven by the urge to make a film and the mindset of keeping it affordable, they realized the best way to do it was to make the film in a condensed form. By focusing the main story on just two men in a room, they could save on the budget while still telling the story. These budget constraints also applied to the actual look of the picture. Wan originally wanted Saw to have a "Hitchcockian" style, but because of the limited shooting time they could afford, not every scene could be planned the ideal way. Yet this is what made possible the rough, gritty camerawork of Saw, helping to bring an atmosphere of grime and dread to every scene. That atmosphere is a hugely important part of Saw.

## [03] Learn more: Saw and Splatter Films (2)  (1683 car., ~1.7 min)

Originally called "splatter films," torture porn is a horror subgenre that incorporates themes of excessive violence, gore, nudity, mutilation, torture and sadism. Earlier films that would fit these themes include Dawn of the Dead, from nineteen seventy-eight, The Evil Dead and Dead Alive, from nineteen ninety-two. An important note to keep in mind about these earlier works (especially the last two) is that the violence was used in a comic sense, meaning the violence and gore were steeped in black humor. It would be the dawn of the two thousands that saw the resurgence of this subgenre, and where we would transition from "splatter films" to "torture porn" (the shift becoming far more sinister and horrific). The two main players in this resurgence would be Eli Roth's (born in nineteen seventy-two) Hostel, from two thousand six, and Saw. These films portrayed their violence through extreme visual measures, including new and more terrible kinds of violent acts that we would see on screen, using the camera to force us to watch such violence.

Wan and Whannell never intended Saw to be a torture porn film. Even though the gore of the traps is the selling point of each Saw sequel, the original film was meant to be a horror mystery with torture scenes. Even if that wasn't their intention, the film was one of the main kicks that launched the popularity of torture porn films. It is partly thanks to Saw that we, as an audience, began to accept more violence in our cinema. Of course there were gruesome horror films before Saw, but the way it and Hostel portrayed violence (not to mention the profits of both) helped encourage studios to push for more works in that style.

## [04] I Know What You Did Last Summer  (1640 car., ~1.6 min)

The postmodern take on horror successfully ridiculed the genre's conventions while being, most importantly, scary. As a subversion of slasher films, it is masterful. Its legacy, however, is less impressive, spawning a number of imitators such as I Know What You Did Last Summer, from nineteen ninety-seven, as well as sequels of steadily declining quality.

Following elegant takes on horror such as The Silence of the Lambs, from nineteen ninety-one, and M. Night Shyamalan's (born in nineteen seventy) hit The Sixth Sense, from nineteen ninety-nine, the genre received new blood from international sources. Japanese horror, or J-horror, made a significant impact in the late nineteen nineties with Ring, from nineteen ninety-eight, followed by Honogurai Mizu No Soko Kara (Dark Water, two thousand two) and Ju-on (Ju-on: The Grudge, two thousand two), all of which were remade by Hollywood.

## [05] [REC]  (1231 car., ~1.2 min)

A fascination with the internet's promotional possibilities and with low-cost handheld filmmaking gave rise to [REC], from two thousand seven, Paranormal Activity, from two thousand nine, The Last Exorcism, from twenty ten, and the phenomenon that was The Blair Witch Project, from nineteen ninety-nine. Yet classic horror mythology, and vampires in particular, continues to supply the most popular themes.

The nineteen nineties gave the world the uneven Interview with the Vampire, from nineteen ninety-four, and Bram Stoker's Dracula, from nineteen ninety-two, followed in the twenty-first century by the Twilight film series (two thousand eight to twenty twelve) and more original works such as thirty Days of Night, from two thousand seven, and Låt den rätte komma in (Let the Right One In, two thousand eight).

Struggling to find the next big wave, attempts to create new horror became more extreme, leading to a proliferation of torture porn and to hits like Hostel, from two thousand five, and the Saw series (two thousand four to twenty ten). Reacting to the violent films that dominated the market, filmmakers tried to raise horror back to the level of critical acclaim achieved by The Exorcist, from nineteen seventy-three.

## [06] Learn more: The Popularity of Found Footage (1)  (1494 car., ~1.5 min)

Learn more: The Popularity of Found Footage.

The first truly significant example of found footage can be found in Cannibal Holocaust, from nineteen eighty, an Italian film whose screenplay centers on the rescue of a group of American filmmakers lost in the Amazon rainforest. Anthropologist Harold Monroe (Robert Kerman) is in charge of the rescue and documents everything on video.

But the genre gained greater prominence with the release of The Blair Witch Project in nineteen ninety-nine. It opens with an introductory text saying that three film students disappeared in a forest in October nineteen ninety-four while shooting a documentary. A year later, their footage was found by an anthropology student. This footage had supposedly been handed over to the police and used as evidence. The police, believing the footage was a hoax, released the films for public viewing. The viewer is then taken through what would have been the students' "witch hunt." There are "six days" of footage, and when the footage ends there is only silence. Made on a very small budget, The Blair Witch Project became a hit with mainstream audiences. People debated it, and the less informed speculated about whether or not it was real. Many disputed the police's opinion, insisting that this footage could not be a "hoax." Little by little, word spread that The Blair Witch Project was a professional production meant to pass as homemade. The Blair Witch Project got a Hollywood-style sequel that never took off.

## [07] Learn more: The Popularity of Found Footage (2)  (2280 car., ~2.3 min)

Almost ten years later, there was a surge of releases in the genre: Paranormal Activity and [REC] were the big names of two thousand seven. The following year came Quarantine, from two thousand eight, and Cloverfield, from two thousand eight. Of the four titles, the most significant in terms of box-office success was Paranormal Activity. One of its standout features is that, like The Blair Witch Project, it relies very little on visual effects: its horror is psychological. As psychologists say, human beings are far more afraid of what they cannot see. The imagination is always more compelling than any concrete image. Some films are not pure found footage but apply the same techniques of psychological horror and naturalistic acting. A good example of a hybrid film is The Fourth Kind, from two thousand nine, starring Milla Jovovich (born in nineteen seventy-five). Here, the production is a blend: there is found footage, but it is part of a staged game, intercut with reenactments by well-known professional actors.

The film opens with Milla speaking to the camera, introducing herself as an actress, as well as the character she will play, Dr. Abigail Tyler. She also states that the film is a dramatization of events that took place from October first to nine, two thousand. She then explains that, to help the viewer better understand the events of the story, the director has included archival footage from Dr. Abigail's psychologist, who personally documented more than sixty-five hours of video and audio material. Milla's statement creates an atmosphere of psychological tension and great realism. The film then unfolds in a context of doubt, suspense and terror. The found footage element complements the horror of the plot, adding a dose of realism and making the viewer (lacking any extra information during the film) afraid of alien abduction.

Generally, horror films have high budgets because special effects are expensive. In the case of found footage, these films enjoy a certain "poetic license": in theory, the actors are the ones filming, and the images always lean toward a more homemade style, so there is no great need to spend money on such effects. In the case of Paranormal Activity, the production cost was around ten thousand dollars, a pittance.

## [08] Frank Darabont  (696 car., ~0.7 min)

Frank Darabont (born in nineteen fifty-nine) wrote and directed the bleak adaptation of Stephen King's The Mist, from two thousand seven. Joss Whedon (born in nineteen sixty-four) was the mastermind behind the subversive The Cabin in the Woods, from twenty twelve, and Kevin Smith (born in nineteen seventy) offered the satirical Red State, from twenty eleven.

Internationally, The Descent, from two thousand five, Los ojos de Julia (Julia's Eyes, twenty ten) and Somos lo que hay (We Are What We Are, twenty ten) offered varied and original visions, showing that, more than any other genre, horror is able to constantly reinvent itself, creating trends, clichés and classics for every generation.
