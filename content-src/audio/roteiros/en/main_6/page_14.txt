## [01] Jaws  (2143 car., ~2.1 min)

Since the release of Jaws, from nineteen seventy-five, summer box office champions (or blockbusters) have been aimed at younger audiences. Epics and dramas for adults are a thing of the past: recent decades have been dominated by family entertainment. For the industry, this shift has meant increased profits that go beyond box office receipts, ranging from toys and other merchandise to sales of videos, DVDs, and Blu-rays, which often generate more revenue than the theatrical release itself.

Disney understood the value of family entertainment from the start. Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, from nineteen thirty-eight, was the most successful film of the year, its hand-drawn animation setting a trend that would last almost fifty years. Disney's last conventional cartoon to dominate the box office was The Lion King, from nineteen ninety-four, a year before a newcomer that came out of nowhere entered the fray.

[CITAÇÃO] One of the few occasions when it's OK to enjoy something for being smart and dumb at the same time, mainly because it's undeniably fun.

said Todd Gilchrist on Transformers.

No studio has dominated family entertainment as completely in recent decades as Pixar. Although it became a subsidiary of The Walt Disney Company in two thousand six, its brand is so strong that it is still regarded as an independent creative entity. In the films it has produced since nineteen ninety-five, its signature blend of dazzling animation with smart, emotionally engaging screenplays that appeal to all ages has won near-unanimous acclaim from critics and audiences alike.

In the Ice Age, from two thousand two, series, from Blue Sky Studios, and the Shrek, from two thousand one, series, from DreamWorks, the originality of their first films stood out, especially in Shrek, with its deconstruction of fairy tales. The hand-drawn animation of The Princess and the Frog, from two thousand nine, marked a dazzling return to a classic style that Disney had practically abandoned. Directors such as Sylvain Chomet (born in nineteen sixty-three) and Hayao Miyazaki (born in nineteen forty-one), at Studio Ghibli, took on the task of filling this gap.

## [02] Learn more: Peter Jackson's Masterpiece (1)  (1839 car., ~1.8 min)

Learn more: Peter Jackson's Masterpiece.

Like the trilogy written by J. R. R. Tolkien, Peter Jackson's adaptation of The Lord of the Rings should be considered a single work, albeit a very long one. Seen in sequence, the three films form an almost perfect whole, from the spectacular prologue to the epic list of credits, a true record-breaker. Everything about this film is unprecedented in the history of cinema. The three parts were shot over a continuous period of sixteen months. And the series' incredible popularity more than lived up to all expectations. It is estimated to have earned almost three billion dollars at the box office, more than any other trilogy, beating even the Star Wars blockbusters. According to some experts' estimates, DVD sales amount to at least the same figure, not to mention the exploitation of a line of licensed products, which means that Middle-earth's GDP rivals that of many real countries. Fortunately, Jackson managed to stun not only the accountants but also the critics. Each film outdid the previous one on best-of lists, and the three together racked up no fewer than thirty Oscar nominations, taking home seventeen statuettes.

If you have seen all three films, you won't be surprised to learn that they won the Oscar for visual effects. They are all packed with monsters and battles that become more impressive with each installment. Even so, it was the tragic figure of Gollum that managed to capture audiences' imagination. Based on movements stored in the computer and on the voice of actor Andy Serkis (born in nineteen sixty-four), who would become a benchmark in motion capture and, a few years later, would play Caesar in the new Planet of the Apes trilogy (twenty eleven to twenty seventeen), the screen Gollum was entirely digital and yet as convincing as a flesh-and-blood character.

## [03] Learn more: Peter Jackson's Masterpiece (2)  (2419 car., ~2.4 min)

Perhaps that is Jackson's greatest achievement: remembering that cutting-edge special effects and technical mastery are not enough to make a great film. Although its leads are hobbits, elves, and wizards, all the characters are deeply human. Gollum has regrets, tries to be good, and taunts Sam, the "fat hobbit." The wizard Gandalf, played to perfection by Ian McKellen (born in nineteen thirty-nine), is at times paternalistic and even mischievous, but never loses his aura of power and authority. And the hobbits Frodo (wide-eyed Elijah Wood) and Sam (Sean Astin) start out as brave but naive adventurers until they attain the status of true heroes. Almost as memorable as the characters is the setting. The trilogy was produced in Jackson's homeland, New Zealand, and very little digital magic was needed to create the dazzling landscapes. One of the most memorable scenes of the spectacular The Lord of the Rings: The Return of the King, from two thousand three, is the lighting of the beacons as the music swells. The camera simply travels from one mountaintop to the next as the warning signal is lit and surges against the sky. Simply marvelous.

It is also remarkable that Jackson and his co-writers Fran Walsh (born in nineteen fifty-nine) and Philippa Boyens (born in nineteen sixty-two) managed to extract a gripping, fast-paced narrative from such a dense and seemingly impenetrable source. Even more remarkable is the fact that even the book's most hardcore fans loved the film. In the short, dramatic prologue, we learn that Sauron, the embodiment of evil, poured all his power into forging a ring, which was lost in an epic battle against an alliance of men and elves. The ring eventually ends up in the hands of the little hobbit Frodo Baggins. This is where the story begins. Frodo agrees to destroy it and thereby defeat Sauron once and for all. However, the ring can only be destroyed if it is cast into the volcanic heart of Mount Doom, which lies, of course, deep inside enemy territory. He sets out on this perilous journey in the company of a group of devoted hobbits, elves, dwarves, and men, including the wizard Gandalf and the mysterious Aragorn (Viggo Mortensen). Betrayal, bloodshed, and battles end up splitting the group, and Frodo, with Sam (and later with Gollum), sets off for the volcano alone, while the others prepare to stand against Sauron in a battle that will shake Middle-earth.

## [04] Learn more: Peter Jackson's Masterpiece (3)  (958 car., ~1.0 min)

Despite the breadth of the canvas Jackson paints, the essence of his work is people, their relationships, and the decisions they must make. Friendship and self-sacrifice are strongly present, as is a sense of mortality. The final scenes make it clear that victory may be noble, yet may still come from an extremely painful personal sacrifice. Tolkien served in the trenches of World War One, and it is hard not to draw parallels between his experiences and the tone of melancholy that ultimately surrounds the final part of the trilogy. For many, the heart of the film lies not in the scenes of bravery but in a quiet moment in which Gandalf consoles Frodo, distraught at having to witness such terrible times. "So do all who live to see such times," says the wizard, "but that is not for them to decide. All we have to decide is what to do with the time that is given to us." It is moments like this that raise Jackson's film to the status of a masterpiece.

## [05] Pirates of the Caribbean  (2421 car., ~2.4 min)

Disney's most successful films of the first decade of the twenty-first century began, incongruous as it may seem, with an amusement park ride. Inspired by the last ride of its kind designed with Disney's funding, producer Jerry Bruckheimer turned it into the Pirates of the Caribbean franchise.

The first film, Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl, from two thousand three, proved to be a surprisingly fun summer hit that harked back to the swashbuckling adventures of Hollywood's golden age, with the eccentric touch of Johnny Depp (born in nineteen sixty-three) as the permanently drunk Captain Jack Sparrow.

Two literary sources were behind the decade's most successful franchises. J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter series got off to a shaky start thanks to the bland direction of Chris Columbus (born in nineteen fifty-eight), but gained momentum with the dark Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban, from two thousand four, directed by Alfonso [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]] (born in nineteen sixty-one).

[CITAÇÃO] How far are you willing to go to save her? - I'd die for her! - Oh good! No worries then.

said Jack Sparrow (Johnny Depp) and Will Turner Three (Orlando Bloom) in Pirates of the Caribbean.

Meanwhile, Peter Jackson (born in nineteen sixty-one) was making the impressive The Lord of the Rings trilogy (two thousand one to two thousand three), brilliantly adapted by Jackson himself, Fran Walsh (born in nineteen fifty-nine), and Philippa Boyens. Clever screenplays were unfortunately left out of the laughable (but hugely successful) Star Wars: Episode I, The Phantom Menace, from nineteen ninety-nine, Star Wars: Episode Two, Attack of the Clones, from two thousand two, and Star Wars: Episode Three, Revenge of the Sith, from two thousand five, which continued George Lucas's (born in nineteen forty-four) Star Wars saga.

Other dominant forces at the box office during this period were the revitalized comic book publishers DC and Marvel. With the success of X-Men, from two thousand, Spider-Man, from two thousand two, and Iron Man, from two thousand eight, Marvel was now able to finance its own productions, starting a trend that would dominate the box office over the next decade. DC got off to a slightly later start, but Batman Begins, from two thousand five, and The Dark Knight, from two thousand eight, both directed by Christopher Nolan (born in nineteen seventy), set the standard for future comic book adaptations.

## [06] Learn more: Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban: The Film That Set the Tone for the Franchise (1)  (2484 car., ~2.5 min)

Learn more: Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban: The Film That Set the Tone for the Franchise.

Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban, as cinema, is one of the best in the series, because it represents a great leap forward from its predecessors. As an adaptation, however, it is somewhat disappointing because of the film's lack of fidelity to the work that gave rise to it. But since we know that literature and cinema are two completely different languages, let us analyze the film from a cinematic point of view.

Chris Columbus, director of the two previous adventures, this time takes on only the role of producer, worn out after so much work. So the Mexican director Alfonso [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]], best known for Y tu mamá también, from two thousand one, was called in to helm this third adventure. Right from the start, it was clear this would be a difficult job, since the third book is one of the most complex and challenging in the series, not only because of its more mature plot, but because it is here that the characters begin to deal more intensely with their teenage feelings. Fortunately, [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]] overcame all these obstacles and created one of the best films in the series, more fun and darker. Despite working with the same crew as the previous films, [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]] makes the production evolve in many respects, starting with the look. While in the first films the costumes, though creative, felt somewhat repetitive, the director made a wise choice in letting the leads wear their own clothes, and this choice works perfectly, since we feel the actors are more at ease in their roles. This gives the film a more modern look, as well as making it more relatable to most of its teenage fans.

And if there is one thing [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]] does well, it is putting his stamp on the film without making it feel "too auteurist." Using his camera more intensely than Columbus, the director opts for close-ups and creative movements, as well as wider shots that convey all the beauty and grandeur of the Hogwarts settings. The director emphasizes the empty, gloomy atmosphere of the landscapes, always overcast, which help create the ideal dark tone. [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]] also gives the film a delightful pace, making it far less tedious than the first two. The customary opening at the Dursleys' house, for example, is quick, but it clearly defines what Potter is now: someone who is growing up, who is starting to take on teenage traits (notice how he is almost always irritated), in short, he is beginning to mature.

## [07] Learn more: Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban: The Film That Set the Tone for the Franchise (2)  (1297 car., ~1.3 min)

The new elements introduced also make the film much more appealing. This time, magical creatures appear in greater numbers, ranging from Hippogriffs (a kind of cross between a horse and an eagle), through a boggart, to the Dementors, beings that suck out people's souls. Once again, the film stands out for the number of well-known names from British film and theater. While Maggie Smith and Alan Rickman need no praise, there are some new additions, such as Michael Gambon, who replaces the late Richard Harris as Albus Dumbledore. Gambon brings a much more active air to the character, erasing the sluggish image Harris had built for the wizard. David Thewlis plays Remus Lupin with great warmth, while Gary Oldman delivers a fantastic portrayal of Sirius Black, the prisoner of the title.

And as already mentioned, Daniel Radcliffe, Rupert Grint, and Emma Watson appear much more at ease in their roles, especially Radcliffe, who here began to show his dramatic talent, embodying all of the young wizard's anguish and fears. And even with its considerable drop in pace in the last twenty minutes, Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban established itself as one of the great entries in the series, the one that will forever be remembered for the new face it gave the young wizard's adventures.

## [08] Avatar  (512 car., ~0.5 min)

In Avatar, from two thousand nine, the most commercially successful film ever made up to that point (it would be surpassed ten years later by Avengers: Endgame, from twenty nineteen), James Cameron (born in nineteen fifty-four) delivered a stunning 3D spectacle, starting a trend of 3D films in the years that followed, though few of them would manage to match its brilliance and good use of the technology. The film also carried a message about protecting the environment, in contrast to most hits of the genre.
