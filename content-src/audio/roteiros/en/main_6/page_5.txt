The diverse European cinema of the nineties.

## [01] Béla Tarr  (1865 car., ~1.9 min)

In the nineteen nineties, European cinema tried to establish a continental identity in the face of what was perceived as Hollywood's cultural imperialism. In the newly redrawn countries of Eastern and Central Europe, filmmakers sought a balance between the desire for a distinctive national cinema and recognition as part of the European tradition. Partly as a consequence of the Cold War, these countries had long-established domestic markets for popular productions, and it fell to just a few directors to make the films with the broadest appeal.

In Hungary, Béla Tarr's (born in nineteen fifty-five) epic Sátántangó (Satantango, nineteen ninety-four) confirmed the director as a natural successor to Miklós Jancsó (born in nineteen twenty-one) and István Szabó (born in nineteen thirty-eight). In the former Yugoslavia, Emir Kusturica (born in nineteen fifty-four) dethroned Dušan Makavejev (nineteen thirty-two to twenty nineteen) with films such as Underground, from nineteen ninety-five, - a surreal comedy about Yugoslavia since World War Two. Both Tarr and Kusturica won international acclaim, which brought few benefits to the filmmakers of their countries.

In Poland, Andrzej Wajda (born in nineteen twenty-six) had handed the role of great national filmmaker to Krzysztof [[pron: Kieślowski|Kyesh-loff-skee]] (nineteen forty-one to nineteen ninety-six) in the nineteen eighties, but the national-icon status of both was undermined by the fact that they moved to France, for political reasons and also drawn by better financial conditions. This behavior was far from rare: [[pron: Kieślowski|Kyesh-loff-skee]]'s "Three Colors trilogy" (nineteen ninety-three to nineteen ninety-four), named after the colors of the French flag, was produced by the Romanian-born Marin Karmitz (born in nineteen thirty-eight), the same producer who later lured Michael Haneke (born in nineteen forty-two) from Austria to France.

Collaborations of this kind not only raise questions about a film's nationality - the "Three Colors" trilogy is made up of French-Polish productions, two of them in French and one largely in Polish - but also suggest that Europe works as a microcosm of the international film industry at a time when Western Europe is losing its rising talent to Hollywood, just as Eastern Europe is losing its own to France.

French film production in this period was paved by a series of expensive, large-scale productions inspired by French literary and historical traditions, usually starring big names. The success of Cyrano de Bergerac, from nineteen ninety, by Jean-Paul Rappeneau (born in nineteen thirty-two), along with that of Jean de Florette, from nineteen eighty-six, by Claude Berri (nineteen thirty-four to two thousand nine), turned Gérard Depardieu into an internationally renowned star.

## [03] Jean-Pierre Jeunet  (1122 car., ~1.1 min)

At the same time, a series of low-budget films by younger directors used Hollywood techniques to express a singular aesthetic vision. The decade saw the rise of major talents such as Jean-Pierre Jeunet (born in nineteen fifty-three) and Marc Caro (born in nineteen fifty-six), who burst onto the international scene with Delicatessen, from nineteen ninety-one, a surreal black comedy set in a post-apocalyptic France. The highly stylized portrayal of suburban youth by Mathieu Kassovitz (born in nineteen sixty-seven) in La Haine (Hate, nineteen ninety-five) focuses on themes of social integration that were on the agenda in contemporary France and resonated internationally. Like Luc Besson (born in nineteen fifty-nine), both Jeunet and Kassovitz went on to make films in the United States.

Hollywood's siren song had a damaging impact on Germany, as many of the talents of the previous decade - Wolfgang Petersen (born in nineteen forty-one) and Roland Emmerich (born in nineteen fifty-five) - also jumped ship. What's more, the promise and prestige attached to the New German Cinema of the nineteen seventies had sunk.

## [04] Tom Tykwer  (1870 car., ~1.9 min)

German cinema struggled to express an identity of its own, and no filmmakers with a consistent body of work emerged. Toward the end of the decade, the romantic thrillers of Tom Tykwer (born in nineteen sixty-five), Winterschläfer (Winter Sleepers, nineteen ninety-seven) and Lola rennt (Run Lola Run, nineteen ninety-eight), offered inspiring hope for the future, but soon afterward he too followed the well-worn path that leads to Hollywood.

In southern Europe, the Italian film industry looked back on the past with nostalgia. Drawing on Neorealism and the comedies of Totò to repeat the success of Nuovo Cinema Paradiso (Cinema Paradiso, nineteen eighty-eight), directed by Giuseppe Tornatore (born in nineteen fifty-six), came Il postino (The Postman, nineteen ninety-four), starring French star Philippe Noiret and directed by Englishman Michael Radford (born in nineteen forty-six), and La vita è bella (Life Is Beautiful, nineteen ninety-seven), by Roberto Benigni (born in nineteen fifty-two). Pedro [[pron: Almodóvar|Al-mo-DOH-var]] (born in nineteen forty-nine) had dominated the national cinema for some time with his esoteric, eccentric vision of post-Franco Spain, filtered through the manic, color-saturated lenses of melodrama, comedy and surrealism. He continued to succeed with films such as La flor de mi secreto (The Flower of My Secret, nineteen ninety-five) and Todo sobre mi madre (All About My Mother, nineteen ninety-nine). However, he had to share the laurels with Julio Medem (born in nineteen fifty-eight), who debuted with the rural drama Vacas (Cows, nineteen ninety-two), and the Chilean Alejandro Amenábar (born in nineteen seventy-two), whose thriller Abre los ojos (Open Your Eyes, nineteen ninety-seven) drew enough international attention to earn a Hollywood remake under the title Vanilla Sky, from two thousand one, with Penélope Cruz again in the lead role.

## [05] Dogme 95 Manifesto  (1036 car., ~1.0 min)

It fell to two Danish directors, Lars von Trier (born in nineteen fifty-six) and Thomas Vinterberg (born in nineteen sixty-nine), to make the seminal attempt at a rebirth of national cinema in this period, with a European outlook. At a symposium held in Paris in nineteen ninety-five to celebrate cinema's first century, they presented the Dogme ninety-five Manifesto and a "Vow of Chastity," a set of rules intended to free artists from the need to compete with Hollywood. Presenting itself as a "rescue action" against the excesses of big-budget filmmaking, the Dogme ninety-five movement set out to counter "certain tendencies" in cinema - a deliberate reference to the famous article "Une certaine tendance du cinéma français" (A Certain Tendency of French Cinema) by François [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] (nineteen thirty-two to nineteen eighty-four), published in the magazine Cahiers du cinéma in nineteen fifty-four. It vowed to overturn modern cinema's cosmetic preoccupations, the predictability of its plots and the superficiality of its action.

## [06] Learn more: Dogma 95 and the Essence of Cinema (1)  (2455 car., ~2.5 min)

Learn more: Dogma ninety-five and the Essence of Cinema.

The Dogma ninety-five movement was an initiative of Danish filmmakers Lars von Trier and Thomas Vinterberg and is regarded as one of the greatest initiatives in film history. It grew out of its official document, called The Manifesto, published on March thirteenth, nineteen ninety-five, in the Danish city of Copenhagen and presented to the whole community at an international symposium on the centenary of cinema. The movement's main proposal was to create a more realistic, less commercial cinema that, in its creators' view, would recover the essence of cinema.

The Manifesto signed by Lars von Trier and Thomas Vinterberg focused on criticizing the overproduction of films, as well as the habit of treating them as true works of art. The idea of treating cinema like a painter's canvas, as well as the use of a range of devices that gave films an exaggeratedly artificial tone, was firmly rejected by both directors. The argument that audiences should not be deceived and that technology took away the director's power as the author of his own work also appears among the Manifesto's ideas. The document also included the "Vow of Chastity," which set out ten principles to be followed under the Dogma ninety-five movement. They are:

Shooting must be done on location. Props and sets must not be brought in (if a particular prop is necessary for the story, a location must be chosen where this prop is to be found). The sound must never be produced apart from the images or vice versa. (Music must therefore not be used.) The camera must be handheld. Any movement - or immobility - attainable by hand is permitted. (The film must not take place where the camera is standing; shooting must take place where the film takes place.) The film must be in color. Special lighting is not acceptable. (If there is too much light, the scene must be cut, or a single lamp may be attached to the camera.) Optical work and filters are forbidden. The film must not contain any "superficial" action. (Murders, weapons and the like must never occur.) Temporal and geographical alienation are forbidden. (That is to say, the film takes place in real time.) Genre films are not acceptable. The film format must be standard thirty-five millimeter. The director must not be credited.

In two thousand five, new principles were added to the Manifesto, to apply to films released from the following year onward. They are:

Filming must be done in digital format. Shooting must take place in Scotland; Shooting cannot exceed a period of six weeks; The total cost of the film cannot exceed one million pounds sterling.

Despite being innovative, the Manifesto is also seen as controversial. Its ideas and films diverge on some points, and the rules are almost impossible to follow. The document's own authors did not follow them strictly. A classic example is Dogville, from two thousand three, directed by von Trier, which, although it followed many of the document's rules, was not recognized by Dogma ninety-five.

By proposing the most faithful possible representation of reality, the Manifesto's creators overlook the fact that reality is not objective. A film's director, like any other human being, has a personal view of things - in other words, is not impartial. This is reflected in how the film is made, what preferences it will have, how the camera is positioned in a scene and how the dialogue will convey what one wants to say. Thus, the representation will never be entirely what is real. Another contradiction is not crediting the director when he will be stamping his own vision on the work.

## [08] Festen  (1801 car., ~1.8 min)

The first Dogme film was Festen (The Celebration, nineteen ninety-eight), shot on Sony Hi-eight video, which follows the story of a family torn apart by a suicide. Von Trier's first venture within the Dogme precepts was Idioterne (The Idiots, nineteen ninety-eight), which became notorious for its frank treatment of sexuality and disability. The success of The Celebration, together with the notoriety of The Idiots, earned Dogme a global audience. By nineteen ninety-nine, the movement had already spread to France with Lovers, from nineteen ninety-nine, by Jean-Marc Barr (born in nineteen sixty), and to the United States with Julien Donkey-Boy, from nineteen ninety-nine, by Harmony Korine (born in nineteen seventy-three). It became a worldwide phenomenon and, ironically, by looking into the very guts of Europe, Dogme ninety-five gave birth to a movement with global impact.

Some smaller countries whose output seemed likewise to center on one or two directors - such as Greece with Theodoros Angelopoulos (born in nineteen thirty-five), Portugal with Pedro Costa (born in nineteen fifty-nine) and Manoel de Oliveira (nineteen oh-eight to twenty fifteen), or Austria with Haneke - struggled to have their remarkable films labeled "European" rather than hailed as signs of a supposed new wave. Angelopoulos's Mia Aioniotita Kai Mia Mera (Eternity and a Day, nineteen ninety-eight) follows a cinematic tradition of depicting the universal theme of a dying man making peace with life. Yet throughout the decade, the tendency to identify individual films as national rather than European seemed almost impossible to change.

## [09] Funny Games  (723 car., ~0.7 min)

Thus Haneke, German by birth and raised in Austria, struggled throughout the decade to shake off the common view of his films as indictments of Austrian society rather than of Western culture as a whole - even when, as happened with his parody of the thriller, Funny Games, from nineteen ninety-seven, he shot in an unspecified location that could have been anywhere in rural Europe, with language as the only clue to the film's origin. Or Von Trier, regarded as essentially a Danish director, yet whose films rarely refer to Denmark. It is only when directors leave their countries that they manage to escape national borders: a testament to the complexity of the tension between nation and Europe that defines the decade.
