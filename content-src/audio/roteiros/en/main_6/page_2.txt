## [01] Robert Altman  (940 car., ~0.9 min)

The nineteen nineties saw the return of directors and actors who had made landmark films in the past. Long after his heyday in the nineteen seventies, Robert Altman (nineteen twenty-five to two thousand six) reemerged with the excellent The Player, from nineteen ninety-two, and Short Cuts, from nineteen ninety-three. The latter's fragmented narrative and huge cast prompted favorable comparisons with another of the director's works, Nashville, from nineteen seventy-five.

Terrence Malick (born in nineteen forty-three), who returned to filmmaking with The Thin Red Line, from nineteen ninety-eight, after a twenty-year hiatus, chose to tackle World War Two, casting a philosophical eye on the conflict in the Pacific. Among actors, two giants of cinema shared the screen for the first time when Al Pacino (born in nineteen forty) and Robert De Niro (born in nineteen forty-three) starred in the crime epic Heat, from nineteen ninety-five.

From his earliest work, Steven Spielberg showed an almost uncanny gift for filmmaking, above all for suspense machinations and special effects, a gift to which audiences responded in droves. But the resounding success of early films such as Jaws, from nineteen seventy-five, and Close Encounters of the Third Kind, from nineteen seventy-seven, - which helped cement modern Hollywood's blockbuster mentality - somehow hurt Spielberg's reputation. Longing for respect, he knew his films would always be regarded as mere mass entertainment and technical exercises.

Even so, the director tried to broaden his range with mature efforts such as The Color Purple, from nineteen eighty-five, and Empire of the Sun, from nineteen eighty-seven, but it was only with Schindler's List, from nineteen ninety-three, the harrowing Holocaust narrative, that he finally produced a film that pleased critics and audiences alike. With the exception of his two high-impact action films, Jurassic Park, from nineteen ninety-three, and The Lost World: Jurassic Park, from nineteen ninety-seven, Spielberg has remained more or less serious ever since.

Saving Private Ryan introduced something new into the director's vocabulary: a violent, visceral intensity. Spielberg had dealt with violence before, of course, but typically in a cartoonish way or else carefully restrained (and later exploited) as a dramatic counterpoint to punctuate moments of extreme horror. Ryan's opening battle sequence, however, is relentless. As soon as the American boats reach the Normandy beach, young men are torn apart by incessant hails of machine-gun fire, whose bullets pierce their useless helmets with a sickening sound. And it does not end there. Soldiers are blown to pieces. Limbs fly through the air and litter the ground. The sea turns blood red. The camera moves erratically as the film takes on the color of death. The "new" Spielberg takes no prisoners.

It is true that the Spielberg of old cannot always resist the pull of sentimentality. A pair of flimsy bookends opens and closes the film on a purely manipulative note, and Spielberg encourages John Williams, his favorite composer, to indulge his most tear-jerking tendencies. What is even more uncomfortable is that Spielberg uses the same techniques to show the horrors of war not only in his stunning opening battle scene but also in the more traditional good-versus-evil conflict of the film's conclusion.

But the familiar good guy-bad guy dichotomy ends up turning the antiwar message into something more challenging. War is hell, Spielberg acknowledges, except when you are winning, because, in the end, it is victory that confers the right to dictate the rules of morality. Saving Private Ryan contains enough darkness behind the smoke of war to fend off accusations of being blindly patriotic. Looking out over thousands of graves, a soldier asks his wife: "Tell me I'm a good man." It is an ambiguous way of asking not only whether "it was worth it," but also whether it was justified. These are questions that surely stay with all those who survive when the camera stops, revealing the death and destruction all around, even if caused by the noblest of causes.

## [04] Martin Scorsese  (1974 car., ~2.0 min)

De Niro continued his partnership with longtime ally Martin Scorsese (born in nineteen forty-two) in GoodFellas, from nineteen ninety, which took the director back to the streets of his youth for the first of three films set in New York. Next came The Age of Innocence, from nineteen ninety-three, and Bringing Out the Dead, from nineteen ninety-nine, but the city's existential angst was better captured by Abel Ferrara (born in nineteen fifty-one): King of New York, from nineteen ninety, and Bad Lieutenant, from nineteen ninety-two, are two of the greatest and darkest cinematic portrayals of the city.

Spike Lee (born in nineteen fifty-seven), one of New York's most famous sons, exposed the demand for films depicting African American life with Jungle Fever, from nineteen ninety-one, Malcolm X, from nineteen ninety-two, and Clockers, from nineteen ninety-five. Mario Van Peebles's (born in nineteen fifty-seven) New Jack City, from nineteen ninety-one, and Menace Two Society, from nineteen ninety-three, by the Hughes brothers (Albert and Allen, b. nineteen seventy-two), reinforced that interest, although lesser directors seemed more interested in glamorizing violence than in focusing on the human drama.

Combining human drama with violence laced with black humor, Quentin Tarantino (born in nineteen sixty-three) easily earned himself the title of enfant terrible of American cinema. Cleverly crafted works such as Reservoir Dogs, from nineteen ninety-two, Pulp Fiction, from nineteen ninety-four, and Jackie Brown, from nineteen ninety-seven, showed that he is a talented, if willful, filmmaker. He also wrote the original screenplay for Natural Born Killers, from nineteen ninety-four, which, along with Reservoir Dogs, Bad Lieutenant, from nineteen ninety-two, and Crash, from nineteen ninety-six, ranks among the most controversial films of the decade.

## [05] The Truman Show  (554 car., ~0.6 min)

Among Hollywood dramas aimed at a mass audience, The Truman Show, from nineteen ninety-eight, offered a perfect platform for the dramatic talents of Jim Carrey (born in nineteen sixty-two) and was Peter Weir's (born in nineteen forty-four) best film since Witness, from nineteen eighty-five. James Cameron's (born in nineteen fifty-four) Titanic, from nineteen ninety-seven, swept the box office and the Oscars in nineteen ninety-eight. However, Cameron's screenplay, the film's weakest element, lost out to Good Will Hunting, from nineteen ninety-seven.

Fight Club is perhaps the high point of David Fincher's career. Many people will probably read that statement with disdain, preferring to name other films by the director as his best, but in terms of the perfect union of form and substance, performances, blend of special effects, and message, Fight Club is, at the very least, his most thought-provoking and intriguing work. After all, the protagonist is the narrator (Edward Norton), but he has no name, recalling the John Doe of Se7en, from nineteen ninety-five. He is an ordinary man, designed so that anyone in the audience can identify with him. He suffers from insomnia and can only cure it by having moments of catharsis in support groups for all kinds of diseases and problems. There he meets Marla Singer (Helena Bonham Carter), another faker like him, who, because he sees his own sham in her, sends him back to square one with his insomnia. He is a recall specialist; that is, he determines whether, mathematically, it is worthwhile for the company he works for to recall the cars it manufactures or whether it is better business to let people die. In this back-and-forth across the United States, he meets Tyler Durden on a plane, one of his "single-serving" friends, who makes soap.

Together, they find a new form of catharsis, fighting each other in the middle of the street. Soon this craze draws the attention of others, and Fight Club then takes shape. From there, Durden, with his natural leadership and anarchist ideas, begins to build a veritable cult that goes around committing anti-establishment acts across the country. But Tyler Durden does not exist. He is merely a way the nameless narrator's mind found to escape his unbearable daily routine, his conformity to what society dictates. After all, why would anyone in their right mind need a yin-yang-shaped coffee table, or Italian shoes, or a trendy apartment? And this revelation is cleverly woven into the film and is there for anyone to see, done even more cleverly than M. Night Shyamalan (born in nineteen seventy) did - in the same year, mind you! - in The Sixth Sense, from nineteen ninety-nine. Famously, David Fincher embodied the anarchist motto of Chuck Palahniuk's book, which provided the basis for Jim Uhls's (born in nineteen fifty-seven) brilliant screenplay, and worked that atmosphere visually. We know something is wrong, but the first time we see the film, we cannot pinpoint what it is and only find out at the end.

But Fight Club is not its ending, just as The Sixth Sense is not either. The film is, in fact, an extremely rich and pleasurable thematic treasure that, in a nutshell, criticizes the status quo. The anarchy the film proposes is liberating, critical, intelligent, profound. It is no joke. What we watch makes us think, makes us look around and realize that having for the sake of having is almost an addiction imposed from the top down. Chances are we do not need - or even truly want - what we have or think we want. Look around you and see if that is not exactly the case.

## [08] Learn more: The First Rule of Fight Club (3)  (1446 car., ~1.4 min)

From the very first frame of his fourth fiction feature, David Fincher handles computer graphics in exemplary fashion. We begin literally inside the Narrator's head and set off on a gigantic flashback recounting how he ended up in that desperate situation, with the barrel of a gun stuck in his mouth. And this perfect fusion of computer and practical effects continues throughout the film, giving the director broad freedom to subvert what we have grown used to expecting from movies. Whether it is the reconstruction of the explosion of the Narrator's apartment or his brief cataclysmic dream on the plane, everything is woven into the work without our noticing when the CGI, supervised by Kevin Tod Haug (born in nineteen fifty-six), kicks in (a Fincher trademark in his work).

In addition, the elegance of Fight Club's production design is impressive. From convincing, distinctive costumes for each character - it is hilarious to watch the deterioration of the Narrator's wardrobe - to haunting sets such as Tyler Durden's "mansion," everything works to shake us out of the torpor we live in and to bring Uhls's fascinating screenplay to life. And the cinematography by Jeff Cronenweth (born in nineteen sixty-two) - son of Jordan Cronenweth (nineteen thirty-five to nineteen ninety-six), who worked with Fincher on Alien³, from nineteen ninety-two, - carries aspects of both Se7en and The Game, from nineteen ninety-seven, over to Fight Club.

Fincher also avoided the obvious with the score and delivered something powerful when he chose the Dust Brothers to create something postmodern, heavy, and representative of the kind of counterculture and anarchy put forward by the original work. The result is exhilarating, with clever music syncing that emphasizes, without overdramatizing, the film's key moments. Last but not least, there is the main trio. Of the three, Bonham Carter stands out the least, since she literally repeats the type of performance we have grown used to seeing from her. That does not mean, however, that she is not good. Still, by comparison, her work pales next to Norton and Pitt who, each in his own way, give memorable performances. The Narrator's transformation from "ordinary man" to leader of Project Mayhem (or second-in-command...) is worthy of a tip of the hat, full of nuances that make his journey perfectly believable. Tyler Durden does not undergo the same degree of transformation, but since his personality only fully emerges in the final third, we can still understand the development of this projection of the Narrator's psyche without finding it strange that the soap salesman we are introduced to on the plane is the same man who blows up entire buildings at the end. Fight Club is a one-of-a-kind experience. An audiovisual triumph that needs to be seen again and again to be appreciated in all its fullness.

## [10] Steven Spielberg  (1766 car., ~1.8 min)

Steven Spielberg (born in nineteen forty-six) balanced popcorn movies such as Jurassic Park, from nineteen ninety-three, with more serious dramas such as Schindler's List, from nineteen ninety-three, a powerful account of the Holocaust with an excellent re-creation of the Warsaw ghetto and of life in an extermination camp. The film's documentary style was used again in Saving Private Ryan, from nineteen ninety-eight.

Besides these veterans, four young directors offered glimpses of brilliant potential. David Fincher (born in nineteen sixty-two) caught the attention of audiences and critics with Fight Club, from nineteen ninety-nine, which, despite being a box office failure, would become a cult classic of the nineteen nineties. Bryan Singer's (born in nineteen sixty-five) The Usual Suspects, from nineteen ninety-five, led audiences through a provocative maze, thanks to the twisty screenplay by Christopher McQuarrie (born in nineteen sixty-eight).

Boogie Nights, from nineteen ninety-seven, directed with remarkable assurance by Paul Thomas Anderson (born in nineteen seventy), re-created the Los Angeles porn scene of the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties, hinting at the body of work that would make him one of the most original filmmakers of the following decade.
