A portrait of European cinema in the twenty-first century.

## [01] Michael Haneke  (1880 car., ~1.9 min)

When future generations look back on the first decade of the twenty-first century, they may see it as something of a golden age of European cinema. For it was during this period that European Union incentives, introduced in the nineteen nineties, finally bore fruit: once the material advantages for directors and producers working within Europe had been proven, co-productions stopped being the exception and became the norm.

Filmmakers such as Michael Haneke (born in nineteen forty-two) and Paul Verhoeven (born in nineteen thirty-eight) can move between different countries - including the United States - without losing their identities as auteurs. Antichrist, from two thousand nine, by Lars von Trier (born in nineteen fifty-six), was made with European money but shot in English; its leads were a Frenchwoman and an American. However, between that film and Dogville, from two thousand three, Von Trier made De Fem Bespaend (The Five Obstructions, two thousand three) and Direktøren for Det Hele (The Boss of It All, two thousand six), the two films in his body of work most concerned with Danish issues.

Von Trier is perhaps the greatest example of this malleable form of nationalism and auteur identity. Although he had already made an English-language film in nineteen ninety-six - Breaking the Waves, from nineteen ninety-six, - the year two thousand marked the release of his first truly international production, Dancer in the Dark, from two thousand. Starring Björk, the film won two awards at that year's Cannes Film Festival - among them the Palme d'Or. The Swedish film Män Som Hatar Kvinnor (The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo, two thousand nine), adapted by Niels Arden Oplev (born in nineteen sixty-one) from the novel by fellow Scandinavian Stieg Larsson, proved a hit with both critics and audiences, and an American adaptation was soon planned for twenty eleven.

## [02] Das Experiment  (1462 car., ~1.5 min)

The two countries that had struggled to find a coherent cinematic identity during the nineteen eighties and nineteen nineties - Germany and Italy - gave rise to two quite distinct, even divergent, movements in the two thousands. From Germany came a series of big-budget films that dealt with historical events without being nostalgic in any way, but were marked by a concern with the country's turbulent past. After directing the modern Das Experiment (The Experiment, two thousand one), a contemporary look at Nazi psychology, Oliver Hirschbiegel (born in nineteen fifty-seven) made, in two thousand four, Der Untergang (Downfall, two thousand four), which chronicled the dictator's final days.

The film was followed in two thousand five by Sophie Scholl, Die letzten Tage (Sophie Scholl: The Final Days, two thousand five), a World War Two drama directed by Marc Rothemund (born in nineteen sixty-eight). In two thousand three, Wolfgang Becker (born in nineteen fifty-four) took a shrewd look at the aftermath of reunification in the sweetly comic Good Bye, Lenin!, from two thousand three, while Uli Edel (born in nineteen forty-seven) turned to the nineteen seventies and the terrorist actions of the Red Army Faction in Der Baader Meinhof Komplex (The Baader Meinhof Complex, two thousand eight).

## [03] Das Leben der Anderen  (1317 car., ~1.3 min)

Yet perhaps it was the Cold War surveillance drama Das Leben der Anderen (The Lives of Others, two thousand six), directed by Florian Henckel von Donnersmarck (born in nineteen seventy-three), that defined German cinema in the two thousands. The film achieved immense popularity around the world, even winning an Oscar, but it also stirred controversy for its deeply moving and sympathetic portrayal of an officer of the Stasi, the secret police of the German Democratic Republic.

To the defenders of these films, they represented a belated reckoning by the German film industry with the Nazi past. Their detractors, on the other hand, dismissed them as cynical and insensitive attempts to profit from it. The Austrian-German Die Fälscher (The Counterfeiters, two thousand seven), by Stefan Ruzowitzky (born in nineteen sixty-one), attracted considerable attention. Austrian cinema had long been seen as the poor cousin of German cinema, but in the two thousands it was on the rise. Young filmmakers (often women) who came out of the Vienna Film Academy, such as Jessica Hausner (born in nineteen seventy-two), with Lourdes, from two thousand nine, and Valeska Grisebach (born in nineteen sixty-eight), with Sehnsucht (Longing, two thousand six), produced subtle and original works that sparked international interest.

## [04] Marco Bellocchio  (1486 car., ~1.5 min)

Italy also looked to the past, but with very different results. Marco Bellocchio (born in nineteen thirty-nine) created an unsettling dramatization of the terrorist activities of the nineteen seventies in Buongiorno, notte (Good Morning, Night, two thousand three). This film, together with Le conseguenze dell'amore (The Consequences of Love, two thousand four), by Paolo Sorrentino (born in nineteen seventy), became a calling card for a new generation of Italian directors, who spoke out both verbally and cinematically against the unbearable weight of the filmic nostalgia that had either stifled the country's cinema or reduced it to a cheap imitation of itself over the previous two decades.

Direct criticism of Silvio Berlusconi's contemporary government was almost impossible, thanks to deplorable acts of censorship. Nevertheless, films such as Mio fratello è figlio unico (My Brother Is an Only Child, two thousand seven) and Gomorra (Gomorrah, two thousand eight) delve into violence and corruption in Italy past and present. These directors' current perspective is marked by new aesthetic approaches that tend to be overstylized, or frenetic and flamboyant, though painted in dull, faded tones - an antidote, perhaps, to the sun-drenched melodramas of the nineteen eighties and the languid pace of the nineteen sixties and of directors such as Michelangelo Antonioni (nineteen twenty-one to two thousand seven) and Federico Fellini (nineteen twenty to nineteen ninety-three).

## [05] Gurinder Chadha  (1953 car., ~1.9 min)

The United Kingdom, France, and Germany in particular witnessed the emergence of a large number of second- and third-generation directors from ethnic minorities, such as Gurinder Chadha (born in nineteen sixty) and Asif Kapadia (born in nineteen seventy-two), Abdellatif Kechiche (born in nineteen sixty) and Rachid Bouchareb (born in nineteen fifty-three), Fatih Akin (born in nineteen seventy-three) and Thomas Arslan (born in nineteen sixty-two). Their works deal with issues such as social integration and the way hybrid cultures are negotiated. In Germany, Akin, a Turkish-German filmmaker, set out to change the German perception of a monocultural, ethnically homogeneous society with films such as Gegen die Wand (Head-On, two thousand four). The Belgian brothers Jean-Pierre (born in nineteen fifty-one) and Luc Dardenne (born in nineteen fifty-four), winners of the Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival, were perhaps a classic case of festival filmmakers, twenty-first-century poetic realists whose bleak reflections on life among the lowest strata of society were acclaimed by critics but failed to draw audiences.

Within what came to be called the new Europe, however, Romania proved to be an exception. Elsewhere, the tendency was for the representation of national cinemas to be limited to a handful of filmmakers who seemed to burst suddenly onto the international circuit, only to fall back into obscurity. Examples include Bosnian Danis Tanovic (born in nineteen sixty-nine), with No Man's Land, from two thousand one, and Hungarian György Pálfi (born in nineteen seventy-four), with Taxidermia, from two thousand six. The early twenty-first century marked a period of prosperity for European cinema, but the gulf between Eastern and Western Europe remains the same.
