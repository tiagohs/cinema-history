## [01] Zhang Yimou  (824 car., ~0.8 min)

During the nineteen nineties, the leading Chinese filmmakers of the Fifth Generation increasingly sought foreign audiences and courted international film festivals, even while facing censorship at home. Zhang Yimou (born in nineteen fifty-one) directed Da Hong Deng Long Gao Gao Gua (Raise the Red Lantern, nineteen ninety-one), which won the Silver Lion at Venice.

With the Palme d'Or winner Ba Wang Bie Ji (Farewell My Concubine, nineteen ninety-three), Chen Kaige (born in nineteen fifty-two) traced the relationship between two Peking Opera stars through the Cultural Revolution. Celebrated abroad, these films were temporarily banned in China. The Fifth Generation drew hostility and criticism not only from the state but also from younger filmmakers, who longed for a more direct engagement with modern Chinese reality.

## [02] Learn more: The Visual Poetry of Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon  (2570 car., ~2.6 min)

Learn more: The Visual Poetry of Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon.

Director Ang Lee said his intention with Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon was simply to make the best martial arts film possible. The artistic success and popularity of the result suggest that he succeeded. Working from a screenplay by his longtime collaborator James Schamus (born in nineteen fifty-nine), Lee assembled a cast of Asian action superstars who also had the artistic talent needed to let the story flow.

Lee also made a brilliant decision in recruiting the famous fight choreographer Yuen Woo-ping. Inspired by the physicality of Chinese theater, Yuen defies the laws of physics, hoisting the actors on wires so they can fight atop the trees, skim across water like skipping stones, run up walls and, several times, battle in midair. For Western audiences, perhaps unaccustomed to these aerial ballets, the sight of the opening scene's chase on foot across village rooftops on a moonlit night proved immediately and irresistibly appealing.

Chow Yun-Fat (born in nineteen fifty-five), Michelle Yeoh (born in nineteen sixty-two) and the relative newcomer Zhang Ziyi (born in nineteen seventy-nine) handle the supernatural physical demands of their roles with ease, but their performances never fall short of the impressive action scenes. Indeed, one of the reasons Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon became such a sensation was the attention the film paid to plot and character development, two rarities even for the most ambitious martial arts films.

A strongly feminist undercurrent also runs through Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon, adding another layer of intelligence. The female characters fight for different reasons, but most of them seem to be fighting for respect in a man's world. Having run away from home, young Zhang, passing herself off as a male warrior, is attacked at an inn by several truculent fighters. Lee turns the inevitable beating she gives dozens of would-be attackers into a joke, a hyperkinetic variation on the old bar brawl. But part of Zhang's passionate response seems to stem from a long-held resentment at believing she would be scorned rather than respected (or feared) as a formidable opponent if they knew she was a woman.

Lee modulates every scene of the film with similar nuance and sensitivity. His fight sequences play out as much as psychological contests as sword fights, with clashes between innocence and experience, peace and rage. Still, Lee leaves room for lightness and visual poetry, striking a rare balance of excitement, beauty, humor and intelligence.

## [03] Xiaowen Zhou  (1422 car., ~1.4 min)

A notable transitional figure was Xiaowen Zhou (born in nineteen fifty-four), who technically belongs to the Fifth Generation but whose chilling satire Ermo, from nineteen ninety-four, already showed the engagement with contemporary society that would characterize the next generation.

The Chinese director with the greatest international impact was Taiwan's Ang Lee (born in nineteen fifty-four), whose first two films, Tui shou (Pushing Hands, nineteen ninety-two) and Xi yan (The Wedding Banquet, nineteen ninety-three), are set in the United States and depict the culture clash between Asia and the West. While other Taiwanese directors such as Edward Yang (nineteen forty-seven to two thousand seven) made art films, like Yi yi, from two thousand, Lee directed the stylish martial arts film Wo Hu Cang Long (Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon, two thousand), which inspired Ying Xiong (Hero, two thousand two) and Shi Mian Mai Fu (House of Flying Daggers, two thousand four).

These visually splendid epics found eager audiences abroad and sealed the internationalization of East Asian cinema. This can also be seen in the work of Hong Kong director Wong Kar-Wai (born in nineteen fifty-six), who in films such as Hua Yang De Nian Hua (In the Mood for Love, two thousand) opted for a so-called "MTV aesthetic," discarding the austerity of Asian art cinema in favor of the style of international commercials and music videos.

## [04] Hong Sang-Soo  (1532 car., ~1.5 min)

While Chinese directors were still wrestling with censorship, South Korean cinema benefited from its liberation from state control in the democratic era. The result was the maturing of the country's filmmaking, which also began to attract international attention. Hong Sang-Soo (born in nineteen sixty) was acclaimed by local and foreign critics for his debut film, Daijiga umule pajinnal (The Day a Pig Fell Into the Well, nineteen ninety-six), which tells four loosely interconnected life stories in modern Seoul.

Chan-Wook Park (born in nineteen sixty-three) won the Grand Prix at the Cannes Film Festival for Oldboy, from two thousand three, a brutal revenge story. Kim Ki-Duk (born in nineteen sixty) gained fame and notoriety with the ultraviolent Seom (The Isle, two thousand), but later opted for a more serene style with the Buddhist parable Bom Yeoreum Gaeul Gyeoul Geurigo Bom (Spring, Summer, Fall, Winter... and Spring, two thousand three).

The success of these films showed the genuine enthusiasm of some Korean audiences for their national cinema. Seopyeonje (Sopyonje, nineteen ninety-three), an underrated melodrama about a family of folk singers directed by Im Kwon-taek (born in nineteen thirty-six), became the most commercially successful Korean film up to that time, but was surpassed in nineteen ninety-nine by Swiri (Shiri, nineteen ninety-nine).

## [05] Learn more: The Ultimate Revenge Story  (2130 car., ~2.1 min)

Learn more: The Ultimate Revenge Story.

Mysticism, poetry, school days and a futuristic bedroom are some of the surprises found in Oldboy, a thriller that draws on Oedipal taboos and the blindness of chance, on hypnotism and fate. Part action film, part drama, part psychological thriller, this groundbreaking work introduced South Korean cinema to more viewers than any other. The story is more direct and engaging than that of director Park Chan-Wook's previous film, the popular Boksuneun Naui Geot (Sympathy for Mr. Vengeance, two thousand two), and it starts explosively. A man is imprisoned for fifteen years without any explanation. When he escapes, he has only five days to find those responsible for his incarceration.

This violent, elegiac masterpiece is based on a Japanese manga by Garon Tsuchiya. Actor Choi Min-sik (born in nineteen sixty-two), who did without stunt doubles, trained rigorously for the role of the hapless victim of a mysterious kidnapping. Dae-su manages to escape from his windowless prison and, once free, in the manner of the Count of Monte Cristo, he swears revenge on the jailer who robbed him of his daughter, his wife and his entire life. Transformed into a ragged fighting machine, like a Korean Charles Bronson in a cheap wig, Dae-su befriends a beautiful sushi-bar chef (Hye-Jeong Kang), whose interest he piques by eating a live squid and then passing out face-first on the counter. That is the blockbuster style of Oldboy. But this complex tale is imbued with a clearly unfolding logic, easily incorporating the film's many flashbacks. The pace of the action is breathtaking, but there is also time for thought. The choreographed fights are both innovative and surprising, and all the violence serves the plot.

Despite the prevailing brutality, this is a film that is, at heart, a black comedy that skillfully combines elements of gangster films and revenge thrillers. After winning the Grand Prix at the two thousand four Cannes Film Festival, the director stunned the audience by thanking the cast and crew, as well as the four squid that sacrificed their lives for that sushi-bar scene.
