## [02] Phase Four  (1770 car., ~1.8 min)

The problem began with volume. With Disney+ up and running since twenty nineteen, Marvel started producing for two screens at once. The so-called Phase Four, launched by the series WandaVision on January fifteenth, twenty twenty-one, comprised seven films, eight series and two specials in less than two years, an output that led even the entertainment press to ask whether the studio still had a map. For the average viewer, every new movie started to feel like homework: you had to have watched the series to know who was who. Behind the scenes, the strain showed too. In September twenty twenty-three, Marvel's visual effects artists voted unanimously to unionize, complaining of tight deadlines, unpaid overtime and overwork on the franchise's films and series. The machine kept turning, but ever faster and with ever less care.

In twenty twenty-three, the cracks became impossible to hide. The year ended with another blow: in December, Jonathan Majors, who played Kang, the villain meant to anchor the entire new saga, was fired after being convicted of assault, and Marvel had to redraw its plans.

While the shared universes spun their wheels, another kind of film was winning back theaters: the event film, the kind audiences feel they have to see on the biggest screen possible. The founding case was Joseph Kosinski's Top Gun: Maverick, from twenty twenty-two. Originally slated for twenty nineteen and postponed several times, first to finish the action sequences and then because of the pandemic, the film drew offers from streaming platforms, all of them turned down by Tom Cruise, who insisted on an exclusive theatrical release. The aerial scenes were shot for real, with IMAX-certified cameras mounted inside the cockpits of F/A-eighteen fighter jets, yielding more than eight hundred hours of flight footage. Released in May twenty twenty-two, it grossed about one point five billion dollars, the biggest box office of Cruise's career. At the end of that same year, James Cameron's Avatar: The Way of Water, from twenty twenty-two, reached two point thirty-three billion dollars, betting on 3D, IMAX and high-frame-rate projection.

The year twenty twenty-six summed up the decade. Learn more: IMAX: the theater as the selling point.

For decades, IMAX was associated with museum documentaries. In the twenty twenties, it became the place where blockbusters are won or lost. With just over one thousand eight hundred screens worldwide, a tiny fraction of the circuit, the company closed twenty twenty-five with a record one point twenty-eight billion dollars at the box office, thirteen percent above its previous best year, twenty nineteen. Christopher Nolan turned the format into a signature, and The Odyssey, from twenty twenty-six, was the first feature shot entirely on IMAX film cameras. Tickets for the seventy millimeter screenings went on sale on July seventeenth, twenty twenty-five, a year before the release, and some sold out in under twelve hours. Only forty-one screens in the world could show that version, and IMAX even refurbished old projectors and trained new projectionists to meet demand. In August twenty twenty-six, the film became the highest-grossing release in IMAX history, surpassing the first Avatar. In a market where almost everything reaches streaming within a few weeks, the theater itself became the selling point.

## [10] Part 8  (778 car., ~0.8 min)

The return of the event neatly closes an era that began with the screens going dark. In March twenty twenty, theaters around the world shut down, and many people bet that streaming had won for good. Five years later, screenings of The Odyssey were selling out a year ahead of release. Along the way, cinema changed shape: it learned to be born from memes and YouTube channels, saw horror and films in languages other than English move to the center, went on strike over streaming and artificial intelligence, and watched major studios merge. What didn't change was the reason to leave the house. In an age when almost everything fits on a phone screen, the movie theater survived by becoming the home of what doesn't fit there: spectacle seen together, in the dark, as an event.
