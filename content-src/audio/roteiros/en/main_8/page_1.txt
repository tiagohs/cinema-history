## [00] Opening  (306 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero eight: From twenty twenty to today. Chapter one: Dark Screens: The Pandemic and the Movies.

In March twenty twenty, COVID-nineteen shut down movie theaters across the entire planet. Amid postponements, drive-ins and the war over release windows, the way films are released would never be the same.

## [01] Parasite  (777 car., ~0.8 min)

The twenty twenties began with a celebration. On February ninth, Parasite, from twenty nineteen, won the Oscar for Best Picture, the first ever for a non-English-language film, and cinema seemed more global and more alive than ever. A month later, the world stopped. On March eleventh, the World Health Organization declared COVID-nineteen a pandemic. China had already closed some seventy thousand screens on January twenty-third, the eve of the Lunar New Year, the most lucrative time of year for the country's theaters. Italy, France, Spain, the United States and Brazil followed within days. By mid-March, the two biggest American chains, AMC and Regal, had closed all their theaters. In more than a century of history, never had so many screens gone dark at the same time.

## [02] No Time to Die  (1875 car., ~1.9 min)

The first warning came even before the WHO's declaration. On March fourth, the producers of No Time to Die, from twenty twenty-one, Daniel Craig's farewell as James Bond, pushed its release from April to November twenty twenty: it was the first major film to change dates because of the virus, and it would not be Bond's last delay. In Brazil, five hundred seventy-seven screens had already gone dark by March seventeenth. That same week, distributors canceled their releases, and on Thursday, March nineteenth, for the first time in history, not a single film opened in the country's theaters. Cinemark, Kinoplex, Cinépolis, UCI and Espaço Itaú, Brazil's main theater chains, closed their doors with no date to reopen. According to Ancine, Brazil's national film agency, the country's moviegoing audience, which had reached one hundred seventy-seven point seven million admissions in twenty nineteen, plunged to thirty-nine point four million in twenty twenty.

Festivals had to reinvent themselves. The Cannes Film Festival, scheduled for May, at first tried to postpone its edition while part of the Palais des Festivals served as a temporary shelter for homeless people. In April, it admitted it would not take place "in its original form" and ended up announcing a selection under the Cannes twenty twenty label, with no competition and no Palme d'Or, something that had not happened since nineteen sixty-eight. The Venice Film Festival was the first of the major festivals to return in person, from September second to twelve, with mandatory masks, an empty seat between every two spectators, temperature checks at the entrance and a red carpet with no crowds. The Golden Lion went to Nomadland, from twenty twenty, by Chloé Zhao. The film premiered on the same day in Venice, in Toronto and at a drive-in set up in the parking lot of the Rose Bowl stadium in California.

## [03] Part 3  (977 car., ~1.0 min)

The Rose Bowl drive-in was not an isolated stopgap. With indoor theaters closed, a format that had seemed like a museum piece was back in fashion. In the United States, open-air theaters, where each family watches the movie from inside its own car, stayed open and became a hot ticket. In Brazil, the Cine Drive-in Brasília, opened in nineteen seventy-three and one of the last survivors of its kind in the country, reopened on the May first holiday in twenty twenty at half capacity and sold two thousand eight hundred tickets that first weekend. In São Paulo, the Petra Belas Artes art house set up a one hundred-car drive-in at the Memorial da América Latina starting June sixteenth, programming classics by Nolan and Stanley Kubrick. Similar initiatives sprang up in the Brazilian state of Minas Gerais and in many other countries. The drive-in saved no one financially, but it became the symbol of an audience that wanted, by any means necessary, to watch movies together.

## [04] Christopher Nolan  (1940 car., ~1.9 min)

Amid the chaos, one filmmaker became the spokesman for the movie theater. Christopher Nolan wanted Tenet, from twenty twenty, starring John David Washington, to be Hollywood's first major release after the shutdown, and he persuaded Warner Bros. not to send it to streaming. Originally set for July seventeenth, the film was pushed to July thirty-first, then to August twelfth, and finally opened on August twenty-sixth, twenty twenty, in seventy countries. It reached the United States on September third, with theaters in New York and Los Angeles still closed. It grossed about three hundred sixty-five million dollars worldwide, only some fifty-eight million dollars of it in the domestic market. Given the circumstances, it was not a flop, but it was not the rescue exhibitors had hoped for either. The studios pushed most of the big releases left on the twenty twenty calendar into the following year.

The year's tally was brutal. According to the Motion Picture Association, the global box office fell seventy-two percent in twenty twenty, closing at twelve billion dollars, compared with more than forty billion dollars the year before. In the United States and Canada, the drop was eighty percent. China, which reopened its theaters in July, grossed three billion dollars and, for the first time, overtook North America as the world's largest film market. In October, when Bond was delayed again, the chief executive of the British chain Cineworld, owner of Regal, called the decision "the final straw" and indefinitely closed all of its theaters in the U.S., the United Kingdom and Ireland, affecting forty-five thousand workers. In April twenty twenty-one, the ArcLight and Pacific chains announced they would not reopen any of their locations, not even the historic Cinerama Dome in Hollywood. Cineworld would file for bankruptcy protection in September twenty twenty-two, with debts of more than four point eight billion dollars.

## [05] Trolls World Tour  (880 car., ~0.9 min)

The first battle of the window wars was fought by colorful dolls. With theaters closed, Universal released Trolls World Tour, from twenty twenty, on April tenth, twenty twenty, straight to digital rental in the United States, at nineteen point ninety-nine dollars, and the film brought in close to one hundred million dollars in three weeks. Buoyed by the result, NBCUniversal chief Jeff Shell told the Wall Street Journal that he planned to release films in both formats even after theaters reopened. The response came on April twenty-eighth: AMC, the world's largest theater chain, announced it would no longer show any Universal films. The boycott lasted three months. At the end of July, the two companies made peace with a landmark deal: theatrical exclusivity, which had been about ninety days, dropped to seventeen, and the chain began receiving a share of digital revenue.

## [06] Learn more: The window wars  (1099 car., ~1.1 min)

Learn more: The window wars.

The theatrical window is the period during which a film plays only in theaters before reaching audiences at home, whether on DVD, digital rental or streaming. It's what guarantees theaters' revenue: if a film comes to TV early, fewer people buy tickets. In twenty nineteen, the major chains demanded about ninety days of exclusivity. The pandemic brought down that wall in less than a year. In twenty twenty, the deal between Universal and AMC created the seventeen-day window, or three weekends. Disney tested paid releases on Disney+, and Warner put an entire year of films on streaming the same day they opened in theaters. When exclusivity returned, it was shorter: in March twenty twenty-one, Warner struck a deal with Cineworld for a forty-five-day window starting in twenty twenty-two, and that period became an industry benchmark. In twenty twenty-six, exhibitors were still negotiating every week: Universal guaranteed a minimum of five weekends in theaters, seven starting in twenty twenty-seven, and Paramount promised a forty-five-day window for its releases.

## [07] Mulan  (833 car., ~0.8 min)

Disney chose a different path. Mulan, from twenty twenty, the two hundred million dollars remake directed by Niki Caro, was scheduled for March twenty twenty and was postponed several times, until the company gave up on U.S. theaters. On September fourth, the film premiered on Disney+ through Premier Access: on top of the subscription, viewers paid twenty-nine point ninety-nine dollars to watch it. In countries without the service, such as China, it went to theaters, but grossed only about seventy million dollars. In October, Disney announced that Soul, from twenty twenty, by Pete Docter, would go straight to Disney+, at no extra cost, on Christmas Day twenty twenty. The film won the Oscar for Best Animated Feature and would only reach U.S. theaters in January twenty twenty-four, three and a half years later than planned.

## [08] Wonder Woman 1984  (1771 car., ~1.8 min)

The hardest blow came on December third, twenty twenty. Warner announced that all seventeen of its twenty twenty-one films would open the same day in U.S. theaters and on HBO Max, where they would stay for a month, a plan nicknamed Project Popcorn. The model debuted with Wonder Woman nineteen eighty-four, from twenty twenty, by Patty Jenkins, on Christmas Day. Many filmmakers were not consulted. Nolan accused the studio of turning its directors into bait to lure subscribers and said the plan made no economic sense. Denis Villeneuve wrote in Variety that Warner might have just killed the Dune, from twenty twenty-one, franchise. The film came out in October twenty twenty-one in theaters and on streaming at the same time, and even so, the sequel was green-lit.

The most famous revolt, however, came from a star. On July ninth, twenty twenty-one, Black Widow, from twenty twenty-one, opened simultaneously in theaters and on Disney+ Premier Access. On July twenty-ninth, Scarlett Johansson sued Disney: part of her pay depended on the box office, and the simultaneous release, she argued, breached her contract and drained theaters. The company responded publicly that the actress had already been paid twenty million dollars for the film. The case ended in a settlement on September thirtieth, twenty twenty-one, for an undisclosed amount. It was a watershed: it became clear that streaming was changing not only theaters but also how the people who make films get paid, a debate that would return in force in the twenty twenty-three Hollywood strikes.

## [09] Frances McDormand  (820 car., ~0.8 min)

Even the Oscars changed their look. The twenty twenty-one ceremony was pushed back from February twenty-eighth to April twenty-fifth, and for the first time films released only on streaming were eligible, as long as they had a planned theatrical release. The show left the Dolby Theatre and moved to Union Station, the Los Angeles train station, with a reduced audience. Nomadland, a quiet portrait of Americans who have lost their homes and live on the road, won Best Picture, Director and Actress, the last for Frances McDormand, her third Oscar in the category. Chloé Zhao became the second woman to win as director and the first woman of color. But the ceremony drew few viewers, in the hall and at home: with ten point four million viewers in the U.S., it was the least-watched broadcast since record-keeping began.

## [10] Spider-Man: No Way Home  (1030 car., ~1.0 min)

The comeback only became clear at the end of twenty twenty-one, when Spider-Man: No Way Home, from twenty twenty-one, starring Tom Holland, opened exclusively in theaters, on December sixteenth in Brazil and the following day in the United States, and became the first film since the start of the pandemic to top one billion dollars. The old world, however, did not come back. The ninety-day theatrical window gave way to windows of seventeen to forty-five days, and in early twenty twenty-six projections still put the global box office about thirteen percent below its twenty seventeen, twenty nineteen average. In Brazil, by twenty twenty-five, there were already more screens than in twenty nineteen, but far fewer moviegoers. It is in this shaken film industry that the rest of the decade begins: social media dictating success, horror as the safe bet, filmmakers coming from YouTube, the strikes and artificial intelligence, the strength of films from outside Hollywood, the streaming wars and the return of the event movie.
