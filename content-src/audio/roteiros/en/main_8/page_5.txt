## [00] Opening  (309 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero eight: From twenty twenty to today. Chapter five: Hollywood on Strike and the Fight over AI.

In twenty twenty-three, writers and actors brought Hollywood to a halt for months to demand their share of streaming and protection against artificial intelligence. Soon after, AI took center stage for good.

## [01] Part 1  (821 car., ~0.8 min)

In the early hours of May second, twenty twenty-three, some eleven thousand five hundred members of the Writers Guild of America (WGA), the union representing film and television writers in the United States, put down their pens. The effect was immediate. The late-night talk shows, written the same day they air, switched to reruns that very week, and picket lines soon formed outside the gates of Netflix, Disney, Warner and Universal. On the other side of the table sat the AMPTP, the alliance that bargains on behalf of the studios and streamers. It was the writers' first strike since two thousand seven, two thousand eight, which had lasted one hundred days. This time, the fight would last one hundred forty-eight days and, in July, the actors would join in. Hollywood was about to grind almost entirely to a halt.

## [02] Residual  (874 car., ~0.9 min)

What was really at stake was streaming money. On traditional TV, every rerun paid writers a residual, a kind of royalty. On the streaming platforms, that became a flat fee, the same for a flop as for a global phenomenon, and no one knew how many people watched each show, because the companies didn't release viewership data. Seasons got shorter, and mini rooms appeared: scaled-down writers' rooms, hired for a few weeks and disbanded before filming began. And there was something new. In November twenty twenty-two, OpenAI had launched ChatGPT, and writers feared that a studio would ask a machine for the first draft of a script and then pay a professional merely to "polish" it, for a smaller fee and with no writing credit. The WGA demanded that AI be barred from writing or rewriting scripts. The studios offered only annual meetings to discuss advances in technology.

## [03] Learn more: 1960: the last double strike  (1187 car., ~1.2 min)

Learn more: nineteen sixty: the last double strike.

Before twenty twenty-three, writers and actors had walked out together only once. On January sixteenth, nineteen sixty, the Writers Guild went on strike; on March seventh, the Screen Actors Guild followed. The cause echoed the one sixty-three years later: a new technology was showing films, and the artists were getting nothing for it. This time the novelty was television, which endlessly aired features bought from the studios. Leading the actors was the guild's president, Ronald Reagan. The strike halted eight major productions, among them Let's Make Love, from nineteen sixty, starring Marilyn Monroe, and ended on April eighteenth with a deal: residuals for TV airings only for films made from then on, and not for those produced since nineteen forty-eight, as the guild had originally demanded. In return, the studios paid two point sixty-five million dollars, which was used to create the profession's first pension plan. Some actors felt Reagan had given away too much. The writers stayed on strike until June twelfth. Reagan stepped down as guild president that same year and, two decades later, would reach the White House.

## [04] Fran Drescher  (1763 car., ~1.8 min)

On July thirteenth, it was the actors' turn. The contract of SAG-AFTRA, a union of about one hundred sixty thousand members, had expired the day before, and its president, Fran Drescher, forever the star of the series The Nanny, announced the strike in a fiery speech in which she called the studios "a very greedy entity." That same night in London, the cast of Oppenheimer, from twenty twenty-three, walked out of the premiere before the screening, and Christopher Nolan told the audience that the actors had gone "to write their picket signs." At midnight on July fourteenth, California time, the first simultaneous strike by writers and actors since nineteen sixty began. The actors were seeking raises to keep up with inflation, a share of streaming revenue and rules on artificial intelligence.

[CITAÇÃO] You cannot change the business model as much as it has changed and not expect the contract to change too.

said Fran Drescher, speech announcing the actors' strike, twenty twenty-three.

For actors, the AI threat had a face and a body. On the day the strike was announced, the union's chief negotiator, Duncan Crabtree-Ireland, said the studios had proposed scanning background performers, paying them for a single day's work and keeping their digital likeness forever, free to use it without further consent or compensation. The union's account, disputed by the studios, made headlines around the world and became the emblem of the nightmare: crowd scenes populated by copies of people who would never be called back. The fear didn't stop with extras. Voice actors watched software imitate their voices, and stars knew that digital de-aging could already put a thirty-year-old face on an eighty-year-old actor. The underlying question was simple: who owns that face?

## [05] Denis Villeneuve  (966 car., ~1.0 min)

Strike rules barred actors from promoting films from studios tied to the AMPTP: no red carpets, interviews, festivals or social media posts. Premieres went ahead without casts, and the release calendar collapsed. Denis Villeneuve's Dune: Part Two, from twenty twenty-four, was pushed from November twenty twenty-three to March twenty twenty-four so it wouldn't open without Timothée Chalamet and Zendaya on the promotional trail. Luca Guadagnino's Challengers, from twenty twenty-four, gave up its slot as the opening film of the Venice Film Festival and also moved to twenty twenty-four. The Emmy ceremony shifted from September to January. Those who suffered most were the ones not on strike: grips, electricians, costume designers and drivers went months without work. In August, a professor at California State University, Northridge, estimated that the losses to California's economy would top five billion dollars if the shutdown lasted until November. It did.

## [06] Part 5  (927 car., ~0.9 min)

The writers went back first. After a marathon of negotiations attended by the studio chiefs themselves, the WGA reached a tentative agreement on September twenty-fourth and ended the strike on the twenty-seventh. The contract was ratified with ninety-nine percent of the vote. Beyond the AI rules, writers won minimum staffing levels for writers' rooms, a bonus paid when a show is watched by twenty percent of a platform's U.S. subscribers within ninety days and, for the first time, access to the total hours viewed for each original production. The actors took longer. The SAG-AFTRA deal came on November eighth, and the strike ended on the ninth, after one hundred eighteen days. Valued by the union at more than one billion dollars, it brought a seven percent pay increase, an eleven percent raise for background actors and a streaming bonus, and it was approved on December fifth by seventy-eight percent of those voting.

## [07] Learn more: What the deals say about AI  (1073 car., ~1.1 min)

Learn more: What the deals say about AI.

The twenty twenty-three contracts were the first in film and television to treat artificial intelligence as a labor issue. Under the writers' contract, AI cannot write or rewrite literary material, and machine-generated text does not count as "source material," meaning it cannot be used to strip credit or rights from the writers who were hired. A writer may use the tool if they choose and if the studio consents, but cannot be required to, and the studio must disclose when any material it hands over was produced with AI. The guild also reserved the right to challenge the use of scripts to train these programs. Under the actors' contract, creating and using a digital replica, whether scanned on set or assembled from existing footage, requires informed consent and payment, including in the case of deceased performers. The studios must also notify the union when they want to use a "synthetic performer," a character created from scratch by computer. In twenty twenty-six, the new actors' contract expanded these protections.

## [08] Indiana Jones and the Dial of Destiny  (1002 car., ~1.0 min)

While Hollywood fought over the rules, the technology was already on screen. In Indiana Jones and the Dial of Destiny, from twenty twenty-three, directed by James Mangold and released at the end of June twenty twenty-three, the prologue set in nineteen forty-four shows Harrison Ford decades younger. According to the actor himself, an artificial intelligence program combed through the Lucasfilm archive, including footage that never made it into the films, to find the right lighting and expressions while he performed with markers on his face. Ford called the result "fantastic" and, in another interview, "a little spooky." Digital de-aging was nothing new: Martin Scorsese had already done the same with Robert De Niro and Al Pacino in The Irishman, from twenty nineteen. The difference was speed. In Here, from twenty twenty-four, Robert Zemeckis used the Metaphysic Live tool to de-age and age Tom Hanks and Robin Wright in real time, right on set, from the characters' teenage years to old age.

## [09] The Brutalist  (1101 car., ~1.1 min)

The loudest controversy erupted in January twenty twenty-five, days before the Oscar nominations were announced. The Brutalist, from twenty twenty-four, editor Dávid Jancsó revealed in an interview that Respeecher, software from a Ukrainian company, had been used to refine the pronunciation of certain vowels in Adrien Brody and Felicity Jones's Hungarian dialogue. Director Brady Corbet responded that the performances were "completely" the actors' own, that no English dialogue had been altered and that the film's architectural drawings had been made by hand. According to Respeecher, Emilia Pérez, from twenty twenty-four, had also used the tool. In twenty twenty-four, even the small horror film Late Night with the Devil, from twenty twenty-four, had faced calls for a boycott over its use of AI in three interstitial images. Brody won the Oscar for Best Actor, and in April twenty twenty-five the Academy answered with a brief rule: AI tools "neither help nor harm" a film's chances of a nomination, but voters should weigh how much a human being was "at the heart of the creative authorship."

## [10] Tyler Perry  (912 car., ~0.9 min)

Another, far more radical front was text-to-video generation. In February twenty twenty-four, OpenAI unveiled Sora, which could create realistic scenes up to a minute long from a single sentence. Days later, Tyler Perry said he had put an eight hundred million dollars expansion of his Atlanta studios on hold because of what he had seen. In September twenty twenty-four, Lionsgate, home of John Wick and The Hunger Games, struck a deal with Runway to train a model on its catalog. In twenty twenty-five, Netflix revealed that a building collapse in Buenos Aires in the Argentine series The Eternaut had been made with generative AI, ten times faster than with traditional effects. And YouTube filled up with fake trailers for movies that didn't exist: two of the genre's biggest channels, with more than two million subscribers combined, were demonetized and eventually removed at the end of twenty twenty-five.

## [11] Tilly Norwood  (2185 car., ~2.2 min)

The studios played both sides. In June twenty twenty-five, Disney and Universal sued Midjourney, an image generator that, they said, was a "bottomless pit of plagiarism," capable of producing their characters on demand. Warner Bros. Discovery filed a similar suit in September; the cases were consolidated and, in twenty twenty-six, were still in the discovery phase, with no ruling. That same September, OpenAI launched Sora two, with a TikTok-style app, and the Motion Picture Association criticized the way the company handled copyright. Even so, on December eleventh, Disney announced a one billion dollars investment in OpenAI and licensed more than two hundred characters for the app, drawing criticism from Hollywood unions. The marriage was short-lived: on March twenty-fourth, twenty twenty-six, OpenAI announced it was shutting down Sora, and Disney walked away from the deal without making the investment.

In September twenty twenty-five, the debate acquired a face that doesn't exist. Created by Particle6, the production company of Dutch comedian Eline Van der Velden, the AI "actress" Tilly Norwood had debuted in a comedy sketch in July. At the Zurich Summit, an industry gathering held during the Zurich Film Festival, her creator said that talent agencies were interested in representing her. Emily Blunt called the idea "really, really scary," and SAG-AFTRA responded with a sharply worded statement. None of this stopped Particle6 from announcing, in July twenty twenty-six, the first feature film starring her, Misaligned. But the rules had already changed. In twenty twenty-six, writers and actors renewed their contracts for four years without a strike, and the actors' deal set new limits on "synthetic performers." In May, the Academy ruled that, starting with the twenty twenty-seven Oscars, only performances "demonstrably performed by humans with their consent" and screenplays written by people would be eligible.

## [12] Mission: Impossible - Dead Reckoning Part One  (1078 car., ~1.1 min)

In the cinema of the twenty twenties, artificial intelligence is at once a tool, a threat and a commodity. Some uses few people dispute, such as cleaning up audio, erasing a safety cable or de-aging a face with its owner's permission; others, like touching up the pronunciation of a line the actor recorded, still divide opinion. And then there is what frightens people: models trained on films, voices and faces without license or payment, and the promise of cheaper production by doing away with the very people who make it. It is no coincidence that the films of twenty twenty-three were already talking about it. The villain of Mission: Impossible - Dead Reckoning Part One, from twenty twenty-three, is an AI called the Entity, and The Creator, from twenty twenty-three, directed by Gareth Edwards, imagines a war between humans and machines. The strikes did not stop the technology, but they established three principles that still frame the fight: consent, compensation and credit. The question is no longer whether AI will make it onto the set. It is who sets the terms.
