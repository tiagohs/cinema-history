The decade in which spy films gained great examples such as the double-oh-seven series.

## [01] Alfred Hitchcock  (1045 car., ~1.0 min)

Spy films can be divided into two groups. The first is made up of thrillers whose narratives are built around manhunts and chases. Examples of this type are the Bulldog Drummond films and the adventures of Alfred Hitchcock (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty), Foreign Correspondent, from nineteen forty, and Saboteur, from nineteen forty-two.

The second group consists of existential thrillers adapted from authors such as Eric Ambler and Graham Greene, like Journey into Fear, from nineteen forty-two, and Ministry of Fear, from nineteen forty-three. These feature ordinary protagonists rather than professional secret agents and are characterized by a style somewhere between noir and expressionism.

The spy film reached its peak during the Cold War, when the genre became a vehicle for the geopolitical and ideological conflict between the free, democratic West and the totalitarian communist bloc. The first films of this period, such as I Was a Communist for the FBI, from nineteen fifty-one, are crude products of McCarthyist paranoia.

## [02] North by Northwest  (1116 car., ~1.1 min)

When the "Red Scare" faded, filmmakers were able to take a more critical look at the espionage game. North by Northwest, from nineteen fifty-nine, by Alfred Hitchcock (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty), addresses the moral ambiguity of the Cold War, and the thriller The Manchurian Candidate, from nineteen sixty-two, by John Frankenheimer (nineteen thirty to two thousand two), explores the anxieties stirred by brainwashing, a typical Cold War theme, with a plot that sinisterly anticipates the assassination of President Kennedy in nineteen sixty-three.

The spy films of the nineteen sixties respond to events such as the construction of the Berlin Wall, from nineteen sixty-one, and the Cuban Missile Crisis, from nineteen sixty-two. The thriller is represented above all by the James Bond films, based on novels by Ian Fleming, beginning with Dr. No, from nineteen sixty-two, followed by From Russia with Love, from nineteen sixty-three.

## [03] Goldfinger  (813 car., ~0.8 min)

The double-oh-seven films are fundamentally different from the books in that the filmmakers replaced Fleming's Soviet villains with a supranational crime syndicate, freeing the screen Bond from the ideological context of the Cold War. The James Bond films combine visual spectacle (exotic locations, action sequences) with scenarios of consumerism and sexual permissiveness.

Box-office hits, the formula and the series have remained in almost continuous production to this day. Goldfinger, from nineteen sixty-four, and Thunderball, from nineteen sixty-five, mark the peak of "Bondmania." Although the later films in the series are mere repetitions of the same formula, James Bond remains the archetype of the secret agent of action, a model for other series such as Mission: Impossible and the Jason Bourne films.

## [04] Martin Ritt  (1053 car., ~1.1 min)

A countercycle of "anti-Bond" films in the mid-nineteen sixties brought a more ambiguous and cynical perspective. Martin Ritt (nineteen fourteen to nineteen ninety) directed The Spy Who Came In from the Cold, from nineteen sixty-five, a faithful adaptation of the work of John Le Carré which, with its shabby antihero and austere black-and-white photography of bleak wintry locations, is the antithesis of the double-oh-seven films.

Their atmosphere of suspicion and moral ambiguity is closer to the existential anguish of film noir than to the fantastical world of James Bond. Michael Caine (born in nineteen thirty-three) plays the suburban spy Harry Palmer in three films based on novels by Len Deighton: The Ipcress File, from nineteen sixty-five, Funeral in Berlin, from nineteen sixty-six, and Billion Dollar Brain, from nineteen sixty-seven. With their informants and double agents, these films reflect the reality of espionage as revealed in cases such as the Cambridge spy ring, whose members included Guy Burgess, Donald Maclean and Kim Philby.
