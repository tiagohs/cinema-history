Part zero four: From nineteen sixty to nineteen sixty-nine. Chapter six: Science Fiction on Film.

After a considerable golden age, science fiction films came of age in the nineteen sixties.

## [01] The Thing from Another World  (1748 car., ~1.8 min)

The science fiction films of the nineteen sixties belong to a very broad genre. Although the nineteen fifties are considered this film genre's "golden age" - marked by the somewhat noir Cold War paranoia of films such as The Thing from Another World, from nineteen fifty-one, -, it was in the nineteen sixties that science fiction came of age.

Space invasions and monster attacks in the nineteen fifties style continued to be made, but science fiction also took other forms, as varied as political speculation - The Manchurian Candidate, from nineteen sixty-two, -, the horror of Seconds, from nineteen sixty-six, and the iconic space opera - Barbarella, from nineteen sixty-eight.

Films that in the nineteen fifties would have been made in black and white now gained color. In King Kong versus Godzilla, from nineteen sixty-three, titans from remote eras return in attractive colors and with a more benevolent trajectory: the fearsome Godzilla appears as a friendly defender of children. The Time Machine, from nineteen sixty, is a "family" adventure in which director George Pal (nineteen oh-eight to nineteen eighty) softens the harsh pessimism of H. G. Wells's novel, published in eighteen ninety-five.

The science fiction of the nineteen fifties had dealt with fear and the unknown; that of the nineteen sixties seized the opportunity created by the widespread fascination with the space age born of the rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union. Lunar exploration sparked interest in space adventures and encouraged advances in special effects (specifically miniature models and matte painting), which made it possible to convincingly realize the technologized future of two thousand one: A Space Odyssey, from nineteen sixty-eight.

## [02] Learn more: Planet of the Apes  (2081 car., ~2.1 min)

Learn more: Planet of the Apes.

A classic among science fiction adventures that remains as powerful today as when it was first released, Planet of the Apes was a project that had the potential to go terribly wrong. Tim Burton's two thousand one remake is an example of everything that could go wrong. Even with the one hundred million dollars budget still two decades away, making a film out of Pierre Boulle's book, La Planète des singes remained a risky premise. Having so many actors dressed in ape suits could well have been an invitation for audiences to laugh rather than to be scared.

Before the film went into production, makeup artist John Chambers eased the studio bosses' fears by shooting a test with Charlton Heston (as the human Taylor) and Edward G. Robinson (as the ape Dr. Zaius). Having shown that the ape makeup was convincing, Chambers was given fifty thousand dollars to develop the simian effects for the film. Robinson, fearing that hours in the makeup chair would threaten his already fragile health, left the cast and was replaced by Maurice Evans.

It was money well spent. The apes that Taylor and his two fellow astronauts encounter when their spaceship crashes on a desolate planet are genuinely frightening, especially when we and Taylor realize that they are in charge and that humans are mute animals in this strange place. The casting is perfect. Heston, who spends most of the film wearing little more than a leather loincloth, is manly and tough. Roddy McDowall and Kim Hunter, as the "friendly" apes Cornelius and Zira, manage to break free of the confines of their impressive makeup and give moving performances. Punctuated by landmark scenes such as Taylor's first sight of the new planet, his capture and, of course, the unforgettable ending, Rod Serling and Michael Wilson's screenplay is intelligent and packed with sensational dialogue. ("Take your stinking paws off me, you damned dirty ape!"). This classic spawned three sequels, a TV series, the aforementioned disappointing remake and even a hilarious parody in an episode of The Simpsons.

## [03] 2001: A Space Odyssey  (2328 car., ~2.3 min)

In Planet of the Apes, from nineteen sixty-eight, released the same year, the crew of a spaceship crash-lands on a planet at a time when apes have evolved to become the dominant species, enslaving humans. The famous image of the shattered Statue of Liberty at the end of the film perfectly symbolizes the fear of nuclear war and its radioactive effects, heightened by the Cuban Missile Crisis.

Cheap, populist cinema in Britain, the United States, and Japan, science fiction began to resonate in other cultures. Serious, if occasionally satirical, essays in futurism were made by art-house directors such as François [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] (nineteen thirty-two to nineteen eighty-four) in Fahrenheit four hundred fifty-one, from nineteen sixty-six, and Jean-Luc [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] (born in nineteen thirty) in Alphaville, from nineteen sixty-five. In the latter, the American Eddie Constantine (nineteen seventeen to nineteen ninety-three) plays the secret agent Lemmy Caution, who would not look out of place in a nineteen forties noir thriller. Although set in a dystopian future, Alphaville was shot on real Parisian locations.

Strangely, with a few exceptions including Ray Bradbury (nineteen twenty to twenty twelve) in Fahrenheit four hundred fifty-one and John Wyndham (nineteen oh-three to nineteen sixty-nine) in The Day of the Triffids, from nineteen sixty-three, there was a certain reluctance in the nineteen sixties to adapt works by living authors: Philip K. Dick would only appear frequently in cinema from Blade Runner, from nineteen eighty-two, onward.

Although many science fiction films were very successful, such as those in the Planet of the Apes series, the masterpiece of the decade was undoubtedly two thousand one: A Space Odyssey, directed by Stanley Kubrick (nineteen twenty-eight to nineteen ninety-nine). Boosted by special effects that remain unmatched, it became the space craze of its time. Even while unsure of its meaning, audiences were mesmerized by its transcendental vision. Acclaimed and criticized, two thousand one dared to be an art film as much as a Buck Rogers adventure, and its influence on the science fiction films that followed is immeasurable.

## [04] Learn more: Kubrick's Ultimate Daring in Cinema (1)  (2363 car., ~2.4 min)

Learn more: Kubrick's Ultimate Daring in Cinema.

An artifact of evident extraterrestrial origin triggers and monitors crucial stages in Man's journey, from ape to star explorer. At the dawn of the age of Man, a mysterious monolith acts as the catalyst for an evolutionary leap among primates, from gatherers and scavengers to armed hunters and killers. Millennia later, a monolith discovered by geologists on duty on the Moon emits alarming shortwave signals in the direction of Jupiter. An expedition to investigate (commanded impassively by Keir Dullea and Gary Lockwood while the specialists sleep in suspended animation) is sabotaged by the spaceship Discovery's computer, HAL (voiced by Douglas Rain), who is psychologically disturbed. Hugely influential yet still unique, coldly detached, obsessive, pretentious, confusing and forever fascinating, two thousand one is all of this and more.

It certainly strayed from Stanley Kubrick's stated intention of making a "good, typical science fiction film" out of Arthur C. Clarke's intriguing short story "The Sentinel" (Clarke co-wrote the screenplay). The film defies genre conventions and differs from any science fiction work made before it. Visually, two thousand one is undeniably stunning. Groundbreaking special effects, which deservedly won an Oscar, offer an admirable blend of imagination and science. Meticulous mime work and the best makeup available at the time create, in the first of the film's four acts, the most perfect portrayal of apes by human beings ever seen up to that point (and still very effective, although surpassed by John Chambers' creation in Planet of the Apes, nineteen sixty-eight). The film is full of unforgettable images: the unexpected and stunning cut from a bone wielded by an ape-man and tossed into the air to a satellite; the alignment of the Sun and the Moon directly on the edge of the monolith; the orbital waltz at the space station and the landing of a spacecraft; the circular habitat of the Discovery crew.

The sound is equally rich, with experimental choral music, classical themes (Richard Strauss's "Also sprach Zarathustra" and Johann Strauss's waltz "The Blue Danube") that are forever associated with the film, and morsels of minimalist dialogue ("Open the pod bay doors, HAL") that have become the source of a wide range of tributes and cultural references.

two thousand one can be considered a mysterious adventure, a sermon, a vision, but even taken simply as a striking and unsurpassed spectacle, it demands to be seen on a big screen to be appreciated in its full scope. Its weaknesses (excessive abstraction and a deficient narrative built on poorly articulated, unresolved speculation about the origins and destiny of human life) are more than compensated for by the gripping relationship between man and machine, its visual austerity and serenity and, above all, its rhapsodic exaltation of the sky, the Earth and the infinite.
