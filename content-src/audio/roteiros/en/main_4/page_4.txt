## [00] Opening  (270 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero four: From nineteen sixty to nineteen sixty-nine. Chapter four: Sex and Taboos on Film.

The effervescent nineteen sixties brought a new attitude toward sex, youth, drugs and personal freedom, influencing the arts as a whole and pushing censorship to its limits.

## [01] Tony Richardson  (1312 car., ~1.3 min)

At the start of the decade, British New Wave director Tony Richardson (nineteen twenty-eight to nineteen ninety-one) swapped social realism for farcical comedy in Tom Jones, from nineteen sixty-three, an adaptation of Henry Fielding's eighteenth-century novel. Provocative and daring, it follows the sexual adventures of young Tom, played by an Albert Finney (nineteen thirty-six to twenty nineteen) completely at ease in the role.

To twenty-first-century audiences, Tom Jones is a prudish account of Tom's amorous encounters, but at the time the film was considered licentious. When Jones and Mrs. Waters dine together, Richardson allusively depicts the couple's lustful desires as they suck oysters, tear into chicken legs, and fill their mouths with juicy pears.

Cinema's reexamination of social mores was not limited to Western society. In Japan, directors such as Shohei Imamura (nineteen twenty-six to two thousand six) showed women's new role and uninhibited sexuality in films such as Nippon konchûki (The Insect Woman, nineteen sixty-three), which tells the story of a young peasant woman turned geisha, raped by her stepfather and disgraced by her "carnal desire". The film traces Japan's history before and after the wars of the twentieth century, as tradition gives way to a liberal industrial society.

## [02] Blow-Up  (1237 car., ~1.2 min)

Over the course of the decade, cinema became more open to forbidden subjects and more inclined to reflect what was happening in an increasingly liberal society. The success of Blow-Up, from nineteen sixty-six, directed by Michelangelo Antonioni (nineteen twelve to two thousand seven), was due in part to its explicit sex scenes and its treatment of drug abuse in contemporary fashion-world London.

Belle de Jour, from nineteen sixty-seven, shocked audiences with the story of a Parisian housewife who works as a prostitute during the day to fulfill her sadomasochistic fantasies. Vilgot Sjöman (nineteen twenty-four to two thousand six) caused outrage with his frank treatment of sex and violence in Jag är nyfiken - en film i gult (I Am Curious (Yellow), nineteen sixty-seven), the story of a promiscuous young woman.

Mike Nichols (born in nineteen thirty-one) perfectly captured the ebb and flow of American society on the brink of the sexual revolution in The Graduate, from nineteen sixty-seven, a study of sex across generations. If most American cinema had been conventional up to that point, Nichols's film bridges the old world and the new, pointing toward the more experimental cinema that would arrive in the nineteen seventies.

## [03] Learn more: Soundtracks and Cinema  (981 car., ~1.0 min)

Learn more: Soundtracks and Cinema.

After asking Paul Simon to produce material for The Graduate, Mike Nichols decided to use songs from two earlier Simon and Garfunkel albums, along with part of the then-unfinished "Mrs. Robinson", in addition to music by Dave Grusin. The film, and cinema in general, profited from the use of popular material. The soundtrack was essential to the film's success, and the album reached the top of the charts before being replaced by Bookends, the one containing the full version of "Mrs. Robinson" and other songs written but not used in the film. The way the music was used is also striking. "The Sounds (later 'Sound') of Silence" plays at the beginning, at the end, and during the sequence of Benjamin at home intercut with his affair with Mrs. Robinson. The lyrics, from "Hello darkness, my old friend", suggest Benjamin's innermost thoughts while never connecting directly to the narrative, and the bleak lines are offset by the upbeat vocals.

## [04] Barbarella  (1201 car., ~1.2 min)

As the nineteen sixties drew to a close and censorship laws were relaxed, films began to shock for shock's sake. In the kitsch science fiction film Barbarella, from nineteen sixty-eight, Jane Fonda (born in nineteen thirty-seven) plays a twentieth-century astronaut who travels to the planet Tau Ceti in search of the missing scientist Durand-Durand.

With Barbarella's constant and inexplicable changes of see-through outfits, its hints of lesbianism in the character's encounters with the lascivious Great Tyrant (Anita Pallenberg), and its scenes with sex devices, this extravagant cult film has an unquestionably sadomasochistic bent. At the end of the decade, Dennis Hopper (nineteen thirty-six to twenty ten) shared the screenplay and the lead role of the road movie Easy Rider, from nineteen sixty-nine, with Peter Fonda (nineteen forty to twenty nineteen).

They play hippies on a motorcycle trip from Los Angeles to New Orleans. Their drug-fueled flings with naked women start out innocent and end up frightening, but are never explicit. Hopper used the new freedom available to filmmakers to pose disturbing questions to the nation's youth. The revolution was no longer sexual, but one of ideas.
