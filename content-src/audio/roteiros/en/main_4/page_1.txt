## [01] British New Wave  (2113 car., ~2.1 min)

Flashback to nineteen fifty-six. A series of short, low-budget British documentaries reaches London screens. As one London newspaper put it, "all the bearded boys in duffel coats and the girls in jeans with cropped hair in the city seem to converge" on the South Bank to watch "Free Cinema" films.

The documentaries frankly tackled subjects such as amusement parks, dance halls, and youth clubs. In the context of British cinema at the time, they were pioneering works. For director and critic Lindsay Anderson (nineteen twenty-three to nineteen ninety-four), the nineteen fifties were, for British cinema, a period of complacency and "lukewarm humanism". It was the era of John Mills war films and stoic dramas in which "chaps" like Jack Hawkins appeared smoking pipes.

The British New Wave was driven by a group of critics turned filmmakers who, in the late nineteen forties, were closely linked to the film magazine Sequence, at the University of Oxford, and later went on to write for Sight and Sound. Its leading figures were Lindsay Anderson, Karel Reisz (nineteen twenty-six to two thousand two), Tony Richardson (nineteen twenty-eight to nineteen ninety-one), and Gavin Lambert (nineteen twenty-four to two thousand five). In their first feature films, the group resolutely turned its focus to the industrial north of Britain.

But the film most widely accepted as the starting point of the British New Wave was made by Jack Clayton (nineteen twenty-one to nineteen ninety-five), a director not linked to the Sequence group. The subversive northern working-class hero and the unprecedentedly direct treatment of sex and social class in Room at the Top, from nineteen fifty-eight, set the template followed by Reisz in Saturday Night and Sunday Morning, from nineteen sixty, Richardson in The Loneliness of the Long Distance Runner, from nineteen sixty-two, and Anderson in This Sporting Life, from nineteen sixty-three.

## [02] John Schlesinger  (745 car., ~0.7 min)

Like Clayton, John Schlesinger (nineteen twenty-six to two thousand three) also kept his distance from the core group, but his A Kind of Loving, from nineteen sixty-two, starring Alan Bates (nineteen thirty-four to two thousand three), follows the same pattern. All these films were made, as Richardson later wrote, with "a minimum of equipment, real locations, and a naturally unkempt look".

Most British New Wave films are adaptations of novels and plays, as is the case with Look Back in Anger, from nineteen fifty-nine, and A Taste of Honey, from nineteen sixty-one, - both by Tony Richardson (nineteen twenty-eight to nineteen ninety-one) - based respectively on Look Back in Anger, by John Osborne, and A Taste of Honey, by Shelagh Delaney.

## [03] Rita Tushingham  (1243 car., ~1.2 min)

The second is unusual for having a woman as its protagonist, played by newcomer Rita Tushingham (born in nineteen forty-two). Its directors' Oxford education explains some critics' skepticism about the authenticity of British New Wave films. An article in The Guardian mocked the way these middle-class southern directors looked to the North in search of "the hard lives and thick accents that best suit their ideological leanings".

As expected, the energy of the British New Wave soon dissipated. Before leaving for Hollywood, Tony Richardson made the period extravaganza Tom Jones, from nineteen sixty-three. After Billy Liar, from nineteen sixty-three, Schlesinger turned his attention south with Darling, from nineteen sixty-five. The far-from-prolific Anderson made no other film until if...., from nineteen sixty-eight. But the spirit of the New Wave transformed British cinema forever. Its influence can be seen in the films of Ken Loach (born in nineteen thirty-six), Mike Leigh (born in nineteen forty-three), and Terence Davies (born in nineteen forty-five) and, more recently, in the work of Andrea Arnold (born in nineteen sixty-one), Lynne Ramsay (born in nineteen sixty-nine), and Pawel Pawlikowski (born in nineteen fifty-seven).
