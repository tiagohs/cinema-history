## [00] Opening  (194 car., ~0.2 min)

Part zero four: From nineteen sixty to nineteen sixty-nine. Chapter two: Latin American Cinema.

The nineteen sixties were a fervent decade for Latin American cinema, led by Cinema Novo in Brazil.

## [01] Cinema Novo  (1030 car., ~1.0 min)

Latin America is a vast and diverse region. Geographically, it covers all the territory stretching from the southern tip of South America to the California border and the Caribbean islands. Politically, in the nineteen sixties it was home to both Brazil's right-wing military government and Cuban communism. In cinema, some of its countries built major industries before and during the nineteen sixties, while others had minimal film production.

The most important national cinema in South America was probably Brazil's. Significant works appeared in Brazilian cinema in the nineteen thirties, but its great leap came in the early nineteen sixties with the flourishing Cinema Novo, a movement whose motto was "an idea in the head and a camera in the hand".

The most prominent figure of Cinema Novo was writer-director Glauber Rocha (nineteen thirty-nine to nineteen eighty-one), whose best-known film, Antonio das Mortes, from nineteen sixty-nine, is the story of a gunman hired to kill bandits who ends up becoming a revolutionary.

Upon attending one of the first screenings of Black God, White Devil, a Brazilian critic reportedly exclaimed: "My God, [[pron: Eisenstein|Eye-zen-stine]] has been reborn... and he's Brazilian!" Born in Bahia, a Brazilian state known as the cradle of Afro-Brazilian culture, Glauber grew up fascinated by mysticism and American Westerns. In his early twenties, he moved to Rio de Janeiro, where he fell in with a group of young cinephiles who would go on to found the influential Cinema Novo movement.

Influenced by radical politics like so many of his generation, Glauber sought to explore the links between historical change and violence in Black God, White Devil. Here he focuses on examples of popular uprisings in the Brazilian backlands.

Shot in an arid black and white that makes the screen practically shimmer with heat, Black God, White Devil alternates frenetic camera movements with dynamically edited sequences, layering Villa-Lobos's music over wind, screams, and gunshots. The final effect is a sense that the film and the country it portrays are about to explode. Shortly before the film was completed and released commercially, the Brazilian military staged a coup, ushering in a dictatorship that lasted twenty years.

## [03] 1964 military coup  (1479 car., ~1.5 min)

Influenced by Italian Neorealism and the French New Wave, Glauber made his film debut in nineteen sixty-two with The Turning Wind, from nineteen sixty-two, and then directed Black God, White Devil, from nineteen sixty-four, an extraordinary visual study of life in the backlands that combines local legend with music by J. S. Bach and Heitor Villa-Lobos to deliver a harsh critique of survival conditions in the drought-stricken, impoverished Northeast. The nineteen sixty-four military coup imposed growing difficulties on the Brazilian film industry, to which Glauber responded with the allegorical Entranced Earth, from nineteen sixty-seven.

In contrast to the stylization of Glauber Rocha's films, other Cinema Novo figures opted for a more realistic style. Former documentary director Nelson Pereira dos Santos (born in nineteen twenty-eight) made Barren Lives, from nineteen sixty-three, a harsh account of rural poverty in the desolate landscape of the Northeast. Ruy Guerra (born in nineteen thirty-one) made The Guns, from nineteen sixty-four, a vigorous study of a group of soldiers tasked with protecting a warehouse from starving peasants.

Toward the end of the decade, after the successive coups of nineteen sixty-four and nineteen sixty-eight, Joaquim Pedro de Andrade (nineteen thirty-two to nineteen eighty-eight) made Macunaima, from nineteen sixty-nine, a wild comedy on racial, sexual, and political themes adapted from Mário de Andrade's novel of the same name.

## [04] Palme d'Or  (977 car., ~1.0 min)

The nineteen sixties also brought Brazilian cinema its most important international award. The Given Word, from nineteen sixty-two, by Anselmo Duarte (nineteen twenty to two thousand nine) - the story of a small landowner who vows to carry a cross to a distant church if his sick donkey recovers - won the Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival, and remains to this day the only Brazilian winner of the prestigious prize.

Duarte explored similar religious themes with The Obsessed of Catulé, from nineteen sixty-four, a film about a man who believes he is Jesus Christ. The socially critical aspects of these works led to Anselmo Duarte being arrested as a suspected communist. He was also criticized by the Cinema Novo filmmakers.

A movement parallel to Brazil's Cinema Novo developed in Mexico, where a group of young critics created the magazine Nuevo Cine and leveled harsh criticism at Mexican commercial cinema and its reliance on safe genres such as musicals and comedies.

In his nineteen sixty-five text The Aesthetics of Hunger, Glauber Rocha wrote: "We know - we who made these ugly, sad films, these screaming, desperate films in which reason did not always prevail - that hunger will not be cured by bureaucratic planning and that Technicolor patches do not hide, but rather aggravate, its tumors. Thus, only a culture of hunger, undermining its own structures, can surpass itself qualitatively: and the noblest cultural manifestation of hunger is violence."

As the movement's main aesthetic features, lack of resources and creative freedom allowed Cinema Novo filmmakers to challenge, provoke, and surprise audiences. However, this freedom, this abandonment of technical preciousness, made each film an expression of its director's personal vision. For this reason, the movement is not necessarily homogeneous in its aesthetics, since the form and content of the films varied greatly across its different phases.

Cinema Novo was shaped in the image of other movements known for their subversiveness, such as Italian Neorealism and the French New Wave. Like their predecessors, the Brazilians wanted to shoot on small budgets, often on real locations and with nonprofessional actors, addressing themes drawn from the reality of the oppressed classes. A passion for cinema and the desire to use it as a tool for change was what drove them all.

Although some Cinema Novo directors saw the French as bourgeois or elitist, they agreed that the "auteur theory" of the French New Wave was an interesting concept that allowed filmmakers to imprint their personal views on their works, including political opinions and aesthetic preferences.

For this reason, the movement has no aesthetic unity. The "aesthetics of hunger" itself, developed by Glauber Rocha, dealt more with conceptual issues than with visual or technical ones. The goal was to expose social inequality in Third World countries and to speak of hunger as a symptom of a sick society. As the filmmaker put it: "[...] our greatest misery is that this hunger, though felt, is not understood."

## [06] J. M. García Ascot  (1244 car., ~1.2 min)

J. M. García Ascot (nineteen twenty-seven to nineteen eighty-six), a member of the Nuevo Cine circle, suggested alternative paths for Mexican cinema in En el balcón vacío (On the Empty Balcony, nineteen sixty-one), based on his wife's childhood memories of the Spanish Civil War and her exile in Mexico, described by John King as "a Proustian evocation of lost time".

In nineteen sixty-five and nineteen sixty-six, two important film adaptations of stories by Colombian writer Gabriel García Márquez were made: There Are No Thieves in This Village, from nineteen sixty-five, by Alberto Isaac (nineteen twenty-five to nineteen eighty-eight), and Tiempo de morir (Time to Die, nineteen sixty-six), by Arturo Ripstein (born in nineteen forty-three).

The most illustrious film artist working in Mexico, however, was the Spanish émigré Luis [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]] (nineteen hundred to nineteen eighty-three), who had left his home country at the time of the Civil War. [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]] built a remarkable career in the Mexican industry throughout the nineteen fifties with films such as Los olvidados (The Young and the Damned, nineteen fifty), Él (This Strange Passion, nineteen fifty-three), and Nazarín, from nineteen fifty-eight, blends of social commentary and surrealism.

## [07] El ángel exterminador  (1027 car., ~1.0 min)

Among [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]]'s last Mexican films is El ángel exterminador (The Exterminating Angel, nineteen sixty-two), a chilling parable about a group of people trapped at a dinner party that - inexplicably - no one can leave. This account of the discreet charm of the bourgeoisie set the tone for the last fifteen years of [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]]'s career, but, with the exception of the satirical Simón del desierto (Simon of the Desert, nineteen sixty-five), all his subsequent films would be made in Europe.

Apart from Brazil's and Mexico's, the most notable film industry in Latin America was Argentina's. Leopoldo Torre Nilsson (nineteen twenty-four to nineteen seventy-eight) had already won international acclaim in the late nineteen fifties for his melodramatic accounts of the decadent upper class, most notably La casa del ángel (The House of the Angel, nineteen fifty-seven), which was screened and awarded at Cannes. The film is based on a novel by Beatriz Guido, co-writer of the screenplay, who would become his wife and regular screenwriter.

## [09] Third Cinema  (994 car., ~1.0 min)

However, the most important Argentine film movement of the nineteen sixties was a conscious attempt to move away from foreign models. Fernando Solanas (born in nineteen thirty-six) and Octavio Getino (born in nineteen thirty-five) collaborated on La hora de los hornos: Notas y testimonios sobre el neocolonialismo, la violencia y la liberación (The Hour of the Furnaces, nineteen sixty-eight). A didactic account of Argentine history and politics, this film became the model for "Third Cinema": a cinema style of its own for the Third World, an alternative to the "First Cinema" of Hollywood and the "Second Cinema" of the new waves.

The Chilean film industry found its footing in the late nineteen sixties. Avant-garde filmmaker Raúl Ruiz (born in nineteen forty-one) made his directorial debut with Tres tristes tigres (Three Sad Tigers, nineteen sixty-eight), a model of the intellectual games that characterize his work. Most of his career, however, would be spent abroad, mainly in France.

## [10] El Chacal de Nahueltoro  (1041 car., ~1.0 min)

More realistic in style is El Chacal de Nahueltoro (The Jackal of Nahueltoro, nineteen sixty-nine), by Miguel Littin (born in nineteen forty-two), an uneven but extremely vigorous, documentary-style portrait of a murderer whose experiences underpin the dramatization of the desperate living conditions of Chile's poor. After the Marxist Salvador Allende won the nineteen seventy elections, Littin briefly served as president of the state-owned company Chile Films, but resigned before Allende's overthrow forced exile on both him and Ruiz.

None of the other Spanish-speaking countries of South America - Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Venezuela, Paraguay, and Uruguay - sustained significant film production throughout the nineteen sixties; in some of these countries, only short documentaries were made. However, many of the most notable Latin American films of the decade were produced off the continent, on the Caribbean island of Cuba, where the nineteen fifty-nine revolution had transformed what had until then been a minor industry.

## [11] Mikhail Kalatozov  (2005 car., ~2.0 min)

In nineteen sixty-one, after Cuban leader Fidel Castro nationalized local industries and established ties with the Soviet Union, the United States broke off diplomatic relations, and Castro's regime moved definitively into the Soviet orbit. This new alignment found cinematic expression when Mikhail Kalatozov (nineteen oh-three to nineteen seventy-three), the Georgian filmmaker behind the Soviet classic Letyat zhuravli (The Cranes Are Flying, nineteen fifty-seven), went to the island to make Soy Cuba (I Am Cuba, nineteen sixty-four). A visually stunning work of propaganda, the film traces the country's path from pre-revolutionary oppression to an idealized, celebratory present.

Cuban filmmaker Tomás Gutiérrez Alea (nineteen twenty-eight to nineteen ninety-six) signed one of the first post-revolutionary productions, the neorealist war film Historias de la revolución (Stories of the Revolution, nineteen sixty). His later work contains an evolving dialectical social critique: La muerte de un burócrata (Death of a Bureaucrat, nineteen sixty-six) emerged as a surprising satire of the spasms of socialist bureaucracy, while Memorias del subdesarrollo (Memories of Underdevelopment, nineteen sixty-eight), a melancholy account of post-revolutionary life, displays remarkable skeptical intelligence for a film produced under a totalitarian regime.

That same year, Humberto Solás (nineteen forty-one to two thousand eight) made Lucía, a vigorous narrative of women's experience across three different periods of Cuban history, notable for its dramatic intensity and its immaculate period reconstruction.

The circumstances of Cuba, the only Latin American country to carry out a successful and lasting leftist revolution, were unique, but the tendency to make politically engaged films was not, as the open social criticism of the best films made in Brazil, Argentina, and Mexico shows. Latin America in the nineteen sixties was a region where cinema could not afford the luxury of not being political.
