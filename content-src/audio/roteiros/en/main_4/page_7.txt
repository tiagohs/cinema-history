Part zero four: From nineteen sixty to nineteen sixty-nine. Chapter seven: Modern Horror on Film.

Much more than a series of scares and chills, horror films of the nineteen sixties often used the genre as a vehicle to address broader themes.

## [01] Village of the Damned  (1154 car., ~1.1 min)

Village of the Damned, from nineteen sixty, imagines a scenario in which society is subverted from within by a force that infiltrates children's minds; Night of the Eagle, from nineteen sixty-two, mixes witchcraft with questions of belief and superstition; Vargtimmen (Hour of the Wolf, nineteen sixty-eight), by Bergman (nineteen eighteen to two thousand seven), a film about a relationship with elements of horror, addresses the role of art and the artist.

Even the master of exploitation film, Roger Corman (born in nineteen twenty-six), found time amid the decadence and theatricality of The Masque of the Red Death, from nineteen sixty-four, to discuss the nature of religious faith. The most interesting discussions took place, perhaps, in the films that focused on contemporary society.

There are clear anti-bourgeois diatribes in the grotesque revels of the rich depicted in Corman's Poe cycle, in Roger Vadim's (nineteen twenty-eight to two thousand) segment of Histoires extraordinaires (Spirits of the Dead, nineteen sixty-eight) and, finally, in the coven of witches on Manhattan's Upper West Side in Rosemary's Baby, from nineteen sixty-eight.

## [02] Learn more: Satanic Cinema  (1084 car., ~1.1 min)

Learn more: Satanic Cinema.

The devil made an early debut in cinema. In Le Manoir du diable, from eighteen ninety-six, director Georges [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] (eighteen sixty-one to nineteen thirty-eight) plays Mephistopheles. The best version of the Faust legend is still Faust, from nineteen twenty-six, by F. W. Murnau (eighteen eighty-eight to nineteen thirty-one), but pacts with the devil have appeared in other films such as The Devil and Daniel Webster, from nineteen forty-one, and The Devil's Advocate, from nineteen ninety-seven. Many dramas, such as Wall Street, from nineteen eighty-seven, and The Devil Wears Prada, from two thousand six, boil down to a new guise for the Faust story, adorned with the specific concerns of their era. Devil worship crept into cinema mainly as a threat, as in The Seventh Victim, from nineteen forty-three, whose New York coven prefigures Ira Levin's, as well as in Hammer's boisterous The Devil Rides Out, from nineteen sixty-seven. One of cinema's best devils was Robert De Niro (born in nineteen forty-three) in Angel Heart, from nineteen eighty-seven.

## [03] Witchfinder General  (901 car., ~0.9 min)

It can be read as a nineteen sixties condemnation of the greedy, in the postwar spirit of equality and civil liberties; Witchfinder General, from nineteen sixty-eight, is a forceful reminder of historical atrocities and also a brutal metaphor for Senator McCarthy's anticommunist witch hunt of the nineteen fifties. Low-budget horror cinema came to the fore in films such as Night of the Living Dead, from nineteen sixty-eight, by George A. Romero (born in nineteen forty), which questioned whether the world is good and morally just.

With intense close-ups, tilted cameras and well-developed characters, this new wave marked the arrival of the independent director and alerted audiences to new concerns. The zombies (revived by the effects of radiation) can be seen as references to racism and war. Unlike many other horror films, the danger extends to the whole country, perhaps even the whole world.

## [04] The Haunting  (1196 car., ~1.2 min)

Beyond the supernatural evil of The Haunting, from nineteen sixty-three, and the alien invaders of The Day of the Triffids, from nineteen sixty-two, horror cinema also took on a human face. The danger could be anyone and the terror was real, as in Peeping Tom, from nineteen sixty, and Psycho, from nineteen sixty. With The Birds, from nineteen sixty-three, Alfred Hitchcock (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty) once again subverted what audiences expect of a heroine.

The classic female lead in horror films is the virginal blonde dressed in white who acts as a moral compass: the kindhearted Mina survives Dracula, whereas having sex can have sinister consequences, as happened to Mia Farrow in Rosemary's Baby. Unlike the vast majority of female leads in horror films, Tippi Hedren's (born in nineteen thirty) Melanie in The Birds is no wilting violet, but a confident woman who pursues a man all the way to his front door. With that, female characters came to have a far greater range than their mere terrified stares.

## [05] Onibaba  (1218 car., ~1.2 min)

The horror film was popular not only in the United States but also in Europe and Asia. The supernatural Onibaba, from nineteen sixty-four, and Kwaidan, from nineteen sixty-four, mark the invasion of Japanese horror thirty years before Ringu (Ring, nineteen ninety-eight). Giallo films made the careers of many European directors, such as Dario Argento (born in nineteen forty).

French horror films had little impact despite the chilling Les Yeux sans visage (Eyes Without a Face, nineteen sixty), but French ancestry can be attributed to films influenced by the Grand Guignol movement, such as the stylized horror (more specifically Grande Dame Guignol) of What Ever Happened to Baby Jane?, from nineteen sixty-two, and the lavish output of Hammer. With a core trio formed by director Terence Fisher (nineteen oh-four to nineteen eighty) and actors Christopher Lee and Peter Cushing, Hammer became Britain's leading studio, regularly producing films such as Dracula: Prince of Darkness, from nineteen sixty-six, and Frankenstein Must Be Destroyed, from nineteen sixty-nine, and, at its peak, The Devil Rides Out, from nineteen sixty-eight, with a screenplay by Richard Matheson (nineteen twenty-six to twenty thirteen).

## [06] Jason genre  (926 car., ~0.9 min)

In Western cinemas, the nineteen sixties were the era when horror films were respected as art. However, it was also the decade in which the genre began to lose focus. The studios realized they could churn out endless sequels and crude imitations. The horror film became a cheap commodity and the drive-in became a home for exploitation fare. Nevertheless, this type of film gave nineteen sixties filmmaking a reach and scope that flourished until the arrival and saturation of the B-movie market of the Jason genre.

It is practically impossible to imagine a world without zombie films. The subgenre has been so heavily explored - even outside horror - that many people see such stories as oversaturated. On October first, nineteen sixty-eight, that wasn't quite the case, and if it weren't for a bureaucratic error involving Night of the Living Dead, it's quite possible that all of today's popularity would never have existed.

## [07] Night of the Living Dead  (2153 car., ~2.1 min)

George Romero's work deserves full credit on its own: the film is a landmark of low-budget cinema that not only establishes the threat of the undead horde but also delivers tension and action combined with a solid layer of social commentary, featuring a Black hero who defies the stereotypes of the time - and a treacherous ending with timeless impact. The feature also doesn't hold back on violence, something that caught nineteen sixties audiences off guard, especially considering there was no age-rating system to control who watched it in theaters: "The movie had stopped being delightfully scary about halfway through, and had become unexpectedly terrifying," wrote Roger Ebert, critic for the Chicago Sun-Times. "There was a little girl across the aisle from me, maybe nine years old, who was sitting very still in her seat and crying."

But what elevated Night of the Living Dead from a great film into a true cultural phenomenon has to do not only with its content but also with its distribution. As it happens, the film was originally called Night of the Flesh Eaters (or A Noite dos Devoradores de Carne, in a loose Portuguese translation) but, since the name was very similar to that of The Flesh Eaters, from nineteen sixty-four, the distributor Walter Reade Organization asked for the title to be changed to Night of the Living Dead. The sudden change came with a slip: to secure copyright on an audiovisual work at the time, the copyright symbol (©) had to appear in the film's opening. When the title was changed, the production forgot to include the notice of intellectual property. Just a tiny mistake, right?

In fact, the absence of the symbol meant that Night of the Living Dead was released straight into the public domain; in other words, anyone could simply get a copy and screen it publicly as many times as they wanted, even charging admission. While this may have hurt the original distributor's pocket, since it wasn't collecting the box-office takings, the accident allowed the film to be widely shown at smaller events, small theaters, drive-ins and every kind of venue that wanted to screen the much-talked-about indie horror.

## [08] Zombi 2  (923 car., ~0.9 min)

Moreover, the public domain also allows different creators to contribute to the characters and concepts of the work in question. Basically any filmmaker could now use Romero's zombies in their projects - and that is exactly what happened, with a veritable infestation of films about the walking dead. The best example of this perhaps comes from Italy, a prolific low-budget film industry, which launched major filmmakers through imitations and reinterpretations of the American film, such as Zombi two (Zombie Flesh Eaters, nineteen seventy-nine), an unofficial sequel to Night of the Living Dead by horror master Lucio Fulci (nineteen twenty-seven to nineteen ninety-six).

The film remains in the public domain to this day and is available in full on the Internet Archive and even on YouTube. Nor was the work hurt much by what happened, grossing thirty million dollars on a tiny budget of one hundred ten thousand dollars.
