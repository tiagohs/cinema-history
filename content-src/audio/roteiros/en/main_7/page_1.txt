## [01] J-horror  (2067 car., ~2.1 min)

Looking at the twenty tens as a whole, it's clear that those years were responsible for the rebirth of horror in theaters. This isn't exclusive to that decade, considering the nineteen seventies and nineteen nineties, but the twenty tens brought new possibilities to this vast genre.

Examples of this are films such as The Cabin in the Woods, from twenty twelve, The Conjuring, from twenty thirteen, It Follows, from twenty fourteen, The Witch, from twenty fifteen, Get Out, from twenty seventeen, and Hereditary, from twenty eighteen. We saw the return of the pillars of the classic slasher genre with the new sequel to Halloween, from twenty eighteen, and eighties horror in the adaptation of It, from twenty seventeen. In addition, with the internet and the streaming explosion, original horror films from other countries took the spotlight, such as The Babadook, from twenty fourteen, and Goodnight Mommy, from twenty fourteen.

Physical media became popular again in the form of vinyl records, deluxe Blu-rays and figurines. And even on television (or, again, streaming), horror came back in full force, as can be seen in Bates Motel, The Walking Dead, The Haunting of Hill House, Lovecraft Country and Stranger Things. At the twenty seventeen Oscars, two films that can be classified as horror or monster movies, Get Out and The Shape of Water, from twenty seventeen, won major awards.

For many horror fans, the two thousands were a slow burn for horror. As mentioned in previous chapters, there were directors who sought to reinvent themselves and brought new purpose to a genre that had until then become repetitive. Japan was one of those responsible for breathing new life into it with the previously discussed J-horror. But the decade remained dominated by many, many remakes, few of which managed to please critics and audiences.

When it premiered in twenty thirteen, The Conjuring did the horror film field a favor. It breathed new life into the genre and also delivered a well-conceived portrayal of one of the strands of this lucrative segment: possession and exorcism. Lili Taylor (born in nineteen sixty-seven) said in interviews that she watched the classic The Exorcist, from nineteen seventy-three, to find inspiration for the most intense scenes, near the resolution of the narrative conflicts of this film directed by James Wan, from a screenplay by Chad Hayes (born in nineteen sixty-one) and Carey W. Hayes (born in nineteen sixty-one).

Over its one hundred twelve minutes, the film takes us into the heart of the Perron family, headed by Carolyn (Lili Taylor) and Roger (Ron Livingston). They moved to a remote farmhouse in nineteen seventy-one and intend to live life to the fullest with their children, but something very strange in the house keeps the family from living in harmony. It is at this point that the couple Ed (Patrick Wilson) and Lorraine Warren (Vera Farmiga) are invited to look into the situation in search of answers for the furniture constantly dragged out of place, the wailing ever present like lullabies, and the omnipresence of a shadow that hovers around the place. What is the dark presence responsible for frightening the residents of this house, which has stood since seventeen thirty-six and holds many memories? Through careful investigation, secrets will be revealed and plot twists will show that films with exorcism at their core still work well when they are directed and told competently.

At the time of its release, many tiresome people complained about the plot's reversal of the history of Bathsheba, a woman accused of witchcraft who actually led a simple life, unlike the inventive process of diabolical possession in the film, in which her husband supposedly caught her in the act of sacrificing their own child in the name of "evil." It is the part of the screenplay that makes one of the film's scariest scenes possible, and it is worth stressing that in a work of fiction, narrative freedom runs wild; it need not be "exorcised" in pursuit of an absolute truth of the facts.

## [06] Blumhouse  (836 car., ~0.8 min)

The market for gore and explicit horror scenes never necessarily went away, but the appetite for it was dying. The aim of those films wasn't to scare, but to shock. Audiences were changing, a new generation and new social problems were emerging and, just as in the past, horror films reflected that.

From here on, horror flourished. The success of both films turned Jason Blum into a modern-day Roger Corman (born in nineteen twenty-six), producing a ton of films every year for little money, and occasionally turning out masterpieces. It is thanks to Blum's vision of low-budget/high-return horror that the Blumhouse studio became a household name and, under its banner, films such as Sinister, from twenty twelve, Creep, from twenty fourteen, Oculus, from twenty thirteen, The Purge, from twenty thirteen, and Get Out reached theaters.

## [07] IFC Midnight  (1237 car., ~1.2 min)

Alongside Blumhouse, other production companies began to emerge on the market, inspired by the idea of investing in more independent, low-budget projects. IFC Midnight, A24 and Millennium Films began distributing and producing tons of horror films, based on Jason Blum's low-cost/high-return model. But more than that, most of these films had a quality that hadn't been seen in a long time. The Witch, produced by A24, was highly praised at its screening during the twenty fifteen Sundance Film Festival, and It Follows, produced by Millennium Films, was nominated for the "Critics Week Grand Prize" at the Cannes Film Festival in twenty fourteen.

At the same time, with the popularity of streaming services such as Netflix, Hulu and Amazon Prime - and, later, Shudder - if a film didn't do well in theaters, it could earn its money back through streaming. The services themselves then began investing in original productions, with Netflix's The Haunting of Hill House, created by Mike Flanagan, from nineteen seventy-eight, being one of the biggest highlights (even though it is a series). Mike would rise to fame after this release, going on to direct the sequel to The Shining, from nineteen eighty, Doctor Sleep, from twenty nineteen.

It is amid many a thou and thee that the screenplay of The Witch, from twenty fifteen, based on historical documents and folk accounts derived from the "witch hunt" hysteria that swept the United States (and Europe) in the seventeenth century, tells us, in a very peculiar way, a macabre drama that takes shape about sixty years before the infamous and widely known "Salem witch trials."

Here we are in New England (the region in the northeastern United States), in the sixteen thirties, and right at the start of the film we see a family of peasants expelled from an agrarian settlement, a plantation, but not exactly as we know them geographically, in a brief trial without much intention of extreme punishment or use of force. The patriarch, William (Ralph Ineson), speaks of divine punishment and the weight of God's hands upon the villagers, but the real reason for his trial and the choice of exile from the place are not explored in the film. In the very first minutes, we see the family (father, mother and five children, among them a baby) leave the place and settle in a forest, from which director Robert Eggers' conceptual and aesthetic work for the horror component of the film immediately emerges.

Let it be clear: The Witch is a scary film. But not for the reasons that viewers of the "new generation of horror" are usually accustomed to. Here we enter the territory of works such as Häxan, from nineteen twenty-two, Vredens Dag (Day of Wrath, nineteen forty-three), Vargtimmen (Hour of the Wolf, nineteen sixty-eight), Rosemary's Baby or The Babadook. The horror in The Witch is historical, cultural, psychological; it draws its fear and its reflection on evil from Christian roots, bringing out the devil in disguise while building an atmosphere of fear, ignorance, apprehension and a great deal of suspense until things go mad in the final half hour of the film.

The faded cinematography, in strong dialogue with the paintings of Diego Velázquez and Francisco de Goya, is a spectacle in itself and is by far the film's best element; better than the excellent costumes (the result of careful historical research) and the precise art direction, which knew how to work with a minimum of items in the exteriors and to organize very well the composition of what should appear in the shots, all of them beautifully captured and within a framing rhythm that earns Robert Eggers the laurels of an applause-worthy director, something that doesn't happen when we look at his screenplay, which is what makes The Witch a much smaller film than the first international impressions led us to believe when it premiered at the Sundance Film Festival in January twenty fifteen.

What we might call the film's "first block" is built on two narrative measures: the first as a psychological push for the audience (we're waiting for the fear!) and the second as the placement of the protagonist family into the setting from which the fear will come.

Notice the screenplay's good opening game, which, as the minutes pass, unrolls litanies, justifiable up to a point, and often underworks the horror, so much so that the opinion is unanimous that the big surprise, the fear and the evil, in fact, only appear at the end of the film, preceded by groundwork, a suggestion with a few "optical crumbs" that make us curious, even charming us with the game of "who is the witch in all this?", which makes everything even more macabre with the revelation of the evil form in one of the last scenes. But in its final minutes, the script takes on the personality of a coven that simply destroys the surprise factor kept until then, and almost everything that could have made sense and stayed strong up to that point fades away.

One shouldn't take away from The Witch its wonderful technical work, as well as some horror elements created throughout the story, notably the fight between the siblings, the accusations and the final stretch, up to the moment the coven appears. However, the total surrender to the clash of evil versus good and religious domination in building this "theater of horrors" gave the film an occasionally tedious development, despite moments of pure dread and psychological torture.

## [12] Warner Bros  (1729 car., ~1.7 min)

In twenty nineteen, the studio decided to go ahead with the plan to make new films based on its great figures, focusing on standalone stories. Produced by Blumhouse, The Invisible Man, from twenty twenty, directed by Leigh Whannell (born in nineteen seventy-seven), with Elisabeth Moss (born in nineteen eighty-two) in the lead role, crept quietly into theaters. The film was a tremendous box office and critical success, with praise for Moss's performance and the story conceived by Leigh, with its inventive modernization of the novel's plot and its combination of scares with "a smart narrative about how people can be manipulated and abused in harmful relationships."

Of all the major studios, Warner Bros was the one that did best with its investments. Unexpectedly, with the release of The Conjuring, the studio launched one of the most financially successful franchises of all time. When looking for someone to direct the remake of IT, Warner Bros. turned to Andy Muschietti (born in nineteen seventy-three), who had previously directed only Mama, from twenty thirteen, - both the short and the remake produced by Guillermo del Toro (born in nineteen sixty-four). IT grossed more than seven hundred point four million dollars and was acclaimed by critics.

Horror films play on our primal fears, but they offer much more than just scares and revulsion. Horror as social narrative has always tracked the fears and anxieties of society at a given moment in history. The dark silent films of the nineteen twenties represented the fears of a world traumatized by World War One. They were a way of visually narrating the psychological damage that one of the bloodiest events in history caused to soldiers and to Europe as a whole.

## [13] Rosemary's Baby  (2253 car., ~2.2 min)

In the nineteen fifties, a world recovering from the traumas of World War Two, the looming threat of nuclear war during the Cold War and technological advances becoming part of people's lives gave rise to American sci-fi horrors. Hostile alien creatures that destroyed entire cities with chemical weapons and possessed people's minds and bodies represented American fear of Russian weaponry and the political terror of the era.

The classic Rosemary's Baby, from nineteen sixty-eight, for example, conveys the fear women had at the time that the government could take control of their bodies. Swap conservative politicians for a satanic cult, and the horror was ready. The horror stories told by cinema over the decades have held mirrors up to society. Or, as Karina Wilson of Horror Film History puts it best: "Horror movies are the current version of the epic poems and ballads our ancestors told around the fire."

[CITAÇÃO] My films deal with the human monster, this social monster. And the villain that lives inside us

said Jordan Peele.

In today's context, the global geopolitical situation, new technologies that transform human relationships and movements organized by minorities have given rise to films that probe the modern social fabric, depicting scenarios of pure contemporary malaise. Psychological horror in recent films sets out to break taboos in order to deal with grief, sanity and the scars of abuse, as in Hereditary and The Babadook, or with paranoia about gender and sexuality, as in It Follows and The Witch.

In horror films, Black people have always had specific roles: from pimps to prostitutes, dangerous creatures worthy of dread and, above all, as the only Black person in a group of friends, who is the first to die. Horror, and cinema as a whole, still owes a great debt when it comes to Black representation, although, as with women, some films have worked to fill this historical gap. This racial disparity is discussed in depth by African American actors and directors in the documentary Horror Noire: A History of Black Horror, from twenty nineteen, an extension of the book of the same name, in which they analyze how African Americans began to leave supporting roles behind to take on the roles of heroes and good guys of the story.

## [14] Jordan Peele  (1898 car., ~1.9 min)

It is in this new wave that the necessary social horror of Jordan Peele, from nineteen seventy-nine, emerges. After working as a comedian for more than ten years, Peele, who had always wanted to be a film director, made his mark on the genre with stories written and directed by a Black man, from a genuine Black perspective on racism, and with a prominent Black cast. The director heightens social aspects, rules and structures to an agonizing level to turn the setting into a true nightmare. An atmosphere of psychological horror hangs over everyday situations. Sometimes the worst feeling of dread in a scene is simply the way the characters involved look at the protagonist, as in Get Out. But he also uses biting humor strategically to break some of the tension from the weight the subject carries.

In Get Out, his debut film, the director uses the sinister concept of rich, white neoliberals transplanting their brains into Black bodies. An older woman with her Black partner suggests that they also serve as sex toys. The protagonist is examined like actual merchandise to be acquired by the party guests. This has a lot to say about the deep-rooted legacy of that traumatic chapter of history, when trafficked Africans were sold as property rather than as human beings.

The horror hovers over the vulnerability of a person who belongs to a minority when faced with the judgmental gaze of those who cannot see them as an ordinary human being. Get Out is about American racism, but the director points out that it isn't so simple to categorize. "My films deal with the human monster, this social monster. And the villain that lives inside us," he explained on the New York Times podcast Still Processing. The film was nominated in four Oscar categories and took home the statuette for Best Original Screenplay, making Peele the first African American director to win the award in that category.

Film and television frequently deal with racism, which was recently taken on by the series Dear White People and has gained more space in recent decades. Treating a social critique through a more specific genre, apart from the usual dramas and comedies, manages to bring freshness to the cinematic language, given that the problem itself, in fact, needs no renewal. Racism is right there for anyone who wants to see it, belittled by the media and by the people, who, when they cannot deny its existence, try to dodge any concrete involvement in the social fact. Navel-gazing is easier. Jordan Peele's recent entry into the horror genre, coming from the world of comedy, gives rise to Get Out, which, through a satirical review of racial conflicts, explicitly and implicitly carries a fervent analytical power on the subject. In the plot, Chris Washington (Daniel Kaluuya) is dating Rose Armitage (Allison Williams), a white girl who plans to take her boyfriend, who is Black, to finally meet her presumably non-racist family. The screenplay introduces, at the forefront, a shrewd subversion of the American suburban setting, initially addressing a specific case of racism involving a police officer, extremely important for our apprehension at the end of the film, in a certain scene that takes us back to this past event. But it is inside the Armitage house that the fear of the absurdity inherent in certain uncomfortable situations will show us the true soul of the work.

## [20] Rod Serling  (867 car., ~0.9 min)

Besides narrating the episodes, Jordan Peele was one of the executive producers of the new Twilight Zone in twenty nineteen. The original series was created in nineteen fifty-nine by Rod Serling (nineteen twenty-four to nineteen seventy-five) and explored science fiction and horror concepts to send social messages. The series, in which each episode told a separate story, delivered one of the most striking experiences of social horror, serving as an allegory of postwar American society. "A Martian can say things that a Republican or Democrat can't," Serling used to say. Peele kept sci-fi as an allegory for contemporary social situations. In the episode "Not All Men," for example, director Christina Choe leads a satire in which an alien infection spreads across the US, mysteriously affecting only men and turning them into aggressive, misogynistic creatures.
