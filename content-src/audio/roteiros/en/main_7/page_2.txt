Part zero seven: From twenty ten to twenty nineteen. Chapter two: Superheroes and Shared Universes.

The decade of superheroes: Disney reigns as owner of Marvel Studios, while Warner, home of DC, fights for its place by releasing new films and building its, troubled, DCEU.

## [01] Cinematic Universe  (876 car., ~0.9 min)

Superhero films gained great prominence during the twenty tens, following a path of success already begun in the previous decade. On one side, Marvel, headed by Kevin Feige (born in nineteen seventy-three), made its Cinematic Universe official with the release of Iron Man, from two thousand eight: the idea was to release a film for each hero who would later form the Avengers, Iron Man, The Incredible Hulk, from two thousand eight, Thor, from twenty eleven, and Captain America: The First Avenger, from twenty eleven, -, the famous comic book team that in the nineteen sixties went head-to-head with the Justice League, from DC Comics.

These films would take place in the same universe and share characters, with Black Widow, played by Scarlett Johansson (born in nineteen eighty-four), and Nick Fury, played by Samuel L. Jackson (born in nineteen forty-eight), as examples.

## [02] The Avengers  (1650 car., ~1.6 min)

The risk was great, something like this had never been done before (Universal may have done something somewhat similar in the thirties/forties with its classic monsters, but in a different way), but with the release of The Avengers, from twenty twelve, it became clear that the venture had paid off: with positive reviews, The Avengers received numerous awards and nominations, including the Oscar and BAFTA for achievement in visual effects, and broke numerous box office records.

Besides Marvel Studios, other studios such as Sony, Universal and Warner took advantage of the craze and brought out a variety of superhero and comic-book films. They were releases with highs and lows, including spectacular films such as Captain America: Civil War, from twenty sixteen, Deadpool, from twenty sixteen, Logan, from twenty seventeen, Guardians of the Galaxy, from twenty fourteen, Kick-Ass, from twenty ten, Black Panther, from twenty eighteen, Chronicle, from twenty twelve, and Joker, from twenty nineteen. These were films that played with a variety of genres, just as many other subgenres had done in the past.

Joker, considered by many to be more of a drama than a superhero film, used the DNA of Martin Scorsese's films (born in nineteen forty-two) to create a dense and complex character, thrown into a hopeless world. Logan, on the other hand, drew inspiration from Western and neo-western films to create a "road movie", in which the famous mutant seeks his redemption in a post-apocalyptic future where most mutants are dead.

## [03] Learn more: The Peak of the Marvel Universe (1)  (2322 car., ~2.3 min)

Learn more: The Peak of the Marvel Universe.

Marvel built a cinematic universe grounded in its ability to adapt and modify the characters it created in the comics. The notion that the mythology invented by Stan Lee (nineteen twenty-two to twenty eighteen), Jack Kirby (nineteen seventeen to nineteen ninety-four) and so many others would have to be altered, without losing its essence, may have been the magic ingredient in the recipe that guides Hollywood today. Ten years after the kickoff, the studio handed the Russo Brothers the mission of closing the Avengers' first arc. Infinity War accomplishes this task successfully and remains faithful to the qualities that made Marvel so acclaimed in recent years.

More than an adventure or action film, like most of its predecessors, this undertaking by the heroes has a much greater sense of urgency. It's an event. In other words, the stakes are clear and impactful. Everything has consequences. For the audience to feel this, screenwriters Stephen McFeely (born in nineteen sixty-nine) and Christopher Markus (born in nineteen seventy) chose to make the villain Thanos the center of the story. Avengers: Infinity War is a film carried by the unexpected charisma of its antagonist, who has contextualized, believable motivations and fits perfectly into the universe presented - he is cruel, merciful, has a sense of humor and is lovable. All this is also due to the good performance of Josh Brolin (born in nineteen sixty-eight) as the Titan, perfectly built by the visual effects company ILM.

This Thanos persona is built from his quest for the Infinity Stones, the gems that illustrate the film's title. In this relentless search, he reveals facets and relationships that bring him closer to the viewer, creating empathy through his actions toward supporting characters such as Gamora. His relationship with his daughters is what guides the plot of Infinity War, and it may be the biggest success of Marvel, which for the second time hits the mark in the emotional construction of a villain - the first was Killmonger, from Black Panther. The character's feelings and humanization are presented without mawkishness and with simple dialogue, always aiming at the adventure's final goal and with the awareness that the film is a blockbuster with no philosophical pretensions.

## [04] Learn more: The Peak of the Marvel Universe (2)  (2259 car., ~2.3 min)

With an enemy established, the directors are left with the challenge of reconciling so many heroes in the same story. And the competence shown in Civil War is repeated here, on a much larger scale. While in Captain America's last film the brothers struggled to figure out how to make the problems of the central dilemma reverberate (the fight between Steve Rogers and Tony Stark), here everyone involved is affected in some way. The Guardians group works perfectly with Thor, in both the comic and the dramatic parts. The transformation of the God of Thunder is striking, as he finally finds a path between comedy and celestial eloquence, continuing what was introduced in Ragnarok.

The impact on Earth is felt by the group led by Iron Man, Spider-Man and Doctor Strange, even though they are in different places at the same time. Robert Downey Jr. (born in nineteen sixty-five) repeats his great chemistry with Tom Holland (born in nineteen ninety-six) and shows that he can work well with Benedict Cumberbatch (born in nineteen seventy-six). The trio also serves as a reminder of the Battle of New York and of the powers of the Stones, explaining the impact that Thanos's power will have on the universe - on top of all that, they star in one of the best action scenes in Marvel history. It's proof that the Russos have evolved a lot when it comes to directing moments packed with special effects. The sequence uses each hero's powers and in the end delivers the emotion needed for an event the size of Infinity War.

The pace and construction of the narrative are only not matched by Captain America and co. Even though the action scenes in Wakanda are much better than those in Black Panther and the relationship between Scarlet Witch and Vision works, the screenplay takes all the weight away from Steve Rogers, Bruce Banner and T'Challa. They shine in action moments, but have little influence on the story and so always seem sidelined, less important in the face of what the other heroes are going through. Since the core of Rogers on film was the conflict with Stark, there is little left to question - the warrior side of all his companions is explored more than the dramatic side. In the end, the most human of the heroes becomes the least interesting.

## [05] Learn more: The Peak of the Marvel Universe (3)  (748 car., ~0.8 min)

Infinity War's ability to make the villain the best feature of a film that had everything to be the heroes' main stage is admirable. Thanos steals the show unexpectedly, with personality and without the raving madness of typical villains. There is purpose, there is justification and there is soul in every word spoken by the purple giant, who also gives the heroes action scenes that will stay in the audience's memory for a long time. Marvel delivers everything the fans wanted, shows progress in the treatment of its characters and remains faithful to the entertainment recipe that made it the icon of contemporary cinema. Avengers: Infinity War is the promised event from beginning to end, and the beginning of a new era in the superhero genre.

## [06] Fantastic Four  (1221 car., ~1.2 min)

But, in parallel to this, releases that only sought to take advantage of a "wave" that still lasts reached theaters, Fantastic Four, from twenty fifteen, being one of the most emblematic cases. Before starting its career in film as a producer, and on the verge of bankruptcy, Marvel sold its main characters to major production companies: Sony Pictures got the rights to Spider-Man and his universe, and Twentieth Century Fox got the X-Men and the Fantastic Four.

[CITAÇÃO] Five years ago, we lost. All of us. We lost friends. We lost family. We lost a part of ourselves. Today, we have a chance to take it all back. This is the fight of our lives. And we're gonna win. Whatever it takes.

said Captain America in Avengers: Endgame.

The deal was that both had to make films at least every ten years; otherwise, the rights would revert to Marvel. It's no coincidence that Sony produced a Spider-Man remake in such a short span of time. The same can be said of Fantastic Four, which sought to follow Marvel's formula in launching one of the most famous families in comics. It didn't work, and the loss was gigantic, sounding the alarm that times had changed and that bad superhero films no longer succeeded simply for being about heroes.

## [07] Avengers: Infinity War  (1304 car., ~1.3 min)

Marvel Studios seemed to understand this from the start, and even while releasing some uneven films, most of them had considerable quality. Its Shared Universe (MCU) was a resounding success with critics and audiences, becoming the highest-grossing franchise in film history. Over the years, more and more characters from one film appeared in another. Captain America: Civil War, for example, marked the return of Spider-Man to the MCU, thanks to a partnership between Marvel Studios and Sony Pictures. The peak of investing in these franchises came after ten years with the release of two films that would close the MCU's first phase in theaters: Avengers: Infinity War, from twenty eighteen, and Avengers: Endgame, from twenty nineteen. Both films were acclaimed by critics and broke box office records, surpassing the two billion mark (with Endgame managing, at the time, to beat the record of Avatar, the highest-grossing film in history).

But in twenty seventeen there was an announcement that would change this universe even more: Disney, which had bought Marvel Entertainment for four billion dollars, now announced the purchase of Twentieth Century Fox for fifty-two billion dollars, bringing with it characters of great importance to the Marvel universe, such as the X-Men and the Fantastic Four.

## [08] Christopher Nolan  (1397 car., ~1.4 min)

DC, owned by Warner Bros., had not released anything involving a shared universe until the release of The Avengers, but it was already on a successful path with Christopher Nolan's (born in nineteen seventy) The Dark Knight trilogy. With the success of the MCU, it was announced in twenty twelve that DC would have its own universe: Zack Snyder (born in nineteen sixty-six) was chosen to lead the venture, releasing new films about Superman, Batman, Aquaman, Wonder Woman, the Flash and Cyborg, culminating in a future Justice League film.

Zack was already known for his dark auteur filmmaking through three hundred, from two thousand seven, and Watchmen, from two thousand nine, and it was no different with Man of Steel, from twenty thirteen. The film received a mixed reception, drawing criticism mainly for the idea of a darker Superman than most people were used to.

Even with the lukewarm reception, Warner went ahead with its plans and in twenty thirteen, at San Diego Comic-Con, announced the film Batman v Superman: Dawn of Justice, from twenty sixteen. The news was received with tremendous excitement because, for the first time, two of the greatest heroes of film and comics would be brought together in one movie. The film reached theaters in twenty sixteen, also featuring Wonder Woman, as well as brief nods to Aquaman, the Flash and Cyborg, hinting at the future Justice League film.

## [09] Wonder Woman  (1539 car., ~1.5 min)

To the surprise of many, the film was a box office failure, dividing critics and audiences between those who liked Zack's darker vision and those who found the film confusing and extremely sluggish. This made Warner lose some enthusiasm for Zack Snyder's vision for the DCEU. Wonder Woman, from twenty seventeen, directed by Patty Jenkins (born in nineteen seventy-one), was released a year later, with a more optimistic feel and avoiding mentions of any other DCEU character. That same year, Justice League, from twenty seventeen, reached theaters, credited to Zack Snyder but finished by Joss Whedon (born in nineteen sixty-four), director of The Avengers. Zack had to leave during filming because his daughter had died.

[CITAÇÃO] Do you bleed? You will...

The film's failure was enormous, as big as that of Batman v Superman. The harshest criticism targeted the film's format, which seemed to try to copy Marvel's light, funny formula in the worst possible way, in addition to the countless changes Joss had made to the film, straying far from the vision proposed by Snyder. Fans were disappointed, but insisted on knowing why the film had turned out so different from what Zack Snyder had proposed since Man of Steel, keeping the hashtag #releasethesnydercut in Twitter's trending topics for years. And in twenty nineteen, seeking to bring new subscribers to its new streaming service HBO Max, Warner listened to the fans and gave Zack the green light to release his version of the film, more than four hours long.

## [10] Learn more: The Epic End of a Saga (1)  (2225 car., ~2.2 min)

Learn more: The Epic End of a Saga.

The era of superheroes on screen marches on with all its might, with films that each year take their place among the biggest box office hits and series that, whether on Netflix, Fox or the CW, keep their audiences practically unshaken. The sad fact, however, remains that there is greater concern with making a work fit in with others from the same universe than with its actual quality, after all, good or bad, such a product will be consumed anyway. It is the abandonment of the blockbuster with traces of auteur cinema in favor of a formulaic, pasteurized structure that, unfortunately, reflects and at the same time influences the comics themselves. How can we not look back and miss works made by auteurs who simply wanted to bring us their vision of a given character from the illustrated pages to the big screen? Films like Batman Begins, from two thousand five, Spider-Man, from two thousand two, or even the controversial Man of Steel no longer exist; they have been turned into mere pieces of huge puzzles, with no hope of ever seeing the complete picture, since, year after year, more works from these cinematic universes are announced. Although no one had any idea what this would become, it was X-Men, from two thousand, back in two thousand, that opened the floodgates for these productions.

With this in mind, the weight Logan carries on its back becomes quite clear: it is the farewell of a character introduced seventeen years ago, of the mutant who was the protagonist of the first adaptation of this whole new cinematic wave, a character who, played by Hugh Jackman (born in nineteen sixty-eight), we have watched evolve and grow older over all these years. If we look beyond the magic of cinema, it is a summary of the actor's entire career: at the time of the first film's release, he was still inexperienced, with only three features on his résumé, and now he has more than forty, including leading roles in prominent works such as Prisoners, from twenty thirteen, and Les Misérables, from twenty twelve. Considering all this history, carried by both Wolverine and Jackman, it is gratifying to see that the character's third solo film takes a more auteur-driven stance.

## [11] Learn more: The Epic End of a Saga (2)  (2053 car., ~2.0 min)

That said, Logan is not meant to be seen as part of a cinematic universe, one more entry in the extensive (and confusing) mythology of the mutants on film, but rather as something apart, a feature about the farewell of Wolverine as we know him, which, despite taking into account what came before, works perfectly on its own, requiring only basic knowledge of the X-Men mythology, which is already more than ingrained in pop culture.

The plot is set in twenty twenty-nine, a future in which mutants are going extinct and the X-Men no longer exist. Logan (Hugh Jackman), whose healing factor no longer works as it used to, works as a limousine driver and supports and cares for Charles Xavier (Patrick Stewart), who suffers from some illness, possibly Alzheimer's, and needs to take medication to control his powers, which go off involuntarily. Alongside Wolverine in this endeavor is Caliban (Stephen Merchant), one of the few remaining mutants. This whole scenario changes, however, when a woman approaches Wolverine asking him to take the girl Laura Kinney (Dafne Keen) north to a place known only as Utopia. Reluctant at first, Logan is left with no choice when a group of mercenaries, led by Donald Pierce (Boyd Holbrook), comes after the girl.

From early on, the work establishes a melancholic tone in its narrative, right from the start we see the protagonist hardened, aged, unkempt and covered in scars. James Mangold (born in nineteen sixty-three), who had also directed The Wolverine, from twenty thirteen, helms the project and is not afraid to show that this is a story for adults. There is ample brutality and rawness in the action sequences, which serve to convey the urgency of the screenplay and the decay of this universe. In many respects it feels as if we were in a revisionist Western, something reinforced by the desert landscapes that fill most of the running time. With this we understand that, in fact, no one is safe: Wolverine is no longer that invincible mutant; he is a man whose pain we feel every time he unleashes his claws.

## [12] Learn more: The Epic End of a Saga (3)  (2253 car., ~2.2 min)

At the same time, we feel a strong sense of nostalgia. We see this universe as a melancholic dystopian future and can't help but wonder what happened to the X-Men and the others of their kind, who were suddenly wiped out. Looking at Logan and Charles Xavier is almost like seeing a portrait of defeat, of a broken dream. Laura therefore serves as a new hope; she is the new taking the place of the old, which must cease to exist. We clearly see in her a younger Wolverine, inexperienced and full of rage, and her constant silence perfectly mirrors Wolverine's disposition in the franchise's first feature film. At the same time, we watch the protagonist struggling to fulfill this role of father, something that is in no way forced in the narrative and that only truly materializes at the ending with the striking line "don't be what they made you."

The beauty of Logan's screenplay lies in these small details, in the way it creates a more realistic story with a genuine sense of loss. At no point does it bother to be didactic; it doesn't use title cards or voice-over to prepare us for this future, but passes on each piece of information we need through its dialogue and wastes no time offering us the causes of it all. At no point do we learn what Xavier's illness is; Alzheimer's is merely hinted at through Zander Rice (Richard E. Grant), who is also the apparent culprit behind the mutants' disappearance, something we also don't understand how it happened and, in fact, don't need to know. The focus here is Wolverine, something made very clear by the title, this is his final journey. In this road movie it is interesting to observe how the superhero film formula is altered. This isn't an action film with dramatic sequences, but a drama with action sequences. That said, it is easy to see how each combat scene carries extra weight. Added to Mangold's direction, which does away with shaky cameras and endless cuts, we get truly engaging action. It is no longer about the old clashes between good guys and villains, but a real fight for survival, each with a lot to lose, something we can clearly see in the hotel scene in Las Vegas, with Xavier about to be killed, and later at the house on the road, which gives us the professor's farewell.

## [13] Learn more: The Epic End of a Saga (4)  (2031 car., ~2.0 min)

In this respect, the trio of screenwriters formed by Scott Frank (born in nineteen sixty), James Mangold (born in nineteen sixty-three) and Michael Green doesn't bother providing a long dramatic scene for Charles's death, they know that the weight of such an event is inherent in the years we've followed the character on screen. The screenplay creates anguish in a not-so-obvious way, takes Charles off screen for a while, and we don't know for sure what his fate will be, however obvious it may be. The farewell between him and Logan then happens in a short and simple way, showing confidence in the work of the two actors, who truly come across as two old friends.

Truly, neither Jackman nor Stewart disappoints at any point. The former delivers his best work since Prisoners, offering a true portrait of someone tired, who wants to die but can't, because he has to take care of the man who took him in so many years ago. Every time his claws come out we feel his reluctance, how much it hurts him, and he uses his anger to get around this. Every scream from the actor sounds like someone letting all his pain pour out, giving us the most human comic book character ever seen on screen, rivaled only by Christopher Nolan's Batman films.

Stewart, in turn, is a sad figure to behold, the perfect image of the brilliant old man whom the years have caught up with. Even though the screenplay doesn't bother to be didactic, his condition is real at every moment. We see in his eyes a man fighting against his own mind; his way of speaking betrays his weariness and his sense of powerlessness in that situation. At the same time, he works as the necessary comic relief, speaking his mind whenever needed. The few comic elements we see in the film are well placed, and the irony present in most of them works as the classic "laughing so as not to cry" of characters who saw the world around them collapse. There is no way to look at Xavier without remembering his past, his attempt to make the world a better place for those of his kind.

## [14] Learn more: The Epic End of a Saga (5)  (2434 car., ~2.4 min)

Newcomer Dafne Keen (born in two thousand five) also doesn't disappoint, giving us a performance that truly captures the essence of the young girl who lived confined and subjected to experiments her whole life. Her silence clearly reflects her distrust of others, and when she finally speaks we are already at a point where she feels more at ease with the characters around her, as she slowly comes to see Logan as her father, something that is consummated at the ending, when she even calls him that. The action sequences involving the character also manage to be especially devastating; after all, no one expected to see a child pierced by a harpoon. Best of all, we are talking about justified violence, which reinforces the fact that the villains see her only as an experiment and not as a child.

And on that point, Boyd Holbrook (born in nineteen eighty-one), best known for his role as Agent Murphy in the series Narcos, delivers an antagonist quite different from what we're used to. Although Rice is the story's real villain, it is Pierce who functions as the work's main antagonist, pursuing the protagonist from the beginning to the very end. He is flawed and human to such an extent that we can see him as the man who does anything for money; the justification for what he does is right there, and we need nothing more. His dialogue also carries the old prejudice against mutants, with him considering himself one of the "good guys," even though he is chasing a child.

Wolverine's clone, in turn, who comes as a surprise in the middle of the film, serves to create a parallel between Wolverine's past and present. In him we see Weapon X, the uncontrolled being of yesteryear, who was "tamed" by Xavier's teachings. He is the Logan who never was part of the X-Men, a mere instrument. The protagonist's death at the hands of his copy therefore means much more than "only Wolverine can kill himself"; it is about a person having to face his past and, although he ends up dying, he comes out victorious, planting the seed of the future, which is Laura. His final words to the girl, "so that’s what it feels like", reflect the abandonment of his violent life in favor of a true family, echoing what Xavier had told him earlier about enjoying life in a home, with people who love each other. At the same time, we can interpret his final line as him finally getting to know death, something so distant because of his healing factor.

## [15] Learn more: The Epic End of a Saga (6)  (1451 car., ~1.4 min)

The ending itself, which thankfully does without any post-credits scene, is perfectly consistent with the work's own premise. This is a story about Logan, the Wolverine, and it ends with his burial, which, not by chance, takes place near where he was born, in Canada. The screenplay wisely does not give us an epilogue, with Laura and the other children reaching their final destination; that is not for the narrative presented here, the seed of the future, as said before, has already been planted, and that is a story for a possible next work. By ending exactly there, the film establishes itself as the story of a man confronting his past and finally becoming part of this world that, for so long, pushed him to the margins of society.

Logan is, therefore, a beautiful farewell to characters we have followed since two thousand, a tribute to Hugh Jackman and his Wolverine, who captivated us over the years and showed clear signs of evolution. By dispensing with formulas and giving us a more auteur-driven film, even if a blockbuster, James Mangold offers us a work that works perfectly on its own, without needing to be part of an extensive universe full of references and disconnected scenes. What we have here is a dramatic film with comic book characters, not a comic book character feature. Definitely one of the best adaptations from the illustrated pages, which abandons pasteurization in favor of something that truly manages to draw us in.

## [16] X-Men: First Class  (1142 car., ~1.1 min)

Fox, before being bought by Disney, kept investing in the X-Men franchise. In X-Men: First Class, from twenty eleven, a large cast brings together an interesting story featuring a young Professor Xavier who still had his hair. With one of its most beloved characters in hand, the studio released at least three films with Wolverine as the protagonist, Logan being the best of them, which made history as the first superhero film nominated for an Oscar for Best Screenplay.

But the biggest surprise was certainly Deadpool, a risky R-rated film. It featured the character as the classic motormouth in his trademark comic-book suit - unlike the version seen in X-Men Origins: Wolverine, from two thousand nine, -, breaking the fourth wall and mocking the competition (including Fox itself). The film was a breath of fresh originality, becoming the highest-grossing adult film in history up to that point - later surpassed by Joker years afterward.
