## [01] Media Rights Capital  (792 car., ~0.8 min)

The history of streaming, the transmission of video and music over the internet, began in February twenty eleven, when two producers from the company Media Rights Capital were trying to sell the idea for a new series to several American television networks. Inspired by a BBC miniseries made in the UK, the pitch for House of Cards was getting a lukewarm reception for good reason. Since two thousand six's The West Wing, no political drama had been successful in the United States. But the lack of enthusiasm from TV executives didn't discourage the two producers. They kept believing the series would be made sooner or later and decided to turn their attention to the next step of the business plan. They began making visits to sell the broadcast rights for the period following the TV run.

## [02] Netflix  (792 car., ~0.8 min)

That was when they knocked on Netflix's door. There, it was a completely different story. Netflix's offer was even better than covering the roughly six million dollars needed to make a pilot, which was standard practice in a market dominated by TV channels. Netflix offered to invest, right off the bat, one hundred million dollars for two seasons, a total of twenty-six episodes. No airing on TV channels. Everything over the internet. At the time, YouTube was already popular and Netflix itself had a promising digital platform, but streaming didn't scare anyone. It took the resounding success of House of Cards to cause the biggest tremor in the entertainment industry in about one hundred years. It was clear that the battle over the production and distribution of content would heat up.

Produced by Esperanto Filmoj along with Participant Media and distributed by Netflix (except in China), Roma, from twenty eighteen, is the film by Alfonso [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]] (born in nineteen sixty-one) that follows his acclaimed Gravity, from twenty thirteen. Making history as the first production distributed on a streaming platform to win a major festival, taking the Golden Lion in Venice, Roma is set in the early nineteen seventies in Mexico City. With a blend of Amarcord and other films that explore family settings over a given period of time (some viewers may even draw connections with Fanny and Alexander, though it's worth saying that the family, and even social, approach here is of a different order), Roma is a film about bonds. A long childhood memory.

At the center of attention we have Cleo (Yalitza Aparicio), a maid who works in Sofia's (Marina de Tavira) house. The house is large and there are four children of different ages. In the screenplay, [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]] openly works with his family memories and dedicates the film to Libo, the family's former nanny. Visually, the picture is splendid. The opening shot trains us for a kind of contemplation and expectation that remains active throughout the entire journey, with the direction exploring depth of field, reflections, frames filled with two or three actions happening at the same time, and always elegant camera movements, matching the anamorphic choice for capture (the aspect ratio simulates two point thirty-five:one, today two point thirty-nine, with a wide expanse capable of nicely highlighting the scenic space or large landscapes).

Considering that the plot's central focus is to show the "chronicle of a life," the director's signature never loses sight of that vision. Every shot is brilliantly conceived, and even though the editing (credited to him and Adam Gough) has some surplus scenes of spatial context, which sometimes last longer than they should or aren't really necessary, the film's true mark is the audience's immersion in the life of this protagonist family and of the household's maids, especially Cleo, who receives from Yalitza Aparicio an excellent character construction: delicate, introspective, absolutely lovable. In the film, she is treated with all the tenderness a character can be treated with on screen, and serves as a sometimes invisible bond between the members of the family.

## [06] Kevin Spacey  (2087 car., ~2.1 min)

Following the maxim that data analysis is to the digital economy what steel was to the industrial age, Netflix executives decided to pour a truckload of money into the project because they had information the TV channels did not. They knew that actor Kevin Spacey (born in nineteen fifty-nine) and director David Fincher (born in nineteen sixty-two), both already attached to the project, were greatly admired. Netflix subscribers didn't usually search for films with Spacey, but when they watched one, they ended up watching several. The same happened with Fincher, director of The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, from two thousand eight, and The Social Network, from twenty ten. One of the biggest effects of House of Cards was to establish Netflix as a catalyst of the digital revolution in the entertainment industry. Until very recently, people could only watch videos by turning on the television. Today, videos are available on TVs, desktop computers, laptops, tablets and smartphones; in other words, anywhere and at any time.

Videos are offered on demand, as we've grown used to saying. With streaming, everything became faster, without the need for lengthy downloads. ## [07] Part 4  (1602 car., ~1.6 min)

The entertainment sector, which has always lived with an environment of incremental change, is now facing a disruption. According to estimates by the consulting firm Boston Consulting Group, fifty percent of the time devoted to entertainment in the United States in twenty twenty will be on demand. Video already accounts for more than seventy percent of global internet data traffic. In this expansion underway in many parts of the world, streaming companies have at least one big advantage. Technology allows them to adopt an effective pricing policy. In the digital age, the marginal cost of producing and distributing a copy of an information good, such as a film or a song, is close to zero. There's no need to manufacture a DVD or CD, or to think about the logistics of getting boxes of products to stores. Everything goes directly over the internet. This allows companies like Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney Plus, HBO Max and Apple TV to make large catalogs available to their customers.

But that's not all. Profit is higher when selling a bundle of products than when selling a single item. When a company sets the price of a product, one of two things invariably happens: either it charges a high price, leaving some consumers out, or it sets a lower price to reach a larger number of people and loses the extra money many interested buyers were willing to pay. In both cases, the company leaves money on the table. According to the concept known as bundling economics, selling bundles of products solves this problem because it ensures that each consumer finds something they really like.

## [08] Streaming, Sharing, Stealing  (932 car., ~0.9 min)

An Amazon Prime or Netflix subscriber who enjoys comedies, for example, has plenty of options. The same goes for fans of other kinds of films and series. This allows the service to charge the highest possible price to most of its customers. It is an application of the law of large numbers, according to which the more elements a set has, the closer it gets to the overall average. "Given how fast things change in the tech world, it's impossible to predict which companies will be the leaders in entertainment ten years from now. What can be said is that subscriptions giving access to large libraries will continue to exist. And the one that innovated in this respect was Netflix," says Michael D. Smith, a professor of information systems at Carnegie Mellon University and one of the authors of Streaming, Sharing, Stealing ("Streaming, Sharing, Stealing," in a loose translation), a book released in the United States last year.

While some of this year's best films stand out for their technical virtuosity, political commentary or star-studded casts, it is comforting to see how Noah Baumbach (born in nineteen sixty-nine) keeps writing works with the basic aim of exploring what is most intimate in his audience (and in himself), which may not sound so "relevant" to some, but few subjects are as universal and complex as human relationships.

Marriage Story, from twenty nineteen, is inspired, for the most part, by a divorce the director went through. This isn't the first time he has written about the subject, since he addressed his parents' separation in the two thousand five film The Squid and the Whale. Starring Adam Driver (born in nineteen eighty-three) and Scarlett Johansson (born in nineteen eighty-four) as Charlie and Nicole, respectively, Marriage Story presents the point of view of a couple trying to do everything they can so the divorce doesn't destroy everything they built along the way, such as their work and a child.

Noah sets out to make a film about the strength of love, even if through the lens of a divorce. Although his direction is detailed and minimalist, highlighting small everyday moments, such as the way a character takes off a shoe or runs a finger along a mug, the work's real triumph lies in its screenplay, imbued with naturalness thanks to excellent dialogue, combined with Randy Newman's gorgeous score, which evokes innocence even in some of the most melancholy scenes.

Right in the opening scene, we are introduced to the couple through a letter describing their best qualities, a smart decision to deliver information without sounding overly descriptive. Throughout the film, Noah doesn't let us forget each of the letter's points, gradually adding mannerisms that complement the opening descriptions, which shows that the screenplay is executed so well that we feel everything was improvised, and this ends up connecting, in some way, to the nature of the direction. It is hard for Baumbach to maintain any kind of impartiality between Charlie and Nicole, since he is mirroring himself in the former, up to a point (many of the events were altered or even invented to serve the plot), but there is a clear narrative of rise and fall, almost visually represented by one of the characters breaking down in tears in a long, powerful sequence in which the couple has to be brutally honest.

## [11] Part 6  (2250 car., ~2.2 min)

The multibillion-dollar question today is how big an impact streaming has on the traditional entertainment industry. One of the few things that can be said with certainty so far is that independent producers of series and documentaries are among those who have done very well. They have never had such a favorable environment, with the market expanding. The annual number of series produced in the United States went from one hundred eighty-two in two thousand two to five hundred thirty-two in twenty nineteen. Film studios, on the other hand, have never felt so threatened.

The supermarket chain Walmart once accounted for thirty percent of DVD sales in the United States in the nineties. That dependence of the studios on a single distributor, however, did not hurt their profit margins. Now everything is different. Never has a distributor had as much power as Netflix. "Netflix studies very closely something the studios don't have: data on the habits of millions of consumers. With that, it can recommend TV shows, series and films to customers that are very likely to suit their taste," says Pedro Ferreira, a professor in the disruptive technology management program at Carnegie Mellon University. The response of some of the studios (Walt Disney, Twenty-first Century Fox, Comcast and Time Warner) was to create their own digital platforms.

said Agu in Beasts of No Nation.

But this relationship is more complex: the studios try to copy Netflix, and Netflix tries to copy the studios. Amazon Prime Video, the streaming arm of the online retailer that entered the Brazilian market in December twenty sixteen, is riding the same wave as its competitor. Both Netflix and Amazon Prime Video invest heavily in original content. The amount Netflix spent on its own productions went from two point five billion dollars in twenty thirteen to seventeen billion dollars in twenty twenty. Amazon's jumped from one point two billion dollars to at least four point five billion dollars over the same period. At the twenty twenty-one Oscars, Netflix led with thirty-five nominations, Amazon received twelve and Apple TV received two. The strategy of these services aims at independence from traditional content producers.

## [12] Part 7  (1176 car., ~1.2 min)

The "Netflix effect" is, for obvious reasons, most evident in television. Broadcasters, both free-to-air and pay TV, sometimes see streaming as a threat (read: fear of losing viewers), sometimes as an opportunity (a chance to keep making money by distributing their productions through a new channel). What is certain is that the expansion of the technology pushed broadcasters out of their comfort zone, all of them now worried about the future of their business model. The industry's history began with free-to-air channels based on advertising revenue. The second phase was that of cable and satellite channels.

It originated in the United States in the eighties, but since then it has gained ground in other markets. The source of revenue in this second phase was subscribers as well as advertisers. Time ended up showing that the two types of TV could coexist without one cannibalizing the other's market. When streaming emerged, it was taken for granted that it had arrived to put an end to that harmony. In so-called mature markets, those of wealthy countries, expressions such as cord cutters, a reference to cable TV consumers who canceled the service, became popular.

## [13] Part 8  (902 car., ~0.9 min)

Pay TV penetration in the United States fell from ninety percent of households in twenty ten to eighty percent in twenty sixteen. Even more frightening were the nevers, young people who grew up watching on-demand streaming video and never signed up for a cable or satellite service. Recently, however, a more benevolent view has begun to gain strength. In a hefty ninety-two-page report on the industry published at the beginning of the year, the financial information company Morningstar sums up the new mood. Those who produce quality content will continue to be valued. Even the loss of viewers at some broadcasters has been given new interpretations. "With social media and messaging apps, people's time is increasingly scarce. Competition for attention keeps growing. And it doesn't come only from streaming channels," says Tristan Veale, a market analyst at the UK-based consultancy Futuresource.

Klaus first saw the light of day in twenty fifteen, after Sergio Pablos (born in nineteen seventy), who had worked in various capacities in animation for Disney on films such as A Goofy Movie, from nineteen ninety-five, The Hunchback of Notre Dame, from nineteen ninety-six, Hercules, from nineteen ninety-seven, Tarzan, from nineteen ninety-nine, and Treasure Planet, from two thousand two, in addition to creating the story for Universal Pictures' hit Despicable Me, from twenty ten, returned to Spain and opened his own studio, SPA. He tried to sell his Santa Claus origin story as a traditional 2D animation, without success, until it was finally rescued by the omnipresent Netflix at the end of twenty seventeen, which, securing distribution, invested the money needed for the work to be completed.

And the first animated feature film to receive the Netflix Original seal is a huge success, capable of finally putting Pablos on the radar for future projects and the streaming company in the competitive race for this kind of work. One of Klaus's great qualities is that its screenplay, despite being made up of a variety of clichés, knows how to fit its pieces together precisely and tell an intelligent, competent and moving story that starts with the exile of Jesper (Jason Schwartzman), the selfish and extremely spoiled son of a traditional family of postmasters, to run the post office of Smeerensburg (it has a deliberate extra "S" compared to the name of the real place, Smeerenburg), in the Arctic Circle, and establishes every detail of what we understand about Santa Claus today from his encounter with a mysterious, burly carpenter (J.K. Simmons) who lives in complete isolation. Since one of the charms of the story is seeing how each element of the jolly old man's legend is created, I won't say anything more about what happens, except that the film, unlike many others out there, doesn't resort to the blatant device of magic, even though it is discreetly present as a lovely backdrop.

## [17] Globo Play  (1524 car., ~1.5 min)

The causes can be debated, as can whether the changes will be good for the networks, but what is not in question is the need for change. HBO, traditionally available only via cable or satellite, launched its own streaming service: HBO Max. In Brazil, the move toward streaming is also clear today. Curiously, each of the major Brazilian networks is acting in a different way.

Globo began experimenting with online video formats in nineteen ninety-eight, during the World Cup in France, when it made match highlights available. That was even before Globo.com, created in two thousand. Three years later the company would launch Globo Media Center, responsible for the network's first regular online video product. Other initiatives followed in the years after, until, in twenty fifteen, four years after Netflix arrived in Brazil, the company launched Globo Play, a robust platform that has become the focus of its video-on-demand efforts. "We want to be with the audience whenever, wherever and however they want. That is what has guided our strategy for producing and distributing content across all platforms," says Carlos Henrique Schröder, Globo's director general. Globo Play's business model is what is known as freemium, a blend of the words free and premium, partly free, partly paid. Without paying, users can watch newscasts, variety shows, clips of telenovelas, sports programs and the live broadcast. To watch full telenovela episodes, comedy shows and series, they have to pay fifteen point ninety reais a month.

## [18] Part 10  (1785 car., ~1.8 min)

In Globo's strategy, the streaming platform doesn't need to mirror the broadcast TV schedule exactly. The bet is that the audience willing to pay to watch a series before it airs is quite significant. TV market analysts who follow Globo closely bet that the network will use its series to compete with Netflix. Those who share this view argue that Globo doesn't want the younger, hipper part of its audience to start associating quality series with its competitor's brand. For now, Globo Play's audience is mostly upper and upper-middle class, and predominantly female. The goal is to expand the subscriber base and try to replicate a phenomenon already seen in the United States. Recent research shows that the ability to watch shows that have already aired does not reduce traditional TV viewership. For part of the audience, being able to watch the previous day's episode works as an incentive to return to the regular schedule. In these cases, streaming is not a substitute. It is a complement.

Even if the positive forecasts for the networks turn out to be fanciful, Globo should still be in a comfortable position. Its situation is almost unique in the world. First, because of its gigantic audience. On broadcast TV, it reaches ninety-eight percent of households. About one hundred million people watch the channel every day. Last year, the thirty highest ratings all belonged to Globo. Anyone who wants to grow in the Brazilian market will need to invest in existing Portuguese-language programming or make new shows. To do so, they will have to hire talent, much of it under exclusive contracts with Globo. "There is not the slightest doubt that Globo is very well positioned," says John Rose, head of the Boston Consulting Group office in New York and an expert in the sector.
