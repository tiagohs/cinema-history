## [01] Disney  (1159 car., ~1.2 min)

In December twenty nineteen, an article published by The Verge announced that, with the release of Star Wars: The Rise of Skywalker, from twenty nineteen, Disney had produced eighty percent of the year's highest-grossing films. The general reaction was, understandably, quite negative. But this news was not particularly surprising, since it was just the latest example of Disney as the ultimate media giant and possibly the holder of a huge monopoly.

This percentage reflects the fact that Disney produced eight of the ten highest-grossing films of twenty nineteen, a feat secured with Star Wars: The Rise of Skywalker entering the year's top ten at the box office. The seven other films included a mix of Marvel movies, Avengers: Endgame, from twenty nineteen, Captain Marvel, from twenty nineteen, Spider-Man: Far From Home, from twenty nineteen, animated films, Toy Story four, from twenty nineteen, Frozen Two, from twenty nineteen, and live-action remakes, The Lion King, from twenty nineteen, Aladdin, from twenty nineteen. The only films on the list not released by the studio were Joker, from twenty nineteen, and It Chapter Two, from twenty nineteen.

The challenge facing Star Wars: The Force Awakens was extreme: rejuvenating the nearly forty-year-old Star Wars franchise, introducing it to a new generation without forgetting the mature fans who keep it one of the biggest and most profitable ever. But J.J. Abrams (born in nineteen sixty-six) and the Lucasfilm team made the right choices for this repositioning. Joined by the screenwriter of The Empire Strikes Back, from nineteen eighty, Lawrence Kasdan (born in nineteen forty-nine), the director starts from the essential structure established in Star Wars: Episode Four, A New Hope, from nineteen seventy-seven, the "Hero's Journey" of Joseph Campbell (nineteen oh-four to nineteen eighty-seven). Rey (Daisy Ridley) is this generation's Luke Skywalker: a young woman abandoned on a desert planet, patiently waiting for her moment. A droid carrying a secret, however, will cross her path, just as R2-D2 and C-3PO crossed Luke's, kicking off a space-opera adventure complete with masters, masked villains, family dilemmas, acceptance of one's own destiny... all following to the letter what was established in nineteen seventy-seven. Even the way the stormtroopers, Kylo Ren (in the position on the board once held by Darth Vader) and the First Order (the new Empire) are introduced is identical.

What's new comes from the young characters. Rey is the heroine that the age of Katniss Everdeen demanded. Star Wars always had a strong female character at its center, Princess Leia (Carrie Fisher), but never gave her the lead role. Here, Rey is the figure around whom the plot unfolds. Her scenes resonate on several levels, providing an inclusion that geek culture, so far-reaching today and in need of direction because of prejudice, desperately needed. And Daisy Ridley may be the best actress ever to pass through the saga, since the excellent Natalie Portman was underused under George Lucas's direction, which showed little interest in acting. Daisy simply owns the screen.

The same can be said of John Boyega. A Black actor in the spotlight, delivering a mix of panic and comic relief that he transforms into intense determination. He is the ideal partner for Daisy. On the other side of the coin, Adam Driver has the hardest role in the film. His Kylo Ren is a tragic figure, but one with little room to be properly explored, since he barely appears without his helmet. It is easy to mistake his still-developing villain for a poorly developed character. There is potential there, and a lot to be explored in the next films.

The classic cast is mostly represented by Han Solo (Harrison Ford), who cracks jokes aimed at the fans and serves as the mentor/explainer and father figure that Obi-Wan Kenobi (Alec Guinness) was in the original. He, Chewbacca and the Millennium Falcon establish the connection to the past that fans were hoping for and provide the desired (and far from gratuitous) fan service that honors the devotion of the followers of Lucas's creation.

The film is not without problems, however. And the most serious of them also stem from this need for repetition. In the original Star Wars and its sequels, there are a number of very hasty and overly naive solutions to problems much bigger than the protagonists. Here it is no different. When the New Republic's resistance discovers the existence of a weapon capable of making the Death Star pale in comparison, it takes just five minutes and the opinion of a sanitation worker to figure out exactly how to destroy it. Absent-minded engineers continue to be the Space Reich's big problem, apparently.

In addition, there is no great weight to the actions of the First Order, an organization that, despite following in the Empire's footsteps, appears without much explanation, something that must have been intended to avoid alienating potential new fans too much. Colossal devastation seems commonplace and doesn't carry the same weight as in the original. Death is always somewhat rushed in The Force Awakens, and one misses Sir Alec Guinness poetically explaining what it is like to feel "a great disturbance in the Force, as if millions of voices suddenly cried out in terror and were suddenly silenced". The Force, that fundamental component of Star Wars, has definitely awakened, but it is still rubbing its eyes. The Star Wars universe, in turn, is more alive than ever in the spectacular mix of practical effects and computer graphics that J.J. Abrams made a point of achieving. The aged, worn-out future of the Original Trilogy reappears here even more covered in the patina of time. Little things, like the dents on a thermal detonator, bring a smile. So much so that sequences that lean too heavily on CGI (such as those involving the alien Maz Kanata) feel strange. Credit goes to the production team, who restored the tangible quality on which Star Wars was built.

Problems aside, Star Wars: The Force Awakens is the Star Wars we had been waiting for since Return of the Jedi. A film that honors the past, takes a deep breath to deal with the present and lays a solid foundation for what is to come... inspiring longtime fans and, we hope, a new generation alike.

## [05] Disney Renaissance  (1919 car., ~1.9 min)

More than the number of films in the top ten, the House of Mouse stands out in the total sum of worldwide box office. Avengers: Endgame grossed almost sixty percent more than the second highest-grossing film, which was also a Disney film, and more than four times as much as It Chapter Two. So, when we look at total box-office revenue, Disney actually captured almost ninety percent of the gross revenue among the top films.

Although twenty nineteen was a big year for Disney, the studio has always had a solid share of the highest-grossing films, and its dominance has only increased over the last decade. In fact, the twenty tens really were the decade of Disney. Of the fifty highest-grossing films released between twenty ten and twenty nineteen, Disney produced fifty-six percent (twenty-eight) and accounted for sixty-two percent of the US box office. By comparison, the studio with the second-largest share, Warner Bros., produced eighteen percent of the top fifty films of the twenty tens and accounted for fifteen percent of the American box office. The fact that several major studios don't even come close to Disney's power is quite remarkable and potentially alarming.

It wasn't always like this. In previous decades, Disney had on average only fifteen percent of the highest-grossing films, which was much more comparable to other major studios such as Warner Bros. and Universal. In fact, before the twenty tens, Disney was the leading studio only in the nineteen nineties, a period known as the Disney Renaissance, explained here in previous chapters. And even then, during those years of expansion with classics such as The Lion King, from nineteen ninety-four, Toy Story, from nineteen ninety-five, and Beauty and the Beast, from nineteen ninety-one, Disney captured only twenty-five percent of the box office.

## [06] Pixar  (1417 car., ~1.4 min)

Disney's latest acquisitions reflect this success. Until then, Pixar had always stood out in the world of animation, releasing quality animated films year after year, which did not change after its purchase by Disney. But two acquisitions changed the game, and a third will perhaps have consequences in the twenty twenties: the two thousand nine announcement of the purchase of Marvel Entertainment for four billion dollars and, in twenty twelve, the purchase of Lucasfilm. The third acquisition, completed in twenty nineteen, was of Twenty-first Century Fox, for seventy-one billion dollars. Today the Marvel Cinematic Universe and Star Wars are among the most profitable franchises in history.

In the nineteen forties, after years of anticompetitive behavior by the major film studios, the United States Supreme Court decided to break up the "old Hollywood system", since it limited consumer choice and, in turn, stifled the industry's creativity. Although Disney's current dilemma is structurally very different from the cases of the nineteen forties, the implications and harmful effects for the film industry are quite similar.

In a world captivated by gigantic superhero epics and remakes of big hits, there is a fear that there will be less and less room for novelty, strangeness and original filmmaking, especially since original film concepts are becoming increasingly risky in the era of streaming and piracy.
