## [01] Golden Globes  (1741 car., ~1.7 min)

In twenty fourteen, Steve McQueen (born in nineteen sixty-nine) made history as the first Black person to lift the Best Picture statuette, for his work on twelve Years a Slave, from twenty thirteen. In twenty eighteen, Jordan Peele (born in nineteen seventy-nine) became the first Black person to win the Oscar for Best Original Screenplay, for Get Out, from twenty seventeen. In two thousand two, Halle Berry (born in nineteen sixty-six) became the first Black actress to take home the Oscar for Best Actress, for Monster's Ball, from two thousand one.

Practically all of these milestones are, above all, unmatched to this day. Even though they reached them only after the turn of the new millennium, Jordan and Halle, for example, remain to this day the first and only ones in their categories, and that means a lot.

Among those who argue that the major American awards (such as the Golden Globes and the Oscars) bear no trace of veiled racism disguised as false preciousness, nor almost completely ignore diversity in Hollywood, the wins mentioned are always used as devices in a defensive argument. The big issue is that the rarity of these events, when compared to wins by white people, for example, only reinforces the problem.

In the first thirty years of the Oscars, only four Black professionals had been nominated. And that includes not only actors, but also directors, screenwriters, producers, cinematographers, costume designers, makeup artists... the list is huge. It took more than eighty years until, finally, in twenty sixteen, the #OscarsSoWhite movement emerged and brought the issue to open discussion across the most varied media outlets.

## [02] Learn more: Sexuality and Identity (1)  (2105 car., ~2.1 min)

Learn more: Sexuality and Identity.

Sexuality, ethnicity, social status, life chances, surrender to drugs, crime, prejudice, bullying. All of these urgent, current issues are richly and beautifully addressed in the delicate and deeply personal Moonlight, the second feature film by Barry Jenkins, who also wrote the screenplay based on a work by playwright Tarell Alvin McCraney. But perhaps, above all, what stays with the viewer when the credits start to roll is a profound sense of the search for identity, a universal theme toward which all the other aspects elegantly converge.

Told in three chapters, each covering a key moment in the life of Chiron, from his childhood to adulthood, the film weaves together socially relevant themes and invites us to reflect on our own formation, on our choices versus the social determinism that can keep us from truly choosing a path of our own, a true one, that starts mainly from within ourselves. While Chiron suffers all kinds of abuse, finding his only "role model" in a drug dealer who takes him in when he is very young, Jenkins, unhurried, stitches together an engaging and deeply rich narrative that deals with the most diverse subjects in a fluid and realistic way, never resorting to didacticism or even pausing so that the viewer can fully fill in the "gaps" left by the two significant time jumps he makes without ceremony, swapping the actors who play Chiron in the process.

It is particularly fascinating how skillfully Jenkins introduces us to Chiron three times in a row without needing many words. The production design, notably the costumes, and the direction of the actors deserve special mention here, as they build perfect bridges from one moment to the next that let us navigate the protagonist's life fluidly and surely, without feeling any hiccup. In fact, it would be fairer to say that we don't feel any hiccup too strongly, because the work of the director and screenwriter here makes us long to know more about Chiron, that is, about every element we are frantically forced to try to fill in with our imagination between each time jump.

## [03] Learn more: Sexuality and Identity (2)  (1995 car., ~2.0 min)

But Jenkins never hands us what we want on a silver platter. No flashbacks. No lingering look at the past. The film strides forward exactly like life. If we blink, a year goes by. If we sleep, a decade goes by. And who we are becomes an amalgam of influences, good and bad, external and internal, conflicting and harmonious, over every day of our lives, lives that may very well be represented on several fronts by Chiron and his struggle to understand exactly who he is, who he should be and who he becomes.

However, there are no definitive answers, much less easy ones. What there is are questions, perhaps glimpses of what will be and what might have been, but never explanations or moments that take the viewer by the hand to lead them down a smoother path. In Moonlight, the goal is to present slices of the life of a young man who is, himself, a slice of society, at once reflecting the reality of many and miles away from the reality of so many others. But there is a convergence in the narrative glue of the search for identity, something that, time and again, at some point in life, affects everyone, even the most self-assured.

In the first third of the film, Chiron, or Little, is played by Alex Hibbert with a shyness and innocence that are truly impressive, befitting his small, fragile frame. The boy runs from bullies at school and ends up entering the life of crack dealer Juan, in a small but unforgettable turn by Mahershala Ali (born in nineteen seventy-four), who more than justifies his Oscar nomination for Best Supporting Actor by handling two opposites so perfectly: his hard street persona and the persona full of the affection he comes to feel for the lost boy. We see, in a succession of sequences interspersed with slight jumps in time, the two growing closer and the father figure Juan becomes for Little, even considering the obvious conflict with his mother Paula (Naomie Harris, also living up to her Oscar nomination), who is addicted to the same drugs Juan sells.

## [04] Learn more: Sexuality and Identity (3)  (2315 car., ~2.3 min)

Just as we are getting used to Little's drama, Jenkins makes his first time cut, taking us a few years into the future, with Chiron now actually called Chiron, already a teenager and played by Ashton Sanders. His daily problems with bullies and his increasingly addicted mother continue, but his sexuality begins to come more strongly into play, something only touched upon in the sequences with Little. From then on, the events are at once tender and frightening, and they contribute to our understanding of the final third, shorter and more direct, when we see Chiron as an adult, now simply called Black (Trevante Rhodes) and completely transformed physically. Here, perhaps in symbolic moments before the first day of the rest of his life, we see a beautiful movement by the character in search of a past he left behind but never forgot, closing the narrative circle in exemplary fashion.

The screenplay is built from the "stacking" of Chiron's experiences. With each new "incarnation" of the character, we understand exactly how he got to where he is and what each action means to him and to those around him. In less skillful hands, the story could easily have become tiresome, perhaps even repetitive, but Moonlight is precisely the opposite. Each segment brings information that we want and that we find all too little, always wanting to witness every minute of the young man's life and unconsciously transporting ourselves, as far as possible and based on each person's experiences, into that setting. It is life as it is, but in a poetic way, with a light, discreet layer of fable in the sense of the universality of feelings and actions.

And, of course, besides Ali, Harris and Janelle Monáe (as Teresa, Juan's girlfriend), who are already worth the price of admission, there is the Hibbert/Sanders/Rhodes trio, who should collectively compete for the Best Actor Oscar for their flawless portrayal of Chiron in his three phases. Even though the screenplay takes great care to make us believe in the transitions and changes of age, it is only through the work of the three that the intention becomes reality, even considering the most radical change, when the scrawny young man transforms into Black, an inevitable persona created by the conditions around him and naturally inspired by the young man's past.

The artistic care lavished on Moonlight helps the film reach a beautiful and rare status of realist poetry in motion that does not rely on transcendental approaches or contemplative cinematography to achieve its goal. Experienced cinematographer James Laxton (Yoga Hosers) takes care to keep the film with both feet on the ground, at the same time evoking an air that verges on a documentary atmosphere and plunging into hypnotic and somewhat ghostly colors in the night shots, notably those bathed in the moonlight of the title, which carries so much symbolism. The same can be said of the contribution of Nicholas Britell (The Big Short) with his absolutely captivating, classically inclined score, full of beautiful strings that Jenkins efficiently synchronizes with the picture so that it only underlines feelings rather than dictating them. The end result is, without fear of being wrong, one of the most beautiful scores of twenty sixteen.

Moonlight is an unforgettable intimate spectacle that will touch each viewer in a different way. If the film can be seen as being about the search for an identity, it never loses its own, striding toward an uncertain future, but one full of life, choices, impositions and, yes, love.

## [06] Selma  (1530 car., ~1.5 min)

And not without reason. A year earlier, in twenty fifteen, Selma, from twenty fourteen, was completely ignored at the awards, despite its top score from hundreds of critics. Ava DuVernay (born in nineteen seventy-two), who would later direct When They See Us, once again being snubbed by the Golden Globes, came nowhere near a Best Director nomination, and David Oyelowo (born in nineteen seventy-six), despite his highly praised performance, also received no nomination.

In the year the hashtags emerged, the situation was even worse. In twenty sixteen, the famous official photo of the Oscar nominees went viral for the absence of any Black people. And most importantly: contrary to some arguments that dismiss the lack of diversity, there was no shortage of opportunities. In the acting categories alone, for example, Idris Elba (born in nineteen seventy-two) was ignored for his role in Beasts of No Nation, from twenty fifteen, and Samuel L. Jackson (born in nineteen forty-eight) was left out for The Hateful Eight, from twenty fifteen.

At the time, George Clooney (born in nineteen sixty-one), one of the many supporters of the twenty sixteen boycott of the ceremony, raised what may be the most important point so far: the problem is rooted in the industry. While few leading roles are offered to Black people, many of them are still stuck in the same stereotypes. It's worth remembering, after all, that of the thirty-four Black women nominated for Best Actress, twenty were for roles as slaves or maids. Almost two thirds.

## [08] Learn more: Strength and Affection in Little Women (1)  (2290 car., ~2.3 min)

Learn more: Strength and Affection in Little Women.

Despite being present in six categories at the twenty twenty Oscars, the Best Picture nomination for Little Women took many people by surprise, since the film had not appeared at other awards. However, this is in no way a demerit nor does it diminish its quality, since the new adaptation of the novel written by Louisa May Alcott is an enchanting story of struggle and love, in every possible meaning of those words.

Directed by Greta Gerwig, the film essentially revolves around four sisters: Josephine "Jo" March (Saoirse Ronan), Margaret "Meg" March (Emma Watson), Elizabeth "Beth" March (Eliza Scanlen) and Amy March (Florence Pugh). Daughters of Marmee March (Laura Dern), they live their ordinary lives without great luxuries while the United States goes through the Civil War, in which their father (Bob Odenkirk) is one of the soldiers. While they wait for the return of the only man of the house, the young women, all connected to the artistic world in some way, must deal with Beth's illness, as she faces a disease she had overcome but which returned even stronger.

The events of the film unfold over a period of seven years, between the end of the war and the first years after it. To tell what happens over the years, the director opts for a narrative that constantly travels between present and past. Although this causes some confusion right at the start of the film, partly due to the excessive use of cuts, which makes the direction present too much information to the audience, Gerwig soon finds her footing and turns this shifting timeline into one of the main strengths of her work.

To mark the past, when the family, though anxious about the father's presence in the war, lives mostly happy moments, with the girls full of adolescent energy and Beth healthy, the director chooses extremely vibrant colors both in the characters' costumes and throughout the mise-en-scène. In addition, saturation is pushed quite high, creating a strong impression of vivacity in every scene.

With the present, however, the opposite happens. The screen is taken over by cold colors and dark tones, as well as low saturation, bringing sadness and coldness to the film, which grow even stronger in the many scenes amid great amounts of snow.

## [09] Learn more: Strength and Affection in Little Women (2)  (2309 car., ~2.3 min)

These back-and-forths in time also resonate with the events of the story. For example, the relationship of Theodore "Laurie" Laurence (Timothée Chalamet) with the March sisters could easily be described this way. Being a charming, wealthy young man, Laurie, even though Jo is his great love, makes some advance, to a greater or lesser degree, toward all the March sisters except Beth.

It is also interesting to see how each artistic field influences the shaping of each character's personality. Take Ronan's character, for example. An avid reader, Jo March writes short stories and novels and dreams of becoming a great writer. With a strong personality, and even called a boy after a certain incident, she goes to New York with the goal of getting her writing published. However, she runs into the blatant sexism of the time, in which nothing she writes is good enough, since her stories have strong female characters as protagonists and "that doesn't sell" because "it's too heroic" and "it lacks romance". Despite these adversities, her questioning, unstoppable drive keeps her moving forward, showing the influence of books on her upbringing and her enormous determination.

Another extremely important element in the film is the score. Present throughout practically the entire running time, Alexandre Desplat's composition is essential to conveying all the emotion of the narrative. Sometimes building gradually and at other times bursting in with great fury, Desplat turns small moments into major events, fully justifying his nomination for Best Original Score.

It is impossible not to comment on the costumes, so important for a period film. Although absolutely beautiful, the garments created by Jacqueline Durran stand out above all for their colors. Working in perfect harmony with the color palette, the costumes help identify feelings and personalities, as when, after a sad event, we see Marmee March entirely in black except for a red scarf around her neck, marking not only her love for that person but also her loving nature.

Little Women is a visually enchanting work with a great narrative, which exposes the difficulties faced by women in the nineteenth century, raised with the sole purpose of marrying, without giving up on romance. A high-quality blend of struggle and love by Greta Gerwig.

## [11] Three Amigos  (1751 car., ~1.8 min)

Long before [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]], Alejandro González [[pron: Iñárritu|Ee-NYAR-ee-too]], Birdman or (The Unexpected Virtue of Ignorance), from twenty fourteen, The Revenant, from twenty fifteen, and Guillermo del Toro, The Shape of Water, from twenty seventeen, won Oscar statuettes for their work as directors, it was practically unthinkable to see anyone other than an American or a European come out on top as director, women, Black people, Asians and people of other ethnicities underrepresented in the media as a whole can attest to that, but let's leave that subject for another time. However, when the Three Amigos, as [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]], [[pron: Iñárritu|Ee-NYAR-ee-too]] and del Toro are known, built the bridge from Mexico City to Hollywood back in the two thousands, it soon became clear that the trio could reach heights and levels previously impossible and improbable.

Even though, among Latin American countries, Mexico has always had the strongest presence at the ceremony of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences of the United States, it took a while for Mexicans to find their first chances of true relevance, from the perspective of popular taste, to shine at the prestigious ceremony, artists from the country had previously been nominated as art directors, such as the acclaimed Emile Kuri (nineteen oh-seven to two thousand) and Edward Carrere (nineteen oh-six to nineteen eighty-four). It was only at the twenty-fifth edition of the awards, from nineteen fifty-three, that a triumph came in one of the Oscars' main categories: Anthony Quinn (nineteen fifteen to two thousand one) as Best Supporting Actor for Viva Zapata!, from nineteen fifty-two, in a win over Hollywood's own Richard Burton (nineteen twenty-five to nineteen eighty-four) and Jack Palance (nineteen nineteen to two thousand six).

## [12] Wild Is the Wind  (1547 car., ~1.6 min)

The success of the everlasting Zorba the Greek, the role that would earn Quinn his second Best Actor Oscar nomination, in nineteen sixty-five, after Wild Is the Wind, from nineteen fifty-seven, placed him alongside José Ferrer (nineteen twelve to nineteen ninety-two), an actor of Puerto Rican origin, the two of them being the only Latinos up to that point to take home at least one golden statuette: Quinn would double his feat with a supporting actor win for Lust for Life, from nineteen fifty-six, while Ferrer won his trophy in nineteen fifty-one, for Cyrano de Bergerac, from nineteen fifty. On the women's side, Katy Jurado (nineteen twenty-four to two thousand two), a star of the golden age of Mexican cinema between the nineteen forties and nineteen fifties, also received a supporting actress Oscar nomination, for Broken Lance, from nineteen fifty-four.

But all these brief, albeit important, achievements concerned Mexico as seen by the cinema of the United States, a situation that changed when the first two Latin American titles, originating from the southernmost part of the North American continent, were nominated for Best Foreign Language Film: we're talking about Macario, from nineteen sixty, and Ánimas Trujano (The Important Man, nineteen sixty-one), classics of Mexican cinema that made Mexico join the usual powerhouses France, Italy, Japan and Sweden in the annual races for the gold medal for non-English-language features. And both got there by going beyond the traditional melodrama of Mexican productions of the time.

## [13] The Given Word  (1615 car., ~1.6 min)

There were two main influences, even if indirect, on these nominees, who would soon be followed in the nineteen sixties by Brazil's own The Given Word, from nineteen sixty-two, and the also Mexican Tlayucan, from nineteen sixty-two. First, the film movements that emerged in France and Italy, which took the seventh art out of the studios to bring it closer to the reality, particularly the social reality, of the post-World War Two world; and second, a singular figure, of Spanish origin but based in Mexico between nineteen forty-six and nineteen sixty after numerous political persecutions: the director and screenwriter Luis [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]] (nineteen hundred to nineteen eighty-three), one of the masters of cinema, best known for masterpieces such as El ángel exterminador (The Exterminating Angel, nineteen sixty-two) and Belle de jour, from nineteen sixty-seven.

The filmmaker, who despite having shot French and Spanish works in the sixties and seventies, for which he was twice nominated for the Oscar for Best Screenplay, chose Mexico as his home until his death. There, he had the opportunity not only to expand his own art but also to influence local production. Exploring more realistic themes, albeit through his surrealist lens, [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]] was, alongside his former friend, the painter Salvador Dalí (nineteen oh-four to nineteen eighty-nine), one of the forerunners of that artistic movement, the director brought the streets and greater psychological depth to Mexican filmmaking, which until then had concentrated on melodramas, comedies, musicals, and noir and horror features. Low-budget productions, in short.

## [14] Pedro Almodóvar  (1574 car., ~1.6 min)

Questioning the power of the Catholic Church in his works, promoting female leads within a sexist, macho culture and, above all, offering pointed analyses of Mexico's social inequalities in his anti-bourgeois films, [[pron: Buñuel|Boon-yoo-ell]] helped cement Mexico's position on the world film map and also became a point of reference for filmmakers of later generations. And although the extensions of the Spaniard's art are more visible in the cinema of filmmakers such as Pedro [[pron: Almodóvar|Al-mo-DOH-var]] (born in nineteen forty-nine) and Miguel Gomes (born in nineteen seventy-two), the concern with reality, the allegorical character and the constant mixing of truth and fantasy are present in the works of the Three Amigos.

Mexican participation at the Oscars, and Latin American participation as a whole, however, went into decline after these early successes. Mexico and Latin America as a whole would only return in full force around the nineteen nineties, the decade in which, not by chance, [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]], del Toro and [[pron: Iñárritu|Ee-NYAR-ee-too]] began shooting their feature films. The comeback would finally come in the race for the Best Cinematography statuette, thirty years after the original nomination of Gabriel Figueroa (nineteen oh-seven to nineteen ninety-seven) for the visuals of The Night of the Iguana, from nineteen sixty-four, with none other than three-time winner Emmanuel Lubezki (born in nineteen sixty-four) in the running for the cinematography award: in nineteen ninety-six the artist received the first of his eight Oscar nominations for A Little Princess, from nineteen ninety-five, directed by [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]].

## [15] Rodrigo Prieto  (1626 car., ~1.6 min)

Chivo, the nickname by which Lubezki is best known in the industry, became a personal friend of the Roma filmmaker back in their teens, when the two attended the same classes at the film school of the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM), the country's most important university. They began their partnership back in the nineteen eighties, the same period in which del Toro, [[pron: Iñárritu|Ee-NYAR-ee-too]] and cinematographer Rodrigo Prieto (born in nineteen sixty-five), today a direct collaborator of Martin Scorsese (born in nineteen forty-two), also started shooting in the capital. Together, these artists forged a friendship and a professional relationship of mutual support at a time when Mexican cinema had been pushed aside by the telenovela boom.

This collaborative environment of creative support reached a first peak in two thousand seven, when [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]], del Toro and [[pron: Iñárritu|Ee-NYAR-ee-too]] received their first direct Oscar nominations, the first two in the Best Original Screenplay category for Children of Men, from two thousand six, and El laberinto del fauno (Pan's Labyrinth, two thousand six), and the last as director and producer of Babel, from two thousand six; but all three would lose. Despite these defeats, however, the Three Amigos, alongside other nominees from previous years such as Fernanda Montenegro (born in nineteen twenty-nine), Benicio del Toro (born in nineteen sixty-seven), Catalina Sandino Moreno (born in nineteen eighty-one), Salma Hayek (born in nineteen sixty-six) and director Fernando Meirelles (born in nineteen fifty-five), helped tear down the barriers that kept Latinos away from awards like the Oscars.

## [16] Part 10  (2011 car., ~2.0 min)

After all, artists from Latin America and their descendants have obviously always been part of Hollywood in one way or another, the American film industry being, of course, an offshoot of society as a whole. A brief historical, cultural and even political digression is in order here: during the twentieth century, above all, the wave of Latin American immigration to the US expanded at full steam, a runaway increase that generated an increasingly pointed response: Hispanophobia or Latinophobia, a sentiment that encompasses fear, aversion, hatred and discrimination, among other negative reactions, against Latin American peoples in the North American giant.

We mention this social phenomenon, recently intensified by the election of Donald Trump to the presidency of the United States, a Republican politician who makes no secret of his xenophobia, especially in his already infamous attempt to build a wall on the border with Mexico to keep Mexicans out, because it bears directly on the victories of [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]], [[pron: Iñárritu|Ee-NYAR-ee-too]] and del Toro. As deserved as they may be from a technical, artistic and cinematic standpoint, the Three Amigos' triumphs as directors at the Academy Awards (for Gravity, Birdman, The Revenant and The Shape of Water, respectively) are also a clear political statement by the institution amid the current state of affairs.

Although the Academy remains a mostly white and male organization, as described at the beginning of this analysis, the recent inclusion of greater diversity within the institution's ranks has been changing the landscape, even if only to a certain extent. These are gradual gains for minorities who, as author Ioan Grillo points out in his New York Times article on the Three Amigos' success at the Oscars, are gaining ground and showing, even if only through the wins of [[pron: Cuarón|Kwa-RONE]], del Toro and [[pron: Iñárritu|Ee-NYAR-ee-too]] for now, that their participation in Hollywood is a "reminder of how ethnic and cultural diversity can enrich the film industry"; and not only that industry, of course.

## [17] Part 11  (669 car., ~0.7 min)

Roma is, in short, one more stone in the building of the dynasty of Mexican directors at the Oscars, who not only established a quantitative hegemony but also created a platform that solidifies the Latino presence in the commercial seventh art of the United States. It is a tiny triumph when placed in the context of the nearly century-old Academy and the capitalist nature of Hollywood, but it is still a meaningful victory in promoting greater diversity and representation at a time when minorities have been suffering harsh setbacks. And the Mexican glory is also Latin American glory, which, we must not forget, though we almost always do, by extension is ours too.
