## [01] Armie Hammer  (1471 car., ~1.5 min)

From twenty ten to twenty nineteen, technology changed drastically, producing effects we still watch today. Realistic environments, convincing fake animals, actors being aged and even doubled, it seemed there was nothing CGI couldn't do. Who could have imagined that a biopic about Facebook co-founder Mark Zuckerberg could also be a CGI marvel? Armie Hammer (born in nineteen eighty-six) played the twins Cameron and Tyler Winklevoss.

Actors playing two twin characters is nothing new. Normally, when an actor plays two characters who interact, filmmakers use techniques such as split screens and over-the-shoulder shots, techniques this film did in fact use as well. But more than that, The Social Network, from twenty ten, by David Fincher (born in nineteen sixty-two), was the first to use face-swapping technology convincingly. During the scenes, actor Josh Pence (born in nineteen eighty-two) served as a double for Armie Hammer. Hammer's face was then transferred onto Pence's body in post-production. The two would switch who was Cameron and who was Tyler from scene to scene. These techniques paved the way for an entire film about doubles in Us, from twenty nineteen, and for Chris Evans (born in nineteen eighty-one) to fight himself in Avengers: Endgame, from twenty nineteen.

Remaking a classic film is a very difficult thing. There are countless parameters that need to be met and even surpassed. Rupert Wyatt (born in nineteen seventy-two), director of Rise of the Planet of the Apes, from twenty eleven, knew this and achieved a true feat that left everyone wanting more. Now in Dawn of the Planet of the Apes, from twenty fourteen, with another director, Matt Reeves (born in nineteen sixty-six) has in his hands a doubly complicated mission: to surpass his predecessor and, on top of that, to deliver an excellent sequel.

Picking up where the first film left off, humanity has been ravaged by the simian flu and chaos has set in. The world population has been nearly wiped out and very few survivors remain. There is no more government and the world has become a giant jungle. In the middle of the forest, Caesar (Andy Serkis), together with other apes he freed from captivity, has built something they can call home. They have grown as a family, as a people, and live democratically in a society led with justice and tolerance by Caesar. Communication is done through sign language, which is passed on to all the apes from a young age. This way, they have lived well and in secret for many years, without even sighting other humans. Blue Eyes (Nick Thurston), Caesar's son, goes out fishing in the morning with his friend Ash (Doc Shaw), and when they return they find an unpleasant surprise: a human. Both are quite scared, Blue Eyes calls for help and, at the slightest movement, the human shoots Ash. By then, Koba (Toby Kebbell) has already approached with reinforcements and is ready to attack the human, who is not alone either. The group believes the apes don't understand what they're saying, but Caesar appears to prove otherwise and send them running.

Mark Bomback (born in nineteen seventy-one), Rick Jaffa (born in nineteen fifty-six) and Amanda Silver (born in nineteen sixty-three) wrote an excellent screenplay that masterfully illustrates the behavioral similarities between apes and humans. A feeling common to most humans. And right in the middle of them are Malcolm and Caesar, who believe there is a way to coexist peacefully. Several issues are addressed, but the one that stands out most is the difficulty of accepting and understanding others with all their differences, especially when there are deeper problems. ## [05] Lola Visual Effects  (1605 car., ~1.6 min)

This feat also involved the face-replacement technique of Lola Visual Effects, which took what it learned on The Social Network and applied it to the Marvel Cinematic Universe. Before his transformation into Captain America, Steve Rogers is small and scrawny, unlike what actor Chris Evans actually was. In some scenes, actor Leander Deeny (born in nineteen eighty) also played the skinny Steve Rogers. In others, the VFX team replaced Deeny's entire head with Evans's head. But for most of these scenes, the visual effects team digitally shrank Evans. To do so, Lola Visual Effects invented a new shrinking method, which it called Steve slimming.

The artists at Lola Visual Effects said they learned a lot from this experience, highlighting the actor's performance without letting the CGI get in the way. That's a lesson they put into practice when de-aging Samuel L. Jackson (born in nineteen forty-eight) in Captain Marvel, from twenty nineteen.

Besides Chris, Captain America: Civil War, from twenty sixteen, featured Robert Downey Jr. (born in nineteen sixty-five) as a young Tony Stark. Lola Visual Effects once again went into action. Some de-aging cases involve a body double and others involve replacing the actor with a digital double. However, the new technology allowed Downey to perform the scene normally as himself. VFX artists used digital compositing to de-age the actor. That way, Downey could still act in the scene as usual and the VFX team wouldn't lose the actor's many nuances, a technological achievement that helped the team of The Irishman, from twenty nineteen, three years later.

## [06] Less Than Zero  (1292 car., ~1.3 min)

De-aging has become an essential process. The changes can be as big as lifting skin and as subtle as increasing the light reflected off the body. One of the biggest challenges in de-aging a well-known star is that audiences are familiar with how they looked when they were younger, especially with an actor like Downey, who has been in movies since he was a child. So the VFX team analyzed old footage of him, as in Less Than Zero, from nineteen eighty-seven, -, which helps explain why this young version of Tony Stark is so convincing.

But the de-aging technique wasn't invented this decade. One of its first uses came in X-Men: The Last Stand, from two thousand six. But looking at the results from back then to now, we can see how much the technology has evolved.

Besides de-aging, the technology can also age an actor. Hayley Atwell (born in nineteen eighty-two) grew older for a crucial scene in which she plays a much older Peggy Carter. Once again, we have Lola Visual Effects to thank for this transformation.

## [07] Weta Digital  (1314 car., ~1.3 min)

Each film is just an excuse for artists to build on the visual effects work done before and improve it. In an interview, the Lola artists said they believe they spent so much time perfecting the neck of an elderly Peggy Carter in Captain America: Civil War that they didn't have much time to work on the actress's face.

Five years later, Captain America would finally get to act his age in Avengers: Endgame, from twenty nineteen, as Chris Evans was aged using a mix of CGI, makeup and a body double. But this time, Evans wore neck prosthetics so the VFX artists could spend even more time perfecting his aged face.

Thanks to motion capture technology, actors could show their full range of emotions while being transformed into basically any kind of creature. Mo-cap, or motion capture, was perfected in the two thousands in The Lord of the Rings trilogy, King Kong, from two thousand five, and Avatar, from two thousand nine. The technique became commonplace during the twenty tens, reaching another level in the Planet of the Apes trilogy. Much of the work on all these films was done by the famous visual effects studio Weta Digital. By the time of Rise of the Planet of the Apes, from twenty eleven, cameras were capable of capturing the subtlest movements and details, which made the apes even more believable.

## [08] Learn more: The Irishman: Much More Than a Mob Movie (1)  (2398 car., ~2.4 min)

Learn more: The Irishman: Much More Than a Mob Movie.

The Irishman, from twenty nineteen, ended up becoming ("unintentionally", in the director's own words) Martin Scorsese's (born in nineteen forty-two) biggest film. A fiction that follows his Silence, from twenty sixteen, the film is based on a book with a long title (I Heard You Paint Houses: Frank "The Irishman" Sheeran and Closing the Case on Jimmy Hoffa), published in two thousand four and written by former prosecutor, investigator and defense attorney Charles Brandt, whose narrative in chronicle form, and it is very important to keep this genre in mind, tells us the story of Frank, a World War Two veteran who joins a union and, for more or less simple reasons, ends up becoming a hitman for the Mafia. As the work begins, the character is looking back on his life and his career in the world of organized crime, recounting his connection with the Bufalino family and the disappearance of a famous and admired labor leader who had been his friend for many years.

In this chronicle conceived by Steven Zaillian, who makes an excellent adaptation of the source material, we have a film that unfolds in two dramatic dimensions, each bringing a host of additional meanings to itself. In the first of them, the filmic universe, the saga has at its core three characters developed in the most classical way possible: hard men, children of a time with limits unrecognizable today, before whom we watch life go by and everything change in politics, business, family and... in the very bodies of these men, who at the end of their lives, from the height of their great power, this time we are not dealing with showy or locally important mobsters, as in Goodfellas, from nineteen ninety, for example. We are talking about the sober Mafia, political as well, mostly silent and powerful at the federal level, must deal with their own demons, seek inner peace and truly face the enemy promised since forever: death.

In The Irishman there is silence. The characters don't need to shout all the time. There's no need for music all the time. And Scorsese's direction is so sharp that he manages to build long sequences of tension for each block of the work solely through what we know of these characters, their behavior, what they are capable of and the implications of their actions at the most varied levels of society, all within an intimate frame.

Looking at the director's immense rigor in guiding the actors and the plot, and in the way he oversaw the editing, which here flows like a river, in one of Thelma Schoonmaker's most brilliant works, managing to make a three point five-hour film dynamic and hold our attention with the same intensity from beginning to end, which relativizes our perception of time, we arrive at the other dimension of the work, with The Irishman serving as Scorsese's own analytical look at his career, working with his friends and putting on screen the cinematic signal of an Era... or the end of a kind of film that today's mass audience, for various reasons, no longer wants, no longer appreciates, no longer sees the intrinsic value of. ## [13] Industrial Light & Magic  (1137 car., ~1.1 min)

Rise of the Planet of the Apes was also the first film to do motion capture on real locations, as opposed to what had been done until then, inside an empty studio. This development would later help them shoot in even more challenging places, such as the Vancouver rainforest in Dawn of the Planet of the Apes, from twenty fourteen, on hot coastal beaches or in the deep snow of War for the Planet of the Apes, from twenty seventeen.

But Caesar wasn't the only great character brought to life by Andy Serkis with motion capture in recent years. Serkis, also responsible for the character Gollum in The Lord of the Rings franchise, played the menacing Supreme Leader Snoke. He appeared as a hologram in Star Wars: Episode Seven, The Force Awakens, from twenty fifteen, and later in person in Star Wars: The Last Jedi, from twenty seventeen. According to Industrial Light and Magic creative director Ben Morris, they didn't yet have the tools needed to create a realistic Snoke in Star Wars: The Force Awakens. By the time Star Wars: The Last Jedi was released, they were able to add a lot of detail to the skin, such as age spots and veins.

## [14] Disney Research  (1848 car., ~1.9 min)

The visual effects team filmed Serkis with high-resolution cameras positioned at every conceivable angle to create a digital clone of him. As Serkis performed, they could automatically capture all of his facial movements, from how he smiled to how he frowned. They even studied elderly people to see how light realistically hit Snoke's skin. The technology developed by ILM in collaboration with Disney Research was used to capture many actors. One of the most prominent: Josh Brolin (born in nineteen sixty-eight) as Thanos. Look closely and you'll see every crease, every pore on the skin of the biggest villain in the Avengers franchise.

In the live-action remake The Jungle Book, from twenty sixteen, actor Neel Sethi (born in two thousand three) played Mowgli and was the only human character in the film. He performed on a completely empty soundstage, alongside puppets and creature performers who would then be digitally replaced by the CGI characters. The biggest breakthrough in this film: muscle movement. MPC Film developed new software to animate an animal's muscle structure. Besides the characters, the environments created for The Jungle Book were made entirely in a virtual environment using virtual reality techniques, allowing the filmmakers to step into these environments and place their characters however they wished.

All of MPC Film's work contributed directly to its work on The Lion King, from twenty nineteen. The VFX artists refined their already developed muscle and movement simulations and added more joints and bones under the skin, as a way to make the characters' movements more natural and believable.

## [15] Should you bring an actor back from the dead?  (1465 car., ~1.5 min)

Rogue One: A Star Wars Story, from twenty sixteen, brought back the Empire's sinister Grand Moff Tarkin. However, actor Peter Cushing died in nineteen ninety-four. So Industrial Light and Magic used CGI to bring him back to life. Another actor, Guy Henry (born in nineteen sixty), stood in for Cushing and did his best impression of the Tarkin character. A head-mounted camera rig captured his facial movements. Then ILM used that performance as a guide to digitally recreate Cushing. The whole process took about eighteen months to complete. Although Cushing's estate gave its approval, the decision was still controversial. Should you bring an actor back from the dead?

In Blade Runner twenty forty-nine, from twenty seventeen, the VFX wizards at MPC Film created a digital double of actress Sean Young (born in nineteen fifty-nine) so she could reprise her role as Rachael from Blade Runner, from nineteen eighty-two. Possibly the most convincing to date. To create the digital Rachael, they 3D-scanned Young and aged her appearance. As with the muscles and joints in The Jungle Book, the most important CGI here is what you don't see: the skull. To create a CGI skull, they combined the 3D scan with work from after Blade Runner. The focus on building this character's skull allowed the artists to build the character's aged features around it. In addition, they focused on the subtleties of Young's face and even on how makeup was done in the nineteen eighties.

## [16] Will Smith  (1394 car., ~1.4 min)

Digital doubles became so realistic that they would eventually allow Will Smith (born in nineteen sixty-eight) to act alongside a younger version of himself, while playing both parts, in Gemini Man, from twenty nineteen.

In Martin Scorsese's Mafia epic, Robert De Niro (born in nineteen forty-three), Al Pacino (born in nineteen forty) and Joe Pesci (born in nineteen forty-three) were de-aged to play Frank Sheeran, Jimmy Hoffa and Russell Bufalino at various points in their lives. There was no need for the actors to wear helmets, tennis balls or dots on their faces. The tracking marks were invisible. The scenes had to be captured with three cameras at once. Besides the center camera, the two additional witness cameras were infrared, which removed all shadows from a given scene. New software called Flux revealed much more detail in the actors' faces: how their faces are shaded and how light hits them.

For additional reference, the VFX team studied the cast's filmographies. This allowed the actors to focus on their performances and the visual effects artists to focus on the de-aging. But there are some things CGI still can't do: such as making an actor's movements look younger. So a posture coach came to the set to make sure the actors were moving according to their ages for a given scene. Perhaps de-aging an actor's movements will be a CGI breakthrough for the twenty twenties.
