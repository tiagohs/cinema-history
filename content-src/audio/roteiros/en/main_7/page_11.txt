## [01] In Tahrir Square: 18 Days of Egypt's Unfinished Revolution  (867 car., ~0.9 min)

The twenty tens were marked by political and social upheavals all over the world. A series of anti-government protests broke out in the Arab countries of North Africa and the Middle East. The movement began in late twenty ten in Tunisia and soon spread to other countries such as Egypt, Libya, Yemen, Syria and Bahrain. The documentary In Tahrir Square: eighteen Days of Egypt's Unfinished Revolution, from twenty twelve, released a few years later, shows this well.

In Japan, the accident at the Fukushima I nuclear plant, in March twenty eleven, was the largest nuclear disaster since Chernobyl. In Paris, Islamist terrorists stormed the headquarters of the satirical newspaper Charlie Hebdo in twenty fifteen and shot dead twelve people, including cartoonists, editors and police officers guarding the premises, one of the greatest attacks on democracy and freedom.

## [02] Learn more: A reflection on humanity in the face of death (1)  (2107 car., ~2.1 min)

Learn more: A reflection on humanity in the face of death.

Christopher Nolan (born in nineteen seventy) is one of the great filmmakers of his generation. In Warner Bros. he found a studio that believes in people's creative power and isn't afraid to give them carte blanche - or an almost blank check - so they can tell their stories. His new film, Interstellar, from twenty fourteen, arrives in theaters full of comparisons with one of the greatest classics of all time, two thousand one: A Space Odyssey, from nineteen sixty-eight, by Stanley Kubrick (nineteen twenty-eight to nineteen ninety-nine). Although the parallel is easy, it is unfair to both.

Interstellar is, indeed, science fiction of the purest kind, full of questions, exploring like few others not only science but also humanity and decidedly unscientific concepts such as faith and love. To create it, Nolan took the theories of physicist Kip Thorne that his brother Jonathan was scripting for Steven Spielberg (born in nineteen forty-six) to direct. After the director of E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial, from nineteen eighty-two, and Close Encounters of the Third Kind, from nineteen seventy-seven, stepped away from the project, Nolan used his current clout in Hollywood to get the rights and rewrite it - still in collaboration with his brother and Thorne.

In this future of uncertain date, Earth suffers from a great blight that has wiped out much of the planet's food, turning everything to dust. The hunger and suffering, heavily inspired by the Great Depression of the nineteen thirties, force NASA to operate in secret, and even the Moon missions are discredited in history books. "We can't justify spending billions of dollars here while people are starving," says Professor Brand (Michael Caine, once again acting under Nolan's lens). The one who is happy to learn that the space agency is still active is former pilot Cooper (Matthew McConaughey, continuing his streak of great roles), who has to choose between staying on the farm with his family on Earth or traveling into space in search of new planets where humanity can be rebuilt.

## [03] Learn more: A reflection on humanity in the face of death (2)  (2081 car., ~2.1 min)

Compulsively controlling, purist and meticulous, Nolan boasts of not having used chroma key during the shoot. The filmmaker and his crew studied old filming techniques and rear projection, shot on location and built as many sets as the budget allowed. Space scenes were projected onto huge screens while the cameras captured the emotions on the actors' faces. And it works! Interstellar has the best performances of any Nolan film - and it needed them, since it deals with themes such as abandonment, loneliness and hope.

And while Spielberg and Scorsese have already ventured into the world of digital and 3D cinema, Nolan declares to anyone who will listen that he prefers to make his features on film, preferably seventy millimeter, hence his fondness for IMAX. And how beautiful it looks! If the system's theaters used to be confined to museums and screenings were limited to films about dinosaurs, underwater explorations and, of course, space, Interstellar arrives proving once again that size does matter.

But don't go to the theater expecting the emotional roller coaster of a Gravity, from twenty thirteen. Interstellar is more contemplative and full of silences. While not denying its Kubrickian roots, Nolan points to two other heavy influences: the Spielberg of the seventies and eighties and The Right Stuff, from nineteen eighty-three, by Philip Kaufman (born in nineteen thirty-six). Nolan told Empire magazine that he wanted to capture the spirit of Close Encounters and Jaws and try it out for today's cinema. If it works in many respects, there is one in particular that ends up spoiling part of the experience: the director's habit of explaining more than necessary, putting into words what is already being shown on screen.

When he released two thousand one, Kubrick said he expected people to leave the theater confused, reflect, and come back to see it again. These questions are what one expects from a work of science fiction. And if two thousand one is the book that poses these questions, Interstellar is the teacher's edition, with all the answers included.

## [04] Zombieland  (2383 car., ~2.4 min)

In politics, there was a significant rise in nationalist right-wing political movements. One of the main milestones was the approval in twenty sixteen, in a popular referendum, of the United Kingdom's exit from the European Union. Known as Brexit, its supporters have economic and nationalist justifications, with an anti-immigration character. In other countries, right-wing and far-right parties and politicians came to power with similar rhetoric: Viktor Orbán in Hungary, from twenty ten, Donald Trump in the United States, from twenty sixteen, and Jair Bolsonaro in Brazil, from twenty eighteen.

This social and political instability, as in the past, was directly reflected in film. The post-apocalyptic genre gained strength both in theaters and on TV. The zombie genre got an extra boost with the release of the TV series The Walking Dead, marking the start of the decade as one of the best series on the subject. Cinema didn't lag behind: Zombieland, from two thousand nine, World War Z, from twenty thirteen, Busanhaeng (Train to Busan, twenty sixteen) and The Girl with All the Gifts, from twenty sixteen, were released.

At the same time, films like Oblivion, from twenty thirteen, Elysium, from twenty thirteen, Interstellar, from twenty fourteen, and Passengers, from twenty sixteen, suggested that it would be better to abandon this planet completely and start over in a distant solar system or on a luxury space station. Remakes of post-apocalyptic films also achieved considerable success, such as Mad Max: Fury Road, from twenty fifteen, Blade Runner twenty forty-nine, from twenty seventeen, and Dredd, from twenty twelve, - the latter treated unfairly at the box office, but acclaimed by critics.

Other genres managed to ride the post-apocalyptic wave: comedy gave us This Is the End, from twenty thirteen, and Seeking a Friend for the End of the World, from twenty twelve; teen adventures/dramas shielded audiences with the Maze Runner and The Hunger Games franchises; in horror, besides zombie films, interesting works emerged such as A Quiet Place, from twenty eighteen, and Bird Box, from twenty eighteen; and in science fiction, besides those already mentioned, we had Cloud Atlas, from twenty twelve, and Pacific Rim, from twenty thirteen.

## [05] Learn more: A zombie film about human weaknesses and greatness (1)  (1898 car., ~1.9 min)

Learn more: A zombie film about human weaknesses and greatness.

Train to Busan, from twenty sixteen, a film by South Korean director Sang-ho Yeon (born in nineteen seventy-eight), who also released an animated film with the same theme in twenty sixteen, called Seoul Station, is a work that should change the minds of many people who claim that "this living-dead thing has run its course." Well... director Sang-ho Yeon proves otherwise. The genre can still present us with very good features and, as we see here in Train to Busan, discuss the classic ingredients of this universe while at the same time manipulating, satirizing and mocking them, as the director does with all the rules established and used to exhaustion by Hollywood cinema since its first contamination, in White Zombie, from nineteen thirty-two, and since its victorious living-dead launch into popular taste, starting with Night of the Living Dead, from nineteen sixty-eight.

In this South Korean film, the viewer doesn't get a moment's rest. Sang-ho Yeon has an impressive command of direction in almost the entirety of the film (he only loses his way in a set of sequences at the end, when he takes a shortcut to American cliché and can't resist doing something that seems to visit World War Z), and even in the [apparently] slow way he starts the story, there is a kind of tension in the air, making it clear that something very bad is about to happen at any moment. With sparing use of the score, and with a simple, low orchestration, tailor-made to create a macabre sense of expectation, this atmosphere is quickly established. The protagonist is introduced and, right from the start, our immense dislike of him is triggered, something that the screenplay's push toward a more human side in the final stretch does not, however, remove from the terrible impression of an arrogant, proud and selfish man we have grown used to.

## [06] Learn more: A zombie film about human weaknesses and greatness (2)  (1654 car., ~1.6 min)

One of the most impressive paths is how the social critique that the zombie universe brings us gains ground in the film without any kind of demonizing device for the villain (living or living dead), and how ideas of excluding others out of fear or even bad faith can turn ordinary citizens into true segregating thugs. Little by little, ideas such as resistance to contamination (the desire not to lose one's identity) and community feeling are dissected through the characters' escape, with a train to the city of Busan as the central line, where Seok Woo is with his daughter Soo-an. The family conflict worked out in the opening (he is taking his daughter to see her mother in another city, despite his initial reluctance to meet his ex-wife) suddenly shifts into a neutral zone, and the fight for survival, sometimes at the expense of others' lives, begins.

The cinematography works miracles with shades of blue, green and yellow, the last two indicating contamination, something rotten, festering, to which the cities and the train are exposed. As more zombies appear, with their deliberately theatrical, somewhat comic movements, the escape becomes frantic, the editing makes the most of every kind of route or movement inside and outside the frame, the scene that uses one of the zombies' weaknesses, the difficulty of seeing in the dark, is superb!, and the direction manages to extract a lot from the claustrophobic setting of the train cars' interior, where most of the action takes place. The idea of a "journey through life" is fixed and becomes more desperate with each city the train reaches and the passengers realize has been contaminated.

## [07] Learn more: A zombie film about human weaknesses and greatness (3)  (1968 car., ~2.0 min)

In a catastrophic scenario of contamination and extermination, is it fair to say that most people lose all scruples and attachment to others? Does the fight for one's own life outweigh the defense of those close by? Does the logic of "better you than me" win out on such an occasion? These questions are answered step by step, as if the director wanted to make us watch everyone there descend into their own private hells, after being introduced to a hell on Earth. The duality between personal aspirations and the use or manipulation of authority to get what one wants are some of the immediate answers. The work makes it clear that no formula, even one that uses iconic characters to represent the recurring types in zombie films, is immutable, and that a good screenplay and direction can use things already crystallized in the History of Cinema to make audiences fear, think and, why not, laugh with a fresh breath for something already familiar.

The change in narrative rhythm, and also in the direction, which moves from tighter shots to increasingly open settings, narrowing them again at the ending, was not the director's best idea, as, like I've already pointed out, he moves closer to the model he so well subverted for almost two hours.

When the film reaches its final sequence, it's as if the filmmaker were laughing at the audience, making them think of two possible scenarios for the ending. These two possibilities torment us and will certainly spark good debates about which choice would be better. I think the dual game delivered by the screenplay here was the ideal way to arrive at a question mark about what happens "after that." The final cut comes at the right moment. It defines fates, but not the final outcome of the whole war, which is left to the audience's speculation.

As the credits roll, we realize we are breathing normally for the first time since the film began, and we find that we have just seen one of the best examples of the genre.
