## [01] Allan Dwan  (2143 car., ~2.1 min)

The Western has been one of Hollywood's staple products since the dawn of cinema. It is undoubtedly the most American of genres, but not always the most acclaimed. Perhaps because they could be produced quickly and on low budgets - the landscape was ready-made and real cowboys were willing to work as extras - Westerns were often dismissed as "pastime" entertainment.

In the silent era, pioneers such as Allan Dwan (eighteen eighty-five to nineteen eighty-one) and Raoul Walsh (eighteen eighty-seven to nineteen eighty) often turned out one film a week, from screenplay to final cut. In nineteen oh-nine, the Bison studio arrived in California; in nineteen ten, Broncho Billy Anderson became the first Western star. According to film historian Ed Buscombe's calculations, two hundred thirteen of the one thousand one American films made that year were Westerns. With the arrival of sound, the genre's popularity declined somewhat, although Western production fell only slightly until the nineteen sixties. Hollywood made more than eight hundred films in the genre in the nineteen fifties.

Stagecoach, from nineteen thirty-nine, by John Ford (eighteen ninety-four to nineteen seventy-three), marks the beginning of a period in which Westerns were directed by major filmmakers, such as Ford himself (returning to the genre after more than ten years away), Michael Curtiz (eighteen eighty-six to nineteen sixty-two), Fritz Lang (eighteen ninety to nineteen seventy-six) and Howard Hawks (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen seventy-seven), who created famous classics such as Red River, from nineteen forty-eight, and Rio Bravo, from nineteen fifty-nine.

[CITAÇÃO] The Searchers is a film you watch once, twice, and you find more meaning in it, you connect with it on other levels... It's so rich! It's so sumptuous!

said Martin Scorsese on The Searchers, nineteen fifty-six.

After World War Two, Hollywood's growing interest focused at first on directors such as Ford, and later on the genre itself; the Western came to be seen as part of American mythology, an imaginary battleground between outlaws and the defenders of order, between savagery and civilization.

## [02] The Assassination of Jesse James by the Coward Robert Ford  (976 car., ~1.0 min)

The Western is Hollywood's most easily recognizable genre. The James gang in The Assassination of Jesse James by the Coward Robert Ford, from two thousand seven, and the bandits in The Great Train Robbery, from nineteen oh-three, wear different hats, but their looks and attitudes clearly identify them as belonging to the same genre.

Westerns are almost always associated with a specific place and historical period: west of the Mississippi River, south of the forty-ninth parallel, north of the Rio Grande, and sometime between eighteen sixty-five (the end of the American Civil War) and eighteen ninety (when the census declared that the Frontier no longer existed).

All of this is a simplification. There are contemporary Westerns (Lonely Are the Brave, from nineteen sixty-two), Canadian Westerns (The Far Country, from nineteen fifty-four), Mexican Westerns (Vera Cruz, from nineteen fifty-four) and East Coast Westerns (Drums Along the Mohawk, from nineteen thirty-nine).

## [03] Outland  (791 car., ~0.8 min)

For some, the Western is a narrative that can be set in Texas, in Vietnam or in outer space: Outland, from nineteen eighty-one, transposes High Noon, from nineteen fifty-two, to one of Jupiter's moons. Even so, the set of historical events, characters and places remains at its core.

The Western both celebrates and rewrites the history of the United States. John Ford, the best-known director of Westerns, said that his portrayal of the gunfight at the O.K. Corral in Tombstone, Arizona, in My Darling Clementine, from nineteen forty-six, was drawn directly from the account given by Wyatt Earp himself. That story - like the stories of many other Westerns that claim to be based on true events - is debatable; it is said that Westerns are not so much about the West as about other Westerns.

## [04] Learn more: John Ford and Racism  (784 car., ~0.8 min)

Learn more: John Ford and Racism.

Ford became sympathetic to Native Americans and African Americans late in his career, but he always had to defend himself against accusations of racism. Ethan is not the only racist in The Searchers, from nineteen fifty-six. Laurie, desperate for Martin to give up his search for Debbie, is equally virulent: "Fetch what home? The leavings of Comanche bucks, sold time and again to the highest bidder, with savage brats of her own?" Being part Cherokee, Martin is apparently more tolerant of interracial mixing. But when his Native American wife (Beulah Archuletta) lies down beside him, he pushes her away. The sadness Martin expresses when she is found dead by the cavalry does not soften the cruelty that the character - and Ford - inflicts on her.

## [05] Black Patch  (1830 car., ~1.8 min)

Given the weight the myth of the Last Frontier carries in the American psyche, this kind of revisionism is to be expected. Sometimes the process itself becomes the subject of Westerns, whether in iconoclastic films such as Black Patch, from nineteen fifty-seven, by Allen H. Miner (nineteen seventeen to two thousand four), or Ford's own The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance, from nineteen sixty-two, famous for the line "This is the West, sir. When the legend becomes fact, print the legend".

Like Altman, Ford knew that the Western was a manufactured legend, but unlike him, Ford saw this in positive terms: only in The Searchers, from nineteen fifty-six, did he properly explore the dark side of the Western hero. In the late nineteen thirties and early nineteen forties, the dominant tone of Westerns was one of affirmation mixed with a certain nostalgia.

[CITAÇÃO] Shane is so excellent... If I were to make a list of the best American films, it would be on it.

said Woody Allen on Shane.

Jesse James, from nineteen thirty-nine, - and its sequel, Fritz Lang's The Return of Frank James, from nineteen forty, - portrays the famous outlaw brothers as defenders of rural values against the encroachment of Northern banks. In They Died with Their Boots On, from nineteen forty-one, Raoul Walsh creates a romanticized image of General Custer, and Buffalo Bill, from nineteen forty-four, by William Wellman (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen seventy-five), does the same.

Ford's My Darling Clementine shows Wyatt Earp (Henry Fonda) arriving in Tombstone and leaving behind a town where cattle rustlers and drunken Indians have been replaced by a church and a school. Shane, from nineteen fifty-three, offers a more self-critical version of the arrival of the gunfighter with a dark past who rids the town of villainy before returning to the wilderness.

## [06] Broken Arrow  (1243 car., ~1.2 min)

Less affirmative Westerns marked the nineteen forties and especially the nineteen fifties. Written by screenwriter Carl Foreman, who would soon be added to the FBI's blacklist. Other blacklisted writers who worked on Westerns include Albert Maltz and Ben Maddow, who wrote Broken Arrow, from nineteen fifty, and Johnny Guitar, from nineteen fifty-four, respectively. Broken Arrow is an early example of a more sympathetic portrayal of Native Americans, as also seen in Apache, from nineteen fifty-four.

Johnny Guitar (one of the French New Wave directors' favorite films) stars Sterling Hayden and Joan Crawford. Hayden named names before the House Un-American Activities Committee in nineteen fifty-one; a character in the film is forced to do the same.

The "psychological Western" became another hallmark of the postwar period. Pursued, from nineteen forty-seven, by Raoul Walsh, features a Robert Mitchum haunted by his past in a dark, noir-style landscape. Paul Newman is an unbalanced Billy the Kid in The Left Handed Gun, from nineteen fifty-eight, and in The Gunfighter, from nineteen fifty, Gregory Peck plays a man pursued by his own reputation as the "fastest gun", knowing that some ambitious young man will turn up to take his place.

## [07] Arthur Penn  (951 car., ~0.9 min)

The Hollywood Western survived into the nineteen sixties and beyond, driven by a new generation of directors who moved from television into the film industry, among them Robert Altman, Arthur Penn (nineteen twenty-two to twenty ten) and Sam Peckinpah (nineteen twenty-five to nineteen eighty-four). Peckinpah was particularly important for passionately revisiting some Western traditions, as in Ride the High Country, from nineteen sixty-two, and more violently in The Wild Bunch, from nineteen sixty-nine, and Pat Garrett and Billy the Kid, from nineteen seventy-three.

The genre even survived the box office failure of Heaven's Gate, from nineteen eighty. Hollywood took a break, but went on producing teen Westerns, Westerns with gay themes and, in two thousand seven, a remake of three:ten to Yuma, from nineteen fifty-seven, the kind of efficient combination of outlaw characters and landscape that became characteristic of nineteen fifties films.
