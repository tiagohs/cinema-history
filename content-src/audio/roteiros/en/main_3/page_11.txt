## [00] Opening  (198 car., ~0.2 min)

Part zero three: From nineteen forty to nineteen fifty-nine. Chapter eleven: The Cold War on Film.

The paranoia of American society after the war and during the Cold War gave cinema some great works.

## [01] Pickup on South Street  (1764 car., ~1.8 min)

Images of panic from another time: a man runs against the traffic, shouting: "They're after you! They're after all of us!" An old lady gives a nervous communist spy permission to kill her. A young woman kills her accomplice for the chance to open a metal box and unleash the hissing serpents of nuclear destruction.

These sequences come from three films of the mid-nineteen fifties: Pickup on South Street, from nineteen fifty-three, Kiss Me Deadly, from nineteen fifty-five, and Invasion of the Body Snatchers, from nineteen fifty-six, shown in American theaters during the country's most troubled era, capturing the psychological atmosphere of a nation in the age of the Cold War and McCarthyism. The arrival of the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) in Hollywood in September nineteen forty-seven left the studio heads trembling with fear. At a secret meeting at the Waldorf Astoria hotel in New York in November of that year, they adopted a policy of cooperation.

Often cited as the first Cold War film, The Iron Curtain, from nineteen forty-eight, directed by William Wellman (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen seventy-five), is a relatively sober story based on the real events of a spy ring in Canada. The political brutality present in many of the films that followed is reflected in titles such as The Red Menace, from nineteen forty-nine, directed by R.G. Springsteen (nineteen oh-four to nineteen eighty-nine), in which a war veteran is lured in by communist spies, or I Was a Communist for the FBI, from nineteen fifty-one, by Gordon Douglas (nineteen oh-seven to nineteen ninety-three), about an undercover agent infiltrating the Communist Party. With cheap budgets and no big stars, the studios' propaganda pieces seemed almost like an excuse.

## [02] The Woman on Pier 13  (1690 car., ~1.7 min)

The Woman on Pier thirteen, from nineteen forty-nine, whose original title was I Married a Communist until the studio decided to change it, directed by Robert Stevenson (nineteen oh-five to nineteen eighty-six), was offered by Howard Hughes to several directors as a loyalty test. The absurdities of the screenplay are offset by Nicholas Musuraca's (eighteen ninety-two to nineteen seventy-five) noir-style cinematography and by the cast headed by Robert Ryan (nineteen oh-nine to nineteen seventy-three).

John Wayne (nineteen oh-seven to nineteen seventy-nine) starred in Big Jim McLain, from nineteen fifty-two, directed by Edward Ludwig (eighteen ninety-eight to nineteen eighty-two), as a gruff investigator for the House Un-American Activities Committee who uncovers a nest of communists in Hawaii (the poster's tagline: "He's a defender of the U.S. on a trail full of traps that leads him to the ends of the earth!").

In the summer of nineteen forty-seven, newspapers reported that unidentified wreckage had been found in New Mexico, giving rise to the first wave of UFO paranoia. Alien invasion plots in science fiction films reflected Cold War anxieties. The Thing from Another World, from nineteen fifty-one, directed by Christian Nyby (nineteen thirteen to nineteen ninety-three) but credited to producer Howard Hawks, shows blood-drinking aliens unwittingly brought to a research station in the Arctic. Its final line, "Watch the skies, everywhere!", could be read as a warning to the nation.

## [03] It Came from Outer Space  (1792 car., ~1.8 min)

It Came from Outer Space, from nineteen fifty-three, is more ambiguous: the aliens are relatively benign, but they temporarily inhabit human bodies. Invasion of the Body Snatchers, from nineteen fifty-six, with its hordes of aliens imitating their human hosts, can be seen as a metaphor for the communist threat or for the frightening American conformity under McCarthyism.

Invaders from Mars, from nineteen fifty-three, was less ambiguous: a little boy watches his parents being turned into robotic-voiced zombies by evil green parasites (read "reds"); then the aliens are annihilated by the virtuous forces of the U.S. Army.

Jack Arnold's (nineteen sixteen to nineteen ninety-two) The Incredible Shrinking Man, from nineteen fifty-seven, is the most touching metaphor for mid-twentieth-century America's jitters. A Los Angeles businessman notices that he is beginning to shrink - an irreversible consequence of nuclear contamination. On his journey toward disappearance, he ends up living in a dollhouse that mimics his own suburban home and faces his final struggle for survival in the basement, a speck amid the debris of American life.

In the nineteen sixties, it seemed possible that some of the political ghosts might be exorcised. John Frankenheimer's (nineteen thirty to two thousand two) The Manchurian Candidate, from nineteen sixty-two, portrayed a McCarthy-like senator as a buffoon used to bring about the communist domination of the United States, and Emile de Antonio's (nineteen twenty to nineteen eighty-nine) Point of Order, from nineteen sixty-four, used television footage of the Army-McCarthy hearings to reveal the truth about McCarthyism. By then, the Cuban Missile Crisis and the Kennedy assassination meant that the fears of the nineteen fifties had taken another form.
