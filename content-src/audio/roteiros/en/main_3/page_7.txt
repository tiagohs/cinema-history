## [00] Opening  (420 car., ~0.4 min)

Part zero three: From nineteen forty to nineteen fifty-nine. Chapter seven: Postwar Hollywood.

The popularity Hollywood films enjoyed abroad during the war was expected to continue into the postwar years. But economic, cultural, social and political circumstances, along with the rise of a new form of entertainment - television - curbed the power and growth of one of the most profitable industries in the United States.

## [01] Song of the South  (1596 car., ~1.6 min)

Hollywood profited greatly from World War Two. Besides proving itself an efficient wartime propaganda machine, the major studios successfully exported their products to the Allied countries, where filmmaking budgets were limited by the war effort.

Hollywood still seemed healthy in the years immediately after the war: nineteen forty-six was the year of highest movie attendance, beating the record set in nineteen thirty-nine. The year's most successful film was Disney's Song of the South, from nineteen forty-six, but the year's supposedly most important release was The Best Years of Our Lives, from nineteen forty-six, by William Wyler (nineteen oh-two to nineteen eighty-one).

A drama about the return of three war veterans, the film addresses the complexity of the men's lives, the memories of combat that haunted them and the challenges their families and friends had to face. It ends on a relatively melancholy note, but there is no escaping the difficulties each of the characters will have to face, reflecting the sense of unease that pervaded much of the United States.

This ambivalence was present even in Frank Capra's (eighteen ninety-seven to nineteen ninety-one) postwar classic It's a Wonderful Life, from nineteen forty-six. The ending may show traces of the sentimentality that marred many of the director's films, but there is no doubt that a dark tone prevails throughout the film, especially in the intimidating performance of James Stewart (nineteen oh-eight to nineteen ninety-seven). After serving in the U.S. Air Force, Stewart had to be persuaded to return to the movies.

## [03] House Un-American Activities Committee  (1377 car., ~1.4 min)

Hollywood's shift toward darker territory had begun earlier, with the arrival of the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) in nineteen forty-seven. The committee was charged with investigating the infiltration of communist sympathizers among the film elite and, as a result, more than two hundred fifty members of the Hollywood community were barred from working in film, while many others were forced to name communists.

One of the most famous informers was Elia Kazan (nineteen oh-nine to two thousand three), whose film On the Waterfront, from nineteen fifty-four, seems like a response to his critics, with a mob informer (played by Marlon Brando) as its protagonist. Unlike Kazan, Ring Lardner Jr. (nineteen fifteen to two thousand), one of the many blacklisted screenwriters, cited for contempt during his nineteen forty-seven testimony, told the committee: "I could answer the question (...) but if I did, I would hate myself in the morning." Writer Lillian Hellman was more incisive. In nineteen forty-eight, she wrote: "Naturally, men frightened to make pictures about the American Negro, men who only in the last year allowed the word Jew to be spoken in a picture, who took more than ten years to make an anti-fascist picture, these are frightened men and you pick frightened men to frighten first. Judas goats, they lead the others to the slaughter for you".

## [04] My Son John  (2067 car., ~2.1 min)

Hollywood was terrified by the investigations, which cast a shadow over the industry. In a submissive reaction, several studios produced anticommunist films, among them My Son John, from nineteen fifty-two, and Pickup on South Street, from nineteen fifty-three. Even children's films, such as the adaptation The five thousand Fingers of Dr. T., from nineteen fifty-three, based on a book by Dr. Seuss, were considered to have communist connotations. The fad passed, but the damage caused by the investigations, along with the ever-growing hole in the studios' coffers, the impact of television and a more diverse audience less interested in what the studios had to offer, marked the end of the golden age of the Hollywood studios.

Although audiences packed movie theaters in the years immediately after the war, ticket sales began to decline in the late nineteen forties. During the war, people who stayed at home had more free time, and going to the movies was an efficient, fun and cheap way to keep busy.

But with the return of the veterans, people became busier with domestic life. The growth of the suburbs, the routine of looking for work and starting to support families were more important. In many cases, because of the postwar recession and the lack of jobs, a movie ticket was considered too expensive.

Television was a cheap form of entertainment. In nineteen forty, there were three thousand seven hundred eighty-five television sets in the United States. By nineteen sixty, nine out of ten homes had a television. After the initial investment, these sets gave families endless hours of entertainment. Recognizing that audiences still loved the movies, the television networks began to compete with the studios, creating series that copied Hollywood's most popular genres. Success came so quickly that smaller studios went bankrupt and the networks bought up their properties and used them to film their own mass entertainment products.

## [05] Learn more: Epics on the Screen  (1596 car., ~1.6 min)

Learn more: Epics on the Screen.

A remake of a silent epic released in nineteen twenty-five, Ben-Hur was part of a series of spectacular films that were huge box office hits in the nineteen fifties. After Quo Vadis, from nineteen fifty-one, The Robe, from nineteen fifty-three, and The Ten Commandments, from nineteen fifty-six, Ben-Hur was hailed as the greatest epic of all epics. But it is also remembered for a sarcastic quip ("Loved Ben. Hated Hur") as much as for its achievements. What prompted the criticism was that director William Wyler adopted the theory of Cecil B. DeMille (eighteen eighty-one to nineteen fifty-nine) that audiences of biblical films allowed themselves to enjoy orgies and violence only because, in the end, divine wrath fell upon the wicked.

A more sophisticated filmmaker than DeMille, Wyler felt that it was not enough to bring the temple down on the villains. So the strong, simple story of Judah Ben-Hur (Charlton Heston), the noble Jew betrayed and enslaved by his false Roman friend Messala (Stephen Boyd), alternates crowd-pleasing action scenes with "inspirational" encounters with Christ, which allow Heston to deal with religious matters. Subsequent sword-and-sandal epics, from Spartacus, from nineteen sixty, to Gladiator, from two thousand, toned down the preaching and turned up the violence, suggesting that a lesson had been learned. Still, while Heston and Boyd shine on their chariots, audiences can't help loving Ben-Hur. The film was the biggest commercial hit of the year and held the record for Oscars until Titanic, from nineteen ninety-seven.

## [06] Quo Vadis?  (700 car., ~0.7 min)

In response to this situation, the Hollywood studios began to explore areas where television could not compete. Thus epic films came into fashion for the first time since the silent era. Quo Vadis?, from nineteen fifty-one, The Robe, from nineteen fifty-three, and Salome, from nineteen fifty-three, The Ten Commandments, from nineteen fifty-six, Alexander the Great, from nineteen fifty-six, and Ben-Hur, from nineteen fifty-nine, offered audiences large-scale spectacle. CinemaScope was created and theaters were modified to accommodate bigger screens. Other gimmicks, from 3D cinema to sensory devices that sprayed scents at the audience or gave small shocks through the seats, were also employed.

## [07] Giant  (1057 car., ~1.1 min)

More traditional genres were combined to satisfy audiences by offering something new. Thus family dramas were blended with Western elements in Giant, from nineteen fifty-six, the portrait of a family in the Texas oil fields directed by George Stevens (nineteen oh-four to nineteen seventy-five). Douglas Sirk (nineteen hundred to nineteen eighty-seven) followed the same path. With his expressionist colors used to grand effect in Magnificent Obsession, from nineteen fifty-four.

All That Heaven Allows, from nineteen fifty-five, and Imitation of Life, from nineteen fifty-nine, and pushing the limits of what was permitted in terms of sexual content in the cinema of the time, his films offered a counterpoint to the mild tones of television dramas. But these fads attracted audiences only briefly, whereas TV series were able to secure viewers in the long run. Television quickly became the dominant form of entertainment for the American public. Hollywood's decline was hastened by legal changes to the studio system and by the work of foreign filmmakers.

## [08] Otto Preminger  (1434 car., ~1.4 min)

The nineteen forty-eight antitrust case - United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc., three hundred thirty-four US one hundred thirty-one - put an end to years of vertical integration, under which the studios could own the theaters where their films were shown. The ruling also restricted block booking and the fixing of ticket prices. The impact of the decision shook Hollywood, wrecking a system that had prevailed for three decades. The new rules did significant damage to the increasingly empty coffers of this film district.

On top of that, there was bad news from abroad. War-ravaged countries saw cinema as a way to rebuild national pride, and nations such as France, Italy and the United Kingdom began producing their own films and stemming the flood of imports from the United States by imposing tariffs and quotas on them.

On the other hand, the end of film censorship gave filmmakers more freedom of expression in the United States. In nineteen fifty-two, the Supreme Court overturned a nineteen fifteen ruling, finally granting films the protection of free speech guaranteed by the First Amendment. The law also allowed individuals to challenge decisions made by censorship boards, and that is what Otto Preminger (nineteen oh-five to nineteen eighty-six) did in nineteen fifty-three, when Kansas, Ohio and Maryland tried to ban his film The Moon Is Blue, from nineteen fifty-three, for the audacity of using the word virgin.

## [09] 12 Angry Men  (1162 car., ~1.2 min)

Questions of responsibility and social rights were also raised in twelve Angry Men, from nineteen fifty-seven, directed by Sidney Lumet (born in nineteen twenty-four). Besides being a tight drama with excellent performances, the film confronts prejudice and stirs the collective conscience three years before the start of a new era of social revolution.

The world and cinema were about to change. The rebellious teenagers of the nineteen fifties became the bikers of the nineteen sixties; the fun-loving hero of many screwball comedies, Cary Grant (nineteen oh-four to nineteen eighty-six), was fighting to save himself in North by Northwest, from nineteen fifty-nine; handsome Tony Curtis (nineteen twenty-five to twenty ten) subverted his image in nineteen fifty-seven by playing press agent Sidney Falco in the noir-tinged Sweet Smell of Success, from nineteen fifty-seven; American directors were beginning to be influenced by French directors; and, in reaction to social and political upheaval, a new era of cinematic violence and liberal productions was about to take shape. Hollywood's golden age was over, and cinema awaited its bold and uncertain future.
