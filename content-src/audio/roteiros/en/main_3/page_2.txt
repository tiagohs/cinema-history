## [00] Opening  (263 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero three: From nineteen forty to nineteen fifty-nine. Chapter two: Asian Cinema.

Asian cinema, also known as "Eastern cinema", gained greater worldwide recognition in the nineteen fifties with the screening of Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon, from nineteen fifty.

## [01] Raja Harishchandra  (900 car., ~0.9 min)

Asia has been making films since the silent era. Japan was retelling stories from kabuki theater as early as the beginning of the twentieth century, while the first Indian film, Raja Harishchandra, from nineteen thirteen, dates from nineteen thirteen. Japanese cinema enjoyed its first golden age in the nineteen thirties, with directors such as Kenji Mizoguchi (eighteen ninety-eight to nineteen fifty-six), Yasujiro Ozu (nineteen oh-three to nineteen sixty-three) and Hiroshi Shimizu (nineteen oh-three to nineteen sixty-six), all making incredible films in an unmistakably Japanese style and idiom. In China, at the same time, films were being produced in the Shanghai film hub, where directors such as Yu Sun (nineteen hundred to nineteen ninety) opted for a realistic melodramatic style to examine the problems of the gigantic and fragile nation, beset by external threats and internal disunity.

## [02] Venice Film Festival  (2089 car., ~2.1 min)

Until the nineteen fifties, however, Asian cinema remained practically unknown in the West. A few Japanese films were shown in Europe and North America before World War Two, as were some Indian silent films directed by the German Franz Osten (eighteen seventy-six to nineteen fifty-six). But it was the screening of Akira Kurosawa's (nineteen ten to nineteen ninety-eight) Rashomon, from nineteen fifty, at the nineteen fifty-one Venice Film Festival, where it won the Golden Lion, that drew audiences around the world to the richness of Japanese cinema, starting a process that would lead to greater acclaim for Asian cinema in the West.

Kurosawa was a fitting ambassador for Japanese cinema. A liberal humanist, he dealt with seemingly universal themes, exploring the inaccessibility of truth in Rashomon and depicting a man's response to mortality in Ikiru, from nineteen fifty-two. An admirer of Western culture, he adapted the literature of his favorite authors for the screen, transplanting Dostoevsky to Hokkaido in Hakuchi (The Idiot, nineteen fifty-one) and blending Japanese and Jacobean theatrical traditions in his samurai Macbeth, Kumonosu Jo (Throne of Blood, nineteen fifty-seven).

Given this Western influence, it is no surprise that his work received so much acclaim abroad, nor that his admired samurai films, Shichinin no Samurai (Seven Samurai, nineteen fifty-four) and Yojimbo, from nineteen sixty-one, were remade as Westerns.

Kurosawa's triumph in Venice helped draw the world's attention to other Japanese directors, many of whom had been making films since the silent era. The serene films from the final phase of Kenji Mizoguchi's (eighteen ninety-eight to nineteen fifty-six) career, particularly Saikaku Ichidai Onna (The Life of Oharu, nineteen fifty-two), Ugetsu monogatari (Ugetsu, nineteen fifty-three) and Sanshô Dayû (Sansho the Bailiff, nineteen fifty-four), combine a profound humanism with extraordinary aesthetic beauty.

## [03] Yasujiro Ozu  (728 car., ~0.7 min)

Mizoguchi's use of the moving camera is unrivaled in delicacy and precision, but his style was never merely decorative. On the contrary, it always served to visually express the emotions and circumstances of his characters. By contrast, another master of postwar Japanese cinema, Yasujiro Ozu (nineteen oh-three to nineteen sixty-three), rarely moved the camera, opting for a quieter, more austere style for telling his subtle stories of the Japanese family. In films such as Banshun (Late Spring, nineteen forty-nine), Bakushu (Early Summer, nineteen fifty-one) and Tôkyô monogatari (Tokyo Story, nineteen fifty-three), Ozu explores the troubled relationships between parents and children with compassion and a resigned wisdom.

Akira Kurosawa is the best-known Japanese director in the world. Seven Samurai, a thrilling and humane epic, is his most enduringly popular masterpiece and also his most watched film. The electrifying, if less profound, Hollywood remake The Magnificent Seven, from nineteen sixty, is the most successful of the many Westerns based on Kurosawa's work - including The Outrage, from nineteen sixty-four, a reworking of Rashomon, from nineteen fifty, and the seminal spaghetti Western Per un pugno di dollari (A Fistful of Dollars, nineteen sixty-four), which Sergio Leone (nineteen twenty-nine to nineteen eighty-nine) lifted wholesale from Yojimbo, from nineteen sixty-one.

This enjoyable cultural exchange is delightful proof of cinema's universal language and appeal. Kurosawa drew inspiration from John Ford's Westerns and made something that boldly breaks with the limited traditions of the typical Japanese jidaigeki - a period film emphasizing sword fights in a medieval Japan portrayed as a fantasy land. Seven Samurai is packed with astonishing action scenes, comic incidents, misadventures, social drama, great character development and the conflict between duty and desire, all handled with impeccable attention to realism.

The film is light, nimble and economical, doing away with unnecessary explanations. It evokes mystery and sustains a mood of apprehension - with brief shots and quick cuts making up the peasants' search for potential protectors and their pleading of their case to Kambei. There are many scenes of overwhelming visual and emotional power - a dying woman dragging herself out of a burning mill and handing her baby to Kikuchiyo, who sits in the stream in shock, sobbing and crying out: "This baby is me. The same thing happened to me", as the blazing mill wheel turns behind him. Yet the film's finest moment is its ending: the three survivors gaze at their comrades' graves while the peasants below, oblivious, turn all their attention to the joyful ritual of planting rice.

## [05] Heinosuke Gosho  (953 car., ~0.9 min)

These great directors were just the best among many notable filmmakers working in the productive postwar Japanese film industry. They include Heinosuke Gosho (nineteen oh-two to nineteen eighty-one), a subtle and versatile director whose films capture an intriguing mix of moods; Kon Ichikawa (nineteen fifteen to two thousand eight), a virulent social critic whose best films savagely satirize postwar society; Keisuke Kinoshita (nineteen twelve to nineteen ninety-eight), director of subtle, sentimental melodramas, unusual in Japan; Mikio Naruse (nineteen oh-five to nineteen sixty-nine), director of moving stories of strong women going through hardship; Kozaburo Yoshimura (nineteen eleven to two thousand), director of restrained dramas, also focused on female characters; and Masaki Kobayashi (nineteen sixteen to nineteen ninety-six), the humanist director of the epic war trilogy The Human Condition (nineteen fifty-nine to nineteen sixty-one).

## [06] 500 films a year  (895 car., ~0.9 min)

In a more action-packed vein, filmmakers such as Hiroshi Inagaki (nineteen oh-five to nineteen eighty) made stylized samurai films that, at their best, combine carefully choreographed violence with criticism of the feudal values that kept Japan backward for many centuries.

This variety of content and depth went hand in hand with the films' popularity: at their peak, Japanese studios enjoyed steady commercial success and, by the end of the nineteen fifties, were producing around five hundred films a year. Only in the following decade, as Japanese audiences turned their attention to foreign films or began staying home to watch television, did the film industry enter a period of decline.

The success of postwar Japanese cinema was due in part to the country's political stability at the time. Films made in the same period in other Eastern countries suffered more from the impact of history.

## [07] Zheng Junli  (1947 car., ~1.9 min)

China, as an old Confucian saying predicted, was living through interesting times. Soon after the war, directors such as Zheng Junli (nineteen eleven to nineteen sixty-nine) made socially critical films and developed an innovative visual style. Junli directed Wuya yu maque (Crows and Sparrows, nineteen forty-nine) when Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government was about to fall and be taken over by the communists; the film was only released after the civil war.

Many of these directors were leftists, but ironically, several of them suffered after Comrade Mao came to power. The harsh criticism of Yu Sun's Wu Xun zhuan, from nineteen fifty, - which tells the story of the nineteenth-century cultural hero Wu Xun - ushered in a period of heavy state intervention in the Chinese film industry. Nationalization and strict rules on subject matter practically stamped out cinematic creativity in the nineteen fifties.

A hesitant liberalization at the end of the decade allowed some notable films to be made in the mid-nineteen sixties, perhaps the most famous of which was Xie Jin's (nineteen twenty-three to two thousand eight) melodrama Wutai jiemei (Two Stage Sisters, nineteen sixty-four). The Cultural Revolution of nineteen sixty-six, however, led to a temporary halt of all film production in China.

The development of Korean cinema was also inhibited by politics. The Korean War ended with the peninsula divided into two separate countries, ruled by regimes of the far left and the far right, both with strict censorship. Despite this, in the nineteen fifties South Korea produced some important films based on popular novels, such as Han Hyeong-mo's (nineteen seventeen to nineteen ninety-nine) Jayu buin (Madame Freedom, nineteen fifty-six), a story of adultery that became a major box office hit.

## [08] Apu Trilogy  (1630 car., ~1.6 min)

At the end of that same decade, several important South Korean filmmakers emerged, among them Yu Hyun-mok (nineteen twenty-five to two thousand nine), and in nineteen fifty-nine the country produced more than one hundred films. Between the end of the Syngman Rhee regime in nineteen sixty and the rise of Park Chung Hee's military dictatorship the following year, Yu managed to direct Obaltan (Aimless Bullet, nineteen sixty-one), a reflective and somber account of life in a poor village, considered by some critics the best Korean film of all time.

Farther west, the thriving Indian film industry began to win international admiration in the postwar period with the work of its greatest director, the Bengali Satyajit Ray (nineteen twenty-one to nineteen ninety-two), who drew attention with Pather Panchali, from nineteen fifty-five, and his next two films, which formed the Apu Trilogy. These films followed the life of a child from his rural origins through adolescence and adulthood, using personal experience as a microcosm of life in India.

Ray went on making incredible films in the following decades, such as Charulata (The Lonely Wife, nineteen sixty-four), a profound study of a married woman's loneliness. A more political engagement was visible in the work of another Bengali filmmaker, Ritwik Ghatak (nineteen twenty-five to nineteen seventy-six), whose naturalistic Nagarik (The Citizen, nineteen fifty-two) predated Pather Panchali but was not a commercial success. Later, he made his masterpiece, Meghe Dhaka Tara (The Cloud-Capped Star, nineteen sixty), about the tragic experience of a refugee woman in Calcutta.

## [09] Grand Prix at Cannes  (1767 car., ~1.8 min)

Ray's and Ghatak's contributions to Bengali-language films represent just one pole of Indian cinema. Another pole was represented by popular Hindi films, produced in the Bombay-based industry known as Bollywood, the maker of entertaining melodramatic films that mixed action, love stories and dance.

Subsequent emigration from the Indian subcontinent made Bollywood films known in the West, but in the nineteen fifties few of these films were distributed internationally. Raj Kapoor's (nineteen twenty-four to nineteen eighty-eight) Awaara, from nineteen fifty-one, however, was nominated for the Grand Prix at Cannes. Another filmmaker who directed notable films in a popular idiom was actor/director Guru Dutt (nineteen twenty-five to nineteen sixty-four), best known for Kaagaz ke Phool (Paper Flowers, nineteen fifty-nine), a tragic account of a filmmaker's professional and marital decline.

In Sri Lanka, Lester James Peries (born in nineteen nineteen), generally considered the country's finest filmmaker, debuted in nineteen fifty-six with Rekava. Sinhalese cinema, like Bollywood, was dominated by stylized melodrama, but Peries's film brought a new realism to it with the story of the punishments inflicted on a boy who believes he has magical powers. Peries expresses a subtle critique of the country's traditional beliefs. There are also active film industries in the Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, Pakistan, Iran and Central Asia.

Since World War Two, the cinema of these countries has remained little known internationally, but there may be gems waiting to be discovered. After all, few people in the West in nineteen forty-five imagined that the then-unknown Asian cinema could have directors of the stature of Kenji Mizoguchi or Satyajit Ray.
