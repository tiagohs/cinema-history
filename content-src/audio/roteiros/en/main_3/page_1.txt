## [00] Opening  (305 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero three: From nineteen forty to nineteen fifty-nine. Chapter one: Film Noir.

Emerging in the United States around the nineteen forties and fifties, one of cinema's most important "genres" is full of political subtext, corruption, crime plots and memorable archetypes, the femme fatale, for example.

## [01] Film noir  (2366 car., ~2.4 min)

Film noir is hard to define. Is it a genre, a style, a mood or a look? Does a noir film have to be shot in black and white? Does it need to be set in a city? Must it involve a violent crime? Does the ending have to be unhappy? Were true noir films produced only in the United States during a classic period, from nineteen forty-one to nineteen fifty-eight? Should films produced elsewhere or after that period be classified as neo-noir, retro-noir or even as a cheap copy of noir films?

Film historians have debated these issues for years without ever reaching a consensus. Some have broadened the definition of film noir even further. The ever-controversial British critic Raymond Durgnat included King Kong, from nineteen thirty-three, and two thousand one: A Space Odyssey, from nineteen sixty-eight, in his noir pantheon, which perhaps diluted the concept beyond all reason. But most critics accept that noir Westerns (Pursued, nineteen forty-seven), noir costume dramas (Reign of Terror, nineteen forty-nine) and even some British noir films (Night and the City, nineteen fifty - the quintessential noir title) can legitimately be included in the canon.

Deciding when Hollywood film noir began is also a problem. Its origins go back to the shadows, angles and paranoid world of German Expressionism, and refugees from Nazism such as Fritz Lang (eighteen ninety to nineteen seventy-six), Robert Siodmak (nineteen hundred to nineteen seventy-three) and Karl Freund (eighteen ninety to nineteen sixty-nine) certainly brought with them visual and thematic elements that fed into conventional Hollywood films and made them darker.

This generation of European filmmakers understood cinema's potential for creating complex psychological effects while exploring controversial themes - something already present in the work of directors such as Alfred Hitchcock (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty), who made his first film noir, Shadow of a Doubt, from nineteen forty-three. Fritz Lang's (eighteen ninety to nineteen seventy-six) M - Eine Stadt sucht einen Mörder (M, nineteen thirty-one), about a serial child killer, is one of the first crime films to combine the dark look and the typical plot of film noir.

## [02] Peter Lorre  (598 car., ~0.6 min)

The German thriller featured the quintessential noir actor, Peter Lorre (nineteen oh-four to nineteen sixty-four), in an incredibly frightening performance; it also had voice-over narration, a device that would become one of the genre's trademarks. Another Lang film, You Only Live Once, from nineteen thirty-seven, with a doomed young couple (Henry Fonda and Sylvia Sidney) fleeing fate and the law, also seems to anticipate aspects of film noir. Even so, neither of these two works conveys the sense of existential claustrophobia and mysterious duplicity that characterizes the typical noir film.

## [03] Learn more: Etymology  (945 car., ~0.9 min)

Learn more: Etymology.

The term was first applied to a film by French critic Nino Frank in nineteen forty-six, by analogy with the crime novels of the Série noire, a collection created by Gallimard in nineteen forty-five, whose books had black and yellow covers, with a black dust jacket and white borders. The term was unknown to directors and actors at the time the classic noir films were made, having been introduced later by film historians and critics. Many of the creators of films noirs later revealed that they had no idea, at the time, that they had given rise to a film subgenre. Noir derives from the suspense novels of the Great Depression era (many were adapted from crime novels of the period) and from the aesthetics of nineteen thirties horror films. The first noirs appeared in the early nineteen forties. Historically, they were shot in high-contrast black and white, under the influence of German Expressionist cinematography.

## [04] Stranger on the Third Floor  (770 car., ~0.8 min)

Another film sometimes considered Hollywood's first noir production is Stranger on the Third Floor, from nineteen forty, a complex and dark thriller, also starring European expatriate Peter Lorre. But it was just a sixty-four-minute B movie directed by the unknown Boris Ingster (nineteen oh-three to nineteen seventy-eight). Despite the cinematography of Nicholas Musuraca, one of the finest cinematographers of film noir, it had little influence at the time. The film most often championed as the one that kicked off the noir cycle in Hollywood is the crime film The Maltese Falcon, from nineteen forty-one, even though its producers had no intention of starting a trend, much less of launching one of the most analyzed, admired and influential cycles in film history.

## [05] Out of the Past  (891 car., ~0.9 min)

The elusiveness of the genre may have been caused, in part, by the fact that it was only called noir after the fact. The concept is a retrospective historical creation, similar to expressions such as "Middle Ages". Filmmakers who made Westerns, tough-guy films or biopics knew exactly what rules they were working with; few (if any) directors of noir films would have referred to their productions as a distinct genre.

The heroes of film noir are rarely nice guys. Cynical, ruthless, always looking for an angle and utterly unsentimental, they were typically lonely, insecure and disillusioned men, unsure of their own character and apathetic about the future. They were generally played by actors such as Humphrey Bogart and Robert Mitchum - star of the seminal work of film noir, Out of the Past, from nineteen forty-seven, - who were able to get the most out of their characters on screen.

## [06] Cry of the City  (1854 car., ~1.9 min)

In the world of noir, even if the hero is lucky enough to survive to the end of the story, he rarely gets the girl. The genre is characterized by a lack of trust between characters, by a loss of innocence, by a hostile urban setting (as in Cry of the City, nineteen forty-eight) and by a sense of despair - usually offset by a biting humor that keeps the audience on the verge of depression. Noir served as a reaction to the Hollywood musicals and romantic comedies of the time. While mainstream cinema prided itself on its spotless heroes and sugary heroines, film noir showcased a platoon of antiheroes and femmes fatales.

The classic period of Hollywood film noir coincides with the years of World War Two and the postwar anti-communist witch hunt. Noir films, with their intimidating atmosphere of fear and paranoia and their sense of fatalism, served as an indirect reaction to the political climate of the time. In many of them, one can detect a certain anxiety about the conflict ravaging Europe that would eventually draw in the United States - or, years later, about the claustrophobic climate of the Cold War, which stifled the whole country, as did the threat of nuclear annihilation.

John Huston (nineteen oh-six to nineteen eighty-seven) directed The Maltese Falcon before the United States entered the war; even so, it is possible to sense, hidden beneath the traditional portrayal of the private detective, a certain apprehension about events in Europe. The Third Man, from nineteen forty-nine, directed by Carol Reed (nineteen oh-six to nineteen seventy-six) and set and shot in postwar Vienna, delves into the chaos that followed World War Two and reflects the prevailing atmosphere of anxiety, pessimism and uncertainty.

## [07] Nicholas Musuraca  (1693 car., ~1.7 min)

Occupied Vienna is controlled by four different military powers, while a murky black market in drugs plagues the already devastated city. Urban landscapes often served as the setting for this kind of film. The dark, damp streets of the big cities created the perfect backdrop for the terrifying events and uneasy atmosphere of film noir. Among the favorite techniques of film noir are low-key chiaroscuro lighting, odd angles, flashbacks, first-person voice-over narration, fast and biting dialogue, and nonlinear narratives. The cinematographers of the time, among them Nicholas Musuraca (eighteen ninety-two to nineteen seventy-five), John F. Seitz (eighteen ninety-two to nineteen seventy-nine) and John Alton (nineteen oh-one to nineteen ninety-six), used expressionist lighting to create the ideal atmosphere and emphasize tension. Arthur Edeson (eighteen ninety-one to nineteen seventy) is considered the creator of classic noir cinematography.

He shot his first film in nineteen fourteen and went on to film several of Douglas Fairbanks's swashbucklers and one of the first sound films, All Quiet on the Western Front, from nineteen thirty. Also in the nineteen thirties, Edeson was responsible for the cinematography of some classic Universal horror films directed by James Whale (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen fifty-seven): Frankenstein, from nineteen thirty-one, The Old Dark House, from nineteen thirty-two, and The Invisible Man, from nineteen thirty-three, in which the combination of German Expressionism with a dark gothic atmosphere and overblown melodrama served to set the standard for horror films. In nineteen forty-one he worked as cinematographer on The Maltese Falcon.

## [08] Double Indemnity  (1488 car., ~1.5 min)

The plots and themes of film noir are rooted in the cheap pulp magazines and American crime novels of the time, whose tone was transferred to the dialogue of the screenplays. Double Indemnity, from nineteen forty-four, another noir classic, was adapted by director Billy Wilder (nineteen oh-six to two thousand two) and writer Raymond Chandler from a novel by James M. Cain. Chandler's writing inspired several important noir films, among them The Big Sleep, from nineteen forty-six.

During the nineteen forties and nineteen fifties, the tone of film noir grew even more intense. The dark areas became more melancholy and transgressive; the lights, bolder and more hysterical; and the framing, more vertiginous, while the sense of fatalism intensified and became even more internalized. "Fate, or some mysterious force," reflects the hero of Detour, from nineteen forty-five, "can put the finger on you or me for no good reason at all".

Other traumas of the period darkened the picture: anti-communist paranoia, postwar disorientation and a sense of emptiness. Crossfire, from nineteen forty-seven, points to the violent potential of war veterans; the specter of Nazism is present in Notorious, from nineteen forty-six; a nagging worry about wives and girlfriends falling prey to interlopers on the home front runs through The Blue Dahlia, from nineteen forty-six; and alcoholism, a growing problem in postwar America, nearly destroys the hero of The Lost Weekend, from nineteen forty-five.

## [09] Orson Welles  (1026 car., ~1.0 min)

Orson Welles's (nineteen fifteen to nineteen eighty-five) audacious Touch of Evil, from nineteen fifty-eight, marked the end of the American cycle of film noir. The film became famous for its uninterrupted three-minute opening take, for its final chase scene, with its extravagant visuals and experimental sound effects.

As the United States sank into Cold War neurosis, the gloomy, fatalistic vision of film noir seemed like a response to, and a disenchanted expression of, the optimism and patriotism found in most Hollywood productions. Those patriotic parades on the avenues of major cities had a bitter flip side in the mean streets: the brighter the spotlights and the louder the drums, the darker the shadows and the greater the emptiness the films echoed.

As film historian Colin McArthur put it: "The themes of film noir are not social; they do not refer to the problems (...) of a specific society; they are metaphysical and have to do with anguish and loneliness, which are essential elements of the human condition."
