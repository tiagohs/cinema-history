## [01] François Truffaut  (1403 car., ~1.4 min)

Few movements in cinema have been as influential as the French New Wave, or nouvelle vague. What began as a gathering of ambitious young critics working at a film magazine turned into a group of filmmakers whose work redefined modern cinema.

The movement's origins lie in the strong presence of two individuals: film archivist Henri Langlois and critic André Bazin. The latter was one of the founders, in nineteen fifty, of the magazine Cahiers du cinéma; Langlois was a co-founder of the Cinémathèque Française, a film archive that held regular screenings in Paris.

Many of the future members of the French New Wave knew Langlois personally and attended the Cinémathèque with almost religious fervor, often discussing the films they had seen into the early hours. Among the filmmakers who later wrote for Cahiers du cinéma were François [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] (nineteen thirty-two to nineteen eighty-four), Jean-Luc [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] (born in nineteen thirty), Jacques Rivette (born in nineteen twenty-eight), and Eric Rohmer (nineteen twenty to twenty ten).

The magazine scorned the conservative French cinema of the time and instead championed American directors and the development of an auteur theory. A director's recognizable signature - a personal stamp that defined a filmmaker's work - was seen as a sign of greatness. The Cahiers critics joined other regulars of the Cinémathèque in their desire to make their own films.

## [02] Learn more: Characteristics of French New Wave cinema  (1404 car., ~1.4 min)

Learn more: Characteristics of French New Wave cinema.

Emphasis on "auteur cinema": this "new wave" brought many innovations. The artists were interested in making auteur-driven cinema, in which the screenplay and the direction were truly valued, as was the acting. It was in this context that the expression "auteur cinema" was coined. Breaking narrative linearity: One of the elements the French New Wave subverts is linearity. There was no concern with telling the story in the chronological order of events, which led to a rupture in narrative structure. Emphasis on outdoor settings: Outdoor locations were also widely used. Natural light and everyday settings came to be valued, unlike what had been done until then, when scenes were shot in studios and controlled spaces. The filmmakers wanted to show the pulsing life of the streets, passersby, and everyday life as the backdrop for their stories. Everyday themes: The themes addressed raised common questions, day-to-day hardships, and mundane reflections on life. Because of this, acting was very important, with improvisation and spontaneity even being emphasized. In addition, other subjects such as romantic and sexual freedom and postwar pacification also appear frequently. Innovation in filmmaking: On the technical side, the French New Wave experimented with new framings and camera movements, and also innovated in the editing of scenes.

## [03] Le Beau Serge  (1524 car., ~1.5 min)

Though immature, the early films of [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]], Rivette, and Rohmer marked an aesthetic shift, moving the action from studios to real locations. This was made possible by technological advances, as cheaper high-quality portable cameras and faster film stocks were by then available, allowing access to almost any place and shooting without the aid of lighting.

The French New Wave officially began with Le Beau Serge, from nineteen fifty-eight, directed by Claude Chabrol (nineteen thirty to twenty ten), but it was [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]]'s The four hundred Blows, from nineteen fifty-nine, and [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]]'s Breathless, from nineteen sixty, that made the biggest waves. After being banned from the Cannes Film Festival in nineteen fifty-eight for his criticism of French cinema, [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] triumphed at the festival the following year. Partly autobiographical and shot in the neighborhood where the filmmaker grew up, The four hundred Blows stars Jean-Pierre Léaud as Antoine Doinel, a character whose life and loves the director would chronicle in five films over the next twenty years.

At once good-humored, playful, daring, and insolent, Breathless came to be seen as the film that defined the French New Wave. It was based on a story by [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] and displayed all the elements that underpinned the movement's style. [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] mixed cinematic and literary references, even going so far as to cast Jean-Pierre Melville (nineteen seventeen to nineteen seventy-three) - one of the French directors Cahiers du cinéma admired - as a character in the film.

## [04] Vivre sa vie: film en douze tableaux  (1005 car., ~1.0 min)

According to some sources, it was Melville who suggested that [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] cut the film so that scenes jumped straight into the action, creating the jump cut that disrupted the flow of conventional "invisible" editing. The story itself was disposable, as alluded to by a character in [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]]'s third film, Vivre sa vie: film en douze tableaux, from nineteen sixty-two, who says of a book she is reading: "the story is silly, but the book is very well written."

Le Signe du lion (Sign of the Lion, nineteen sixty-two), by Eric Rohmer (nineteen twenty to twenty ten), was received with less enthusiasm, but its shooting locations and literary references place it in the same group as his colleagues' films. Paris nous appartient (Paris Belongs to Us, nineteen sixty-one), by Jacques Rivette (nineteen twenty-eight to twenty sixteen), was the longest of the early New Wave films, but the way it explores Paris makes it one of the most beautiful, even if it lacks the bold sloppiness of [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]]'s and [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]]'s films.

## [05] Claude Chabrol  (1529 car., ~1.5 min)

Claude Chabrol, probably more than any other director of the French New Wave, was always fascinated by a single genre, and he found in it a gold mine for exploring human weakness. After his debut feature, Le Beau Serge, a simple portrait of French provincial life, The Cousins, from nineteen fifty-nine, served as the model for the films that followed it. That same year, À double tour (Web of Passion, nineteen fifty-nine) successfully recombined the movement's elements within a more classical narrative. Chabrol kept going down this path, building a distinctive body of work.

Among the films by other directors also considered part of the French New Wave are Cléo from five to seven, from nineteen sixty-two, which includes a cameo by [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] as a short film within a feature film, and the seductive Happiness, from nineteen sixty-five, whose portrait of patriarchal society and the silent suffering of a betrayed wife stands in stark contrast to more male-centered films.

Both were directed by Agnès Varda (born in nineteen twenty-eight), whose debut feature, La Pointe Courte, from nineteen fifty-five, blended a fictional story of an unhappy marriage with documentary scenes of a fishing village caught in an economic crisis. The film was a novelty for its time, both in its narrative structure and in the way it was made, and today it is recognized as a work of contemporary relevance by one of France's greatest filmmakers.

## [06] Alain Resnais  (811 car., ~0.8 min)

Alain Resnais (born in nineteen twenty-two) is another director whose early works align with the French New Wave. Last Year at Marienbad, from nineteen sixty-one, is arguably his most enigmatic film. The screenplay was written by Alain Robbe-Grillet (nineteen twenty-two to two thousand eight), a member of an avant-garde literary movement - the nouveau roman - whose creative process became intertwined with that of the French New Wave.

[[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]]'s Jules and Jim, from nineteen sixty-two, became one of the emblematic films of the French New Wave. The film, which tells of a love triangle set before, during, and after World War One, uses several techniques and elements that became trademarks of the new style, such as the incorporation of newsreel footage, freeze frames, dolly shots, and voice-over narration.

## [07] Jeanne Moreau  (1398 car., ~1.4 min)

It also features a classic score composed by Georges Delerue. In addition, it stars Jeanne Moreau (nineteen twenty-eight to twenty seventeen), a symbol of the French New Wave. Moreau also appears in The Lovers, from nineteen fifty-eight, directed by Louis Malle (nineteen thirty-two to nineteen ninety-five), which proposed an amoral and casual approach to sex - another metaphor present in several works of the French New Wave.

[[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] was the most prolific filmmaker of the movement and kept experimenting with genre, showing his love of literature, cinema, and philosophy in films such as A Woman Is a Woman, from nineteen sixty-one, and Band of Outsiders, from nineteen sixty-four. [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]]'s films, like so many others of the French New Wave, are full of references to American cinema - the unscrupulous characters of Breathless and Band of Outsiders, from nineteen sixty-four, are ardent fans of Hollywood B movies.

[CITAÇÃO] Don't use the brakes. Cars are made to go, not to stop!

said Michel Poiccard (Jean-Paul Belmondo) in Breathless.

[[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] went on to influence generations of younger filmmakers. When Quentin Tarantino (born in nineteen sixty-three) named his production company A Band Apart, he was paying homage to the legendary French director. Pierrot le Fou, from nineteen sixty-five, would mark the start of the overt politicization of [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]]'s work, which only grew over the course of the decade.

## [08] Part 7  (857 car., ~0.9 min)

Later, the close-knit confrérie of the French New Wave would be torn apart by infighting and quarrels. By the end of the nineteen sixties, [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] and [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] were barely speaking. But at the start of the movement, the members of the group generally collaborated and helped one another. The group did not consist only of directors. Among the actors strongly associated with the French New Wave are Jeanne Moreau, Anna Karina, Michel Bouquet, Jean-Paul Belmondo, Jean-Claude Brialy, Stéphane Audran and, of course, [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]]'s cinematic alter ego, Jean-Pierre Léaud. Behind the cameras was another group of frequent collaborators, such as cinematographers Henri Decaë, Sacha Vierny, Nicolas Hayer, Néstor Almendros, and Raoul Coutard, editors Lila Herman, Cécile Decugis, Claudine Bouché, and Agnès Guillemot, and composers Georges Delerue and Michel Legrand.
