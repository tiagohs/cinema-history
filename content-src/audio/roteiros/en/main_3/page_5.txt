## [01] Carl Theodor Dreyer  (592 car., ~0.6 min)

The nineteen fifties saw a revival of Nordic cinema, which had been at the forefront of filmmaking during the silent era. In the late nineteen tens and early nineteen twenties, the best films of Carl Theodor Dreyer (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen sixty-eight), Victor Sjöström (eighteen seventy-nine to nineteen sixty) and Mauritz Stiller (eighteen eighty-three to nineteen twenty-eight) achieved an almost unrivaled delicacy and naturalism. But with the arrival of sound and the dominance of Hollywood, the sparsely populated Nordic countries became linguistically and culturally isolated.

## [02] Ingmar Bergman  (1987 car., ~2.0 min)

By the late nineteen forties, however, Scandinavia - especially Sweden - was once again producing creative films. Film historian Peter Cowie stated that "probably no other country with a comparable population has achieved Sweden's artistic success in cinema". Key to this success were the photogenic beauty of its rural and urban landscapes and the contrast between austere Lutheran mores and the twentieth-century evolution of society into a secular social democracy.

These themes found expression in the nineteen fifties in the work of Ingmar Bergman (nineteen eighteen to two thousand seven). His finest early achievements, Sommarlek (Summer Interlude, nineteen fifty-one), above, and Sommaren med Monika (Summer with Monika, nineteen fifty-three), were melancholy romantic films set against the backdrop of the Stockholm archipelago and its wild, fleeting beauty, which mirrors the transience of love. They belong to a distinctly Swedish vein also exemplified by Hon dansade en sommar (One Summer of Happiness, nineteen fifty-one), by Arne Mattsson (nineteen nineteen to nineteen ninety-five), another tragic love story set against a rural backdrop.

Bergman then moved into a more allegorical and existential phase with Det sjunde inseglet (The Seventh Seal, nineteen fifty-seven). Despite its medieval setting and fantastical premise, the film deals with a quintessentially twentieth-century dilemma: a man without faith searching for meaning in life. In Jungfrukällan (The Virgin Spring, nineteen sixty), Bergman revisits the Middle Ages, exploring Scandinavia's ambiguous cultural heritage: paganism and Christianity. Smultronstället (Wild Strawberries, nineteen fifty-seven) was a gentler, more realistic account of a man taking stock of his life, a foreshadowing of the more psychological emphasis evident in the filmmaker's work of the nineteen sixties.

## [03] Learn more: The definitive portrayal of death in cinema  (1857 car., ~1.9 min)

Learn more: The definitive portrayal of death in cinema.

The image of a black-cloaked, white-faced Death (Bengt Ekerot) playing chess on the beach with a weary, questioning crusader (Max von Sydow) is as ingrained in the collective memory of film lovers as King Kong atop the Empire State Building. This scene from the Swedish gem The Seventh Seal sums up the importance, the allure and the impact that new forms of cinema had at a certain time when Hollywood's certainties were at a low ebb.

But it is a shame that this scene has come to stand for the whole film in the popular imagination. There is an unfair notion that writer and screenwriter Ingmar Bergman was too solemn in trying to make something that could be an archetype of seriousness and artistic quality. In fact, The Seventh Seal, although rooted in the great themes of Bergman's peak, is a very entertaining, often comic film - a medieval fable influenced by the director's enthusiasm for Akira Kurosawa's samurai films - as concerned with celebrating simple pleasures as with pointing out complex torments.

The knight, constantly tormented by questions about God and nothingness (he even approaches a condemned witch to ask her what the Devil knows about God), represents one side of Bergman. The simple circus performer, gently chided by his practical wife ("You and your dreams and visions", she says in the film's last line), represents the other. He seeks redemption through honest entertainment and is dismayed when his innocent performance is overshadowed by the grotesque, Church-approved spectacle of a crowd of penitents being whipped and tortured. Bergman is outraged and saddened by the evil men do, especially when it is sanctioned by a supposed religion, but the film also celebrates physical and spiritual love, collective artistic expression, food and drink, and natural beauty.

## [04] Alf Sjöberg  (1828 car., ~1.8 min)

Alf Sjöberg (nineteen oh-three to nineteen eighty) has been unfairly overshadowed by the two remarkable Swedish artists with whom he was associated. His best-known film, Hets (Torment, nineteen forty-four), was written by Bergman, who takes credit for its dark psychological atmosphere, although its claustrophobic tension is surely due to the director. In the nineteen fifties, Sjöberg adapted works by Sweden's greatest playwright, August Strindberg, producing an unforgettable version of Fröken Julie (Miss Julie, nineteen fifty-one) and an innovative and entertaining historical film, Karin Månsdotter, from nineteen fifty-four, based on Strindberg's play Erik Fourteen.

Little known outside Sweden, Hasse Ekman (nineteen fifteen to two thousand four) deserves greater recognition for Flicka och hyacinter (Girl with Hyacinths, nineteen fifty), a haunting story about a young woman's suicide. The film displays the unusual sexual frankness of postwar Swedish cinema, while at the same time examining the compromises the country made in deciding to remain neutral during World War Two. Also noteworthy is the work of Arne Sucksdorff (nineteen seventeen to two thousand one), who creatively blends fiction and documentary.

The greatest Danish director, Carl Theodor Dreyer, made only one film in the nineteen fifties. Ordet (The Word, nineteen fifty-five) is a masterpiece, an austere and compassionate parable whose miraculous elements are beautifully restrained by the subtle realism of the performances and settings. The same decade saw the emergence of Gabriel Axel (born in nineteen eighteen), who later achieved international fame with Babettes gæstebud (Babette's Feast, nineteen eighty-seven). His debut film, Altid Ballade (Always Trouble, nineteen fifty-five), is a charming realist story about a working-class family.

## [05] Arne Skouen  (997 car., ~1.0 min)

The most talented Norwegian director was Arne Skouen (nineteen thirteen to two thousand three), whose film Ni liv (Nine Lives, nineteen fifty-seven) received international acclaim and an Oscar nomination. Norway's first female director, Edith Carlmar (nineteen eleven to two thousand three), debuted with the chilling Doden er et kjærtegn (Death Is a Caress, nineteen forty-nine) and remained active in the nineteen fifties. Her last film, the coming-of-age story Ung flukt (The Wayward Girl, nineteen fifty-nine), marks the first screen appearance of actress Liv Ullmann (born in nineteen thirty-eight).

Other notable Finnish works include Valkoinen peura (The White Reindeer, nineteen fifty-two), a Lapp folk tale adapted for the screen by Erik Blomberg, which makes skillful use of the Nordic landscape, and the war film Tuntematon sotilas (The Unknown Soldier, nineteen fifty-five), by Edvin Laine (nineteen oh-five to nineteen eighty-nine), still the biggest hit in Finnish cinema to this day.
