## [00] Opening  (261 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero three: From nineteen forty to nineteen fifty-nine. Chapter four: Cinema in France.

A turbulent period for the French film industry, the nineteen fifties were marked by an Oedipal conflict between the new generation of filmmakers and their predecessors.

## [01] François Truffaut  (1880 car., ~1.9 min)

The leader of the change was François [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] (nineteen thirty-two to nineteen eighty-four), writing for [[pron: Cahiers du Cinéma|Ka-yay doo See-nay-mah]], a magazine launched in April nineteen fifty-one by critics André Bazin, Jacques Doniol-Valcroze and Joseph-Marie Lo Duca. [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] read the first issues of Cahiers in military prison in early nineteen fifty-two. By the following year, he was already writing for the magazine. In nineteen fifty-four, he contributed a call to the barricades. "A Certain Tendency of the French Cinema" was a polemical article against what he despised, which he called le cinéma de papa.

The young critic attacked filmmakers who had specialized in tasteful literary adaptations. There was more to his revolt than mere contempt for the conservative style he saw embodied in the work of Jean Aurenche (nineteen oh-three to nineteen ninety-two) and Pierre Bost (nineteen oh-one to nineteen ninety-five), the leading screenwriters of the period, and of directors such as Claude Autant-Lara (nineteen oh-one to two thousand) and Yves Allégret (nineteen oh-seven to nineteen eighty-seven). "Several producers of the time had collaborated with the Germans during the war," filmmaker Marcel Ophüls (born in nineteen twenty-seven) added regarding [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]]'s essay. "So there were many reasons to try to get rid of that oppressive influence and, at the same time, to root out the unions' stranglehold on French cinema."

[[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] extolled American B movies at the expense of the impassive, self-styled French cinema "of quality". He passionately championed a more personal style of directing, in which filmmakers could take risks and leave a unique stamp on their work. This was the birth of what became known as "la politique des auteurs". By the end of the nineteen fifties, however, French cinema had produced several films that belied the decade's image as complacent and superficial.

## [02] Le Salaire de la peur  (1018 car., ~1.0 min)

Le Salaire de la peur (The Wages of Fear, nineteen fifty-three), by Henri-Georges Clouzot (nineteen oh-seven to nineteen seventy-seven), about truck drivers hauling nitroglycerin along the potholed roads of South America, is a frenetic action film that keeps the viewer riveted. His Les Diaboliques (Diabolique, nineteen fifty-five) is a superbly frightening thriller with a plot full of twists. Beyond these, La Ronde, from nineteen fifty, by Max Ophüls, an adaptation of Schnitzler set in Vienna (but shot in France), is a stylized, sophisticated and caustic account of the love lives of a group of interconnected characters. In fact, French cinema of the nineteen fifties was much more interesting and complex than [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]]'s article suggests. It was not a decade in which a group of young cinephiles simply watched the films of Alfred Hitchcock and Howard Hawks (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen seventy-seven) at the Cinémathèque, scorning the previous generation and then launching their own careers as directors.

## [03] Henri-Georges Clouzot  (901 car., ~0.9 min)

Established filmmakers such as Henri-Georges Clouzot (nineteen oh-seven to nineteen seventy-seven), Robert Bresson (nineteen oh-one to nineteen ninety-nine), Georges Franju (nineteen twelve to nineteen eighty-seven), Jacques Becker (nineteen oh-six to nineteen sixty), Jacques Tati (nineteen oh-seven to nineteen eighty-two), René Clément (nineteen thirteen to nineteen ninety-six), Max Ophüls (nineteen oh-two to nineteen fifty-seven), René Clair (eighteen ninety-eight to nineteen eighty-one) and Jean [[pron: Renoir|Ren-wahr]] (eighteen ninety-four to nineteen seventy-nine) made important films during what proved to be a transitional decade in the history of the French film industry. Long before [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]], and by the end of the decade, Jean-Luc [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] (born in nineteen thirty) and his Cahiers colleagues had begun shooting their own films. Other filmmakers were also already clashing with the previous generation.

## [04] Learn more: Truck Drivers on Film  (1205 car., ~1.2 min)

Learn more: Truck Drivers on Film.

With its endless combination of wheels, landscape and machismo, the world of truck drivers has served as the backdrop for melodramas such as They Drive by Night, from nineteen forty, by Raoul Walsh (eighteen eighty-seven to nineteen eighty), and Thieves' Highway, from nineteen forty-nine, by Jules Dassin (nineteen eleven to two thousand eight). The international success of The Wages of Fear gave rise to variations with more exotic settings: the Sahara in Ice Cold in Alex, from nineteen fifty-eight, and the Ivory Coast in The Big Gamble, from nineteen sixty-one.

The intimidating size of trucks inspired not only raucous comedies in the nineteen seventies, among them Smokey and the Bandit, from nineteen seventy-seven, and Convoy, from nineteen seventy-eight, but also the remarkable debut film of Steven Spielberg (born in nineteen forty-six), Duel, from nineteen seventy-one, which draws on the simplicity of its premise: Dennis Weaver believes a truck driver wants to kill him. In Roadgames, from nineteen eighty-one, by Richard Franklin (nineteen forty-eight to two thousand seven), genial trucker Stacy Keach realizes he has attracted the attention of a maniac.

## [05] Agnès Varda  (1631 car., ~1.6 min)

Agnès Varda's (born in nineteen twenty-eight) debut film, La pointe courte (nineteen fifty-five - which she describes as a bombshell of nonconformity), was released in nineteen fifty-five. Drawing on Brecht and William Faulkner's modernist novel The Wild Palms, the twenty-five-year-old director told two parallel stories. La pointe courte was not a box office success, but it showed a young filmmaker far removed from the "tradition of quality" that [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] so despised.

It is also debatable whether the prolific and versatile Aurenche and Bost were really as conventional as [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] claimed. "[[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] shot at the wrong target (...) He should have attacked the directors, not the screenwriters," Bertrand Tavernier later wrote in defense of the pair. "By attacking Aurenche, he attacked the person closest to the New Wave - the person who was already ready to experiment and who was the most open." Tavernier hired Aurenche as a screenwriter on several of his films in the nineteen seventies, among them L'horloger de St Paul (The Watchmaker of St. Paul, nineteen seventy-four).

French cinema of the nineteen fifties was characterized, in a way, by its great technical masters, such as René Clément. His film Jeux interdits (Forbidden Games, nineteen fifty-two), about two children who use fantasy to cope with trauma and death during World War Two, was attacked by some as sentimental, but it won the Golden Lion in Venice and the Oscar for Best Foreign Language Film.

## [06] Jean Cocteau  (1410 car., ~1.4 min)

Clément had worked with Jean Cocteau (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen sixty-three) on La belle et la bête (Beauty and the Beast, nineteen forty-six) and directed a fine film about the Resistance, La bataille du rail (The Battle of the Rails, nineteen forty-six), but he had the misfortune of being labeled a conservative filmmaker. Claude Autant-Lara suffered the same fate. Among his best-known films of the decade are the caustic comedy L'Auberge Rouge (The Red Inn, nineteen fifty-one) and an adaptation of Stendhal's Le Rouge et le Noir, from nineteen fifty-four. Damaging as [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] was to his reputation, it was nothing compared to the disgrace Autant-Lara brought upon himself late in life by getting involved in right-wing politics and supporting the Nationalist Party.

After leaving Hollywood and returning to Europe, Max Ophuls revisited thoroughly French themes in a series of period films made in the nineteen fifties. Le plaisir, from nineteen fifty-two, was adapted from three stories by Guy de Maupassant. The atmosphere of Madame de... (The Earrings of Madame de..., nineteen fifty-three), set in turn-of-the-century Paris and revolving around a story of lost earrings and infidelity, is summed up by the moment when a man realizes his marriage is "only superficially superficial". The settings may be the boudoirs and ballrooms of high society, but the characters' suffering was all too real.

## [07] French Cancan  (1794 car., ~1.8 min)

After almost fifteen years away, Jean [[pron: Renoir|Ren-wahr]] returned to his homeland to make French Cancan, from nineteen fifty-four, set in belle époque Montmartre. His best film was Casque d'Or, from nineteen fifty-two, an intensely romantic evocation of the Parisian underworld of the eighteen nineties. Almost its equal was the crime film Touchez pas au grisbi, from nineteen fifty-four, which revived the acting career of Jean Gabin.

That film had a great influence on postwar French crime cinema - as can be seen in the minimalism of Du rififi chez les hommes (Rififi, nineteen fifty-five), by Jules Dassin (nineteen eleven to two thousand eight), shot on location, with an exuberantly laconic narrative style and a celebrated silent safecracking sequence that lasts almost half an hour. A similarly somber atmosphere pervades Bob le flambeur, from nineteen fifty-six, by Jean-Pierre Melville (nineteen seventeen to nineteen seventy-three), about an aging gangster who plans to rob a casino. The French approach to film noir displayed a melancholy lyricism that would later influence American films of the genre.

Robert Bresson, another director attacked by the Cahiers group, created an existentialist masterpiece with Un condamné à mort s'est échappé (A Man Escaped, nineteen fifty-six), about a French Resistance fighter imprisoned by the Nazis who spends his time planning his escape. [[pron: Truffaut|Troo-foe]] admired its spare style. As he himself wrote, this was "one of those films of which one can say that it does not contain a single useless shot, nor one that could have been cut or shortened. It is the very opposite of films that look like a montage, a collage of images". As his words attest, French cinema of the nineteen fifties was not as stagnant as he himself suggested in his famous essay for Cahiers.
