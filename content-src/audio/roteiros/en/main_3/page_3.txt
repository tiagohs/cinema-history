Part zero three: From nineteen forty to nineteen fifty-nine. Chapter three: Italian Neorealism.

Emerging in the nineteen forties and marked by stories about the working class shot with very few resources, Italian Neorealism was an important movement that sought to portray living conditions, oppression and inequality in post-World War Two Italy.

## [01] Cesare Zavattini  (2116 car., ~2.1 min)

The term "neorealism" was coined by Italian critics in the early nineteen forties. Screenwriter Cesare Zavattini (nineteen oh-two, nineteen forty-nine) was one of the movement's leading figures and one of its most important theorists. He urged filmmakers to go out into the streets, ride buses and streetcars and steal the stories of everyday life, writing in his war diary Diario di cinema e di vita: "Let us set the camera up in the street, in a room, let us watch with insatiable patience, let us train ourselves to contemplate our fellow men in their simplest gestures."

Zavattini wrote the screenplays for films such as Ladri di biciclette (Bicycle Thieves, nineteen forty-eight), Umberto D., from nineteen fifty-two, and Miracolo a Milano (Miracle in Milan, nineteen fifty-one), all focused on the hardships ordinary people faced in Italy at the time. Italian cinema of the neorealist period is full of stories about people trying to solve everyday dilemmas.

The movement was a reaction against the style of the nineteen thirties Telefono Bianco (White Telephone) films, which copied Hollywood movies and focused on bourgeois life. Neorealist films generally used amateur actors and were shot on location in poor areas, portraying people going about their daily activities, with an emphasis on gritty realism and a sense of resilient energy.

Ossessione (Obsession, nineteen forty-three), directed by Luchino Visconti (nineteen oh-six to nineteen seventy-six), is sometimes cited as the first neorealist film, although it is a work with strong noir elements. Its screenplay is based on a crime novel by James M. Cain, The Postman Always Rings Twice, from nineteen thirty-four. Visconti worked with his fellow writers and filmmakers from the magazine Cinema, among them Giuseppe de Santis. It is a sordid story set in the Italian countryside about a drifter who has an affair with a restaurant owner's wife; the lovers plot to kill the husband. Visconti ran into censorship problems over the film, which was banned in Italy under the fascist regime.

## [02] Learn more: The spark of a revolution  (2122 car., ~2.1 min)

Learn more: The spark of a revolution.

Regarded as the spark of an aesthetic revolution in cinema, Rossellini's Roma città aperta (Rome, Open City, nineteen forty-five) was the first great work of Italian Neorealism, and it managed to shatter the conventions of the "white telephone" films of Mussolini's regime, which were in vogue in Italy in the early nineteen forties. Rossellini's film about the Italian Resistance was written during the underground struggle against the Nazis. Recalling [[pron: Eisenstein|Eye-zen-stine]]'s formula of the "choral film", it tells the story of a group of patriots hiding in the apartment of a lithographer named Francesco (Francesco Grand Jaquet). The communist who leads the group, Manfredi (Marcello Pagliero), is hunted by the Gestapo and eventually captured and executed. Pina (Anna Magnani), Francesco's wife, and Don Pietro (Aldo Fabrizi), a good-natured priest, die trying to help Manfredi escape. Yet it is the solidarity of Rome as a city that foreshadows the final victory over the invaders.

The scarcity of technical and financial resources turned out to be a virtue of Rome, Open City, which was shot in a documentary style. By showing real people in real locations, the film brought a breath of fresh air to Western cinema. The freedom of the camera's movements and the authenticity of the characters, combined with a new way of telling a story, were among the qualities that made the film the revelation of the nineteen forty-six Cannes Film Festival, where it received the Palme d'Or. Neorealism soon became the aesthetic model for directors interested in a vivid depiction of history and society.

One of the most striking aspects of the film is Rossellini's approach to each character's drama. Some of the film's heroes will remain forever in viewers' hearts. Who could forget the sight of the pregnant Pina running through the bullets, or of the good priest shot before the terrified eyes of the children? Although it may lean toward melodrama, the story is as moving today as it was then. It is no surprise that, after this role, Magnani became one of the greatest actresses in Italian cinema.

## [03] Vittorio de Sica  (2002 car., ~2.0 min)

The protagonists of neorealism range from the very young to the very old. Umberto D., directed by Vittorio de Sica (nineteen oh-two to nineteen seventy-four), tells the story of an impoverished old man (Carlo Battisti) who clings desperately to his dignity. He wanders around Rome with his only loyal companion: a dog. On the other hand, children play prominent roles in Bicycle Thieves and Miracolo a Milano (Miracle in Milan, nineteen fifty-one). But neorealist directors did not always take a documentary approach to their material.

De Sica's Miracle in Milan, for example, is about a group of beggars living in a shantytown on the outskirts of postwar Milan and could well be described as "magical realism". It deals with both greedy landowners and mercenary squatters, with a strong element of fantasy. And not all neorealist films used amateur actors: Roma città aperta (Rome, Open City, nineteen forty-five), by Roberto Rossellini (nineteen oh-six to nineteen seventy-seven), stars Anna Magnani (nineteen oh-eight to nineteen seventy-three), one of the leading names of postwar Italian cinema, and the actor, writer and director Aldo Fabrizi (nineteen oh-five to nineteen ninety).

Rome, Open City won the Grand Prix at the Cannes Film Festival and had an immediate impact. Legend has it that the director put the film together from "scraps" - bits of film stock given to him by American army cameramen who liberated Rome in World War Two. His filming style, however, was constrained by the conditions under which he worked. Rossellini went on to direct Paisà (Paisan, nineteen forty-six), a long film about the end of the war in Italy. It was followed by the brutal Germania anno zero (Germany, Year Zero, nineteen forty-eight), set in the ruins of Berlin, which details the struggle of a young boy fighting to survive right after the defeat of the Nazis. There is a great difference between the films of Rossellini's war trilogy and his work with Ingrid Bergman in Stromboli, from nineteen fifty.

## [04] Part 3  (864 car., ~0.9 min)

This film tells a strange and haunting story about a young Lithuanian immigrant who marries an Italian fisherman to escape a refugee camp at the end of the war. She goes to live with her husband on a remote island and finds herself bewildered and oppressed by the macho society she encounters there.

By the early nineteen fifties, neorealist cinema in Italy no longer displayed the immediacy that had characterized the movement in the years following the war. Conditions had changed, Italian society was wealthier and filmmakers were less concerned with portraying those living on the margins of society. Even so, the influence of neorealism was vast; Cinema Novo in Brazil, Free Cinema in Britain, the French New Wave and the cinéma vérité movement in documentary were all influenced by the work of directors such as De Sica and Rossellini in the nineteen forties.
