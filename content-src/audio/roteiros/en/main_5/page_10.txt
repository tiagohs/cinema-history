## [01] Kazuo Kuroki  (2036 car., ~2.0 min)

In Japan, the styles and genres that had been popular since the Pacific War (nineteen forty-one to nineteen forty-five) no longer drew audiences. Some studios went bankrupt, while others relied on repeating a successful formula, such as Shochiku with the long-running Tora-san series - the bittersweet, good-humored story of the rough but essentially decent Torajiro Kuruma (charmingly played by Kiyoshi Atsumi). Forty-eight episodes were produced from nineteen sixty-nine to nineteen ninety-five, providing nostalgic, reliable entertainment for the New Year and the summer Obon festivities.

Less commercial was the work of the Art Theatre Guild (ATG), which often dealt with political and social themes in an avant-garde style. Some of the best Japanese art films, by directors such as Kazuo Kuroki (nineteen thirty to two thousand six), Masahiro Shinoda (born in nineteen thirty-one), Shuji Terayama (nineteen thirty-five to nineteen eighty-three) and Yoshishige Yoshida (born in nineteen thirty-three), were made with ATG funding.

Especially admirable are the ATG films of Nagisa Oshima (born in nineteen thirty-two). Shonen (Boy, nineteen sixty-nine) is a moving study of a boy forced by his parents to take part in a criminal scheme. Gishiki (The Ceremony, nineteen seventy-one) offers a masterful account of the postwar experience in Japan, told through the story of one family. However, Oshima had to look abroad for the resources to fund his most controversial film, the explicit study of sexual obsession Ai no Korîda (In the Realm of the Senses, nineteen seventy-six).

Mainland China, under the impact of the Cultural Revolution until nineteen seventy-six, produced very few films. Chinese-language cinema, however, flourished in Hong Kong and Taiwan, where martial arts films, especially those produced by Hong Kong's Shaw Brothers studio, attracted international attention.

## [02] Learn more: A Touch of Zen  (1750 car., ~1.8 min)

Learn more: A Touch of Zen.

Critic Peter Rist called King Hu "the least appreciated of the 'great directors' in the history of cinema" and "one of the most original filmmakers of the nineteen sixties". Hu was the master of wuxia, or martial arts, a genre and tradition that dates back in Chinese cinema to the silent films, but that truly took on its modern form after Hu and some of his contemporaries infused it with a new sophistication in the late nineteen sixties.

Hu took three years to make A Touch of Zen, which is often cited as his masterpiece, in which he fused Chinese history and Buddhist theology with choreographed action and elegant camera movement. He directs with great skill, and the way he uses the camera, with tracking shots and cranes, sometimes reminiscent of Kenji Mizoguchi (eighteen ninety-eight to nineteen fifty-six), is inventive throughout. Working in Taiwan rather than in Hong Kong, where he had made his first wuxia film, Da zui xia (Come Drink with Me, nineteen sixty-six), he makes the most of the dazzling mountain landscapes, which provide a dramatic backdrop.

The action scenes are certainly memorable, but the film is notable for its slow build-up, with the first third consisting of hints and foreshadowing rather than explicit violence. There is also a dose of understated humor. Unfortunately, the time and care Hu lavished on the film were not rewarded with immediate commercial success. Its length led to it being shown in two parts in Taiwan and cut by distributors in Hong Kong. In nineteen seventy-five, this final version won a special technical prize at Cannes and, in two thousand five, A Touch of Zen was ranked ninth on the Hong Kong Film Awards' list of the Best one hundred Chinese Motion Pictures.

## [03] Bruce Lee  (738 car., ~0.7 min)

Among the most famous martial arts stars in the West was Bruce Lee (nineteen forty to nineteen seventy-three), whose incredible fighting skill and early death turned him into a legend. But it was the films directed by Chia-Liang Liu (born in nineteen thirty-six) that elevated the kung fu genre. Films such as Shao Lin San Shi Liu Fang (The Thirty-sixth Chamber of Shaolin, nineteen seventy-eight) fused action, humor and drama. Equally notable are the swordplay films of King Hu (nineteen thirty-one to nineteen ninety-seven), who released his masterpiece Hsia nu (A Touch of Zen, nineteen seventy-one) in nineteen seventy-one, followed by Ying chun ge zhi Fengbo (The Fate of Lee Khan, nineteen seventy-three) in nineteen seventy-three.

## [04] Sholay  (1308 car., ~1.3 min)

In India, Bollywood continued to stage extravagant musicals, as well as producing more and more action and gangster films and so-called "masala" films, which combined many genres. Among the most famous Bollywood films of the decade was Sholay, from nineteen seventy-five, a "curry Western" directed by G. P. Sippy (nineteen fifteen to two thousand seven) that became the biggest box-office hit in Indian history, and Amar Akbar Anthony, from nineteen seventy-seven, by Manmohan Desai (nineteen thirty-six to nineteen ninety-four), which examined India's cultural and religious diversity through the story of three brothers raised respectively as Hindu, Muslim and Christian.

Meanwhile, in Bengal, Satyajit Ray (nineteen twenty-one to nineteen ninety-two) continued to work in his precise, realist vein: Shatranj Ke Khilari - The Chess Players, from nineteen seventy-seven, was praised for its careful historical reconstruction. His films also took on a more direct political emphasis: Pratidwandi (The Adversary, nineteen seventy-one) and Jana Aranya (The Middleman, nineteen seventy-five) focused on the problems of urban unemployment and the immoralities of capitalism, while the award-winning Ashani Sanket (Distant Thunder, nineteen seventy-three) showed the effects of World War Two on a Bengali village.

## [05] Shaw Brothers  (1824 car., ~1.8 min)

Another Indian filmmaker who dramatized political and social issues was Shyam Benegal (born in nineteen thirty-four), with the Indian New Wave feature Ankur, from nineteen seventy-four. This film includes a level of violence and profanity unusual for Indian films of the time and was nominated for the Golden Bear at the Twenty-fourth Berlin International Film Festival.

During the early seventies, wuxia (wuxia novels, usually translated as "martial chivalry") gave way to a new revival of the kung fu film, more graphic, more fearless and spoken in Mandarin, which became the dominant genre throughout the decade until the early eighties. Highly trained martial artists such as Ti Lung (born in nineteen forty-six) and Gordon Liu (born in nineteen fifty-one) became some of the biggest movie stars, as ever-greater proportions of films' running time were devoted to fight scenes.

The Chinese Boxer, from nineteen seventy, starring and directed by Jimmy Wang Yu (born in nineteen forty-three), is widely considered the film that created this kung fu "boom." But staying at the forefront, at least initially, were the Shaw Brothers studio and filmmaker Chang Cheh (nineteen twenty-three to two thousand two). Chang's Bao chou (Vengeance!, nineteen seventy) was another film that popularized the genre, to which he contributed dozens of films, among them Ma Yong Zhen (The Boxer from Shantung, nineteen seventy-two), Wu Du (The Five Venoms, nineteen seventy-eight) and Can que (Crippled Avengers, nineteen seventy-nine).

Kung fu cinema was especially influenced by Chang's preoccupation with his vision of masculine values and male friendship. The warrior women who had been prominent in the wuxia films of the late sixties were set aside, with a few prominent exceptions such as the popular Angela Mao (born in nineteen fifty).

## [06] Learn more: The Bruce Lee Classic  (1472 car., ~1.5 min)

Learn more: The Bruce Lee Classic.

Having established his credentials as an action-movie superstar with low-budget Hong Kong productions such as Fist of Fury and The Big Boss, Bruce Lee, a cha-cha champion (as well as a karate and kung fu champion), only got the chance to complete one film made to Hollywood production standards. Lee, a martial artist whose physical skills reached a spiritual level, invades (along with his allies John Saxon and Jim Kelly) the island lair of a master villain and disposes of hordes of second-tier henchmen (including Jackie Chan, who gets his neck broken in a quick scene).

Director Robert Clouse (nineteen twenty-eight to nineteen ninety-seven) has just the style you would expect from someone directing a Brock Landers adventure (heavy use of zooms and lens flares), and Lee, whose real-life Hong Kong English was heavily accented but fluent and distinctive, is stuck with "Charlie Chan dialogue" consisting of wise, cryptic pronouncements. The action remains formidable, however, since, without the help of wires or the effects used in recent films such as Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon, Lee magnificently executes his moves, wielding his signature nunchucks and flexing his oiled torso.

Influential on an entire genre of martial arts films that followed, and also a template for every "fighting" video game, Enter the Dragon earns its place in film history purely through Lee's charismatic presence and its inimitable fight sequences.

## [07] Lau Kar Leung  (2035 car., ~2.0 min)

Chang's only rival as the genre's most influential filmmaker was his longtime action choreographer, Lau Kar Leung (nineteen thirty-four to twenty thirteen). Lau began directing his own films for Shaw Brothers in nineteen seventy-five with Shen da (The Spiritual Boxer, nineteen seventy-five), a forerunner of the kung fu comedy. In subsequent films such as Hung Hsi Kuan (Executioners from Shaolin, nineteen seventy-seven), The Thirty-sixth Chamber of Shaolin, from nineteen seventy-eight, and Shi ba ban wu yi (Legendary Weapons of China, nineteen eighty-two), Lau emphasized the tradition and philosophy of the martial arts and sought to give the fights more authenticity, speed and complexity.

The kung fu boom was driven in part by enormous international popularity, not only in East Asia. In the West, imported kung fu films, dubbed and often altered and retitled, shown as "B movies" in urban theaters and on television, made Hong Kong cinema widely known - though not necessarily highly respected - for the first time. In particular, African Americans and other ethnic minorities embraced the genre (as exemplified by the popular hip-hop group Wu-Tang Clan), perhaps because it was an almost unprecedented source of adventure stories with non-white heroes, which also revealed a strong current of ethnic and/or nationalist pride.

Amid all this, no other figure was more responsible for this international fame than Bruce Lee, a martial artist and actor born in the United States and raised in Hong Kong. Lee completed only four films before his death at age thirty-two: Tang Shan da Xiong (The Big Boss, nineteen seventy-one), Jing Wu Men (Fist of Fury, nineteen seventy-two), Meng Long Guo Jiang (The Way of the Dragon, nineteen seventy-two) and Enter the Dragon, from nineteen seventy-three. But in this brief career, he became the first global Chinese movie star.

## [08] Jackie Chan  (1667 car., ~1.7 min)

Asian film historian Patrick Macias attributes his success to "(bringing) the warrior spirit into the present... developing his own fighting style... and possessing superhuman charisma." His first three films broke local box-office records and were successful in much of the world. The English-language film Enter the Dragon, the first coproduction between the United States and Hong Kong, made ninety million dollars worldwide, becoming the most internationally successful film from that part of the globe up to that time. In addition, his decision from the outset to work for the then-new Golden Harvest studio, instead of accepting the Shaws studio's usual, notoriously ungenerous contract, was a factor that contributed to the meteoric rise of Golden Harvest and the decline of Shaw.

Following Lee's untimely death, a small industry of fake Lee films emerged, with leads who adopted similar names (Bruce Li, Bruce Lai, etc.), or unused footage of Lee, or some combination of both. The passing fad did little to earn the respect of most Western audiences for the relatively new phenomenon of martial arts cinema. But despite this posthumous treatment, Lee continues to cast a long shadow over Hong Kong cinema.

The only Chinese actor to rival Lee's global fame is Jackie Chan (born in nineteen fifty-four). Like many kung fu actors of his era, Chan trained in Peking Opera and got his start in film as a stuntman, notably in some of Lee's films. For a while, he was groomed by Lo Wei, director of The Big Boss and Fist of Fury, to be another Bruce Lee "clone" in several films, including Xin Jing Wu Men (New Fist of Fury, nineteen seventy-six), with little success.

## [09] Learn more: Jackie Chan's Influential Classic  (1044 car., ~1.0 min)

Learn more: Jackie Chan's Influential Classic.

Drunken Master is perhaps the last great classic of the period experts call the old school of kung fu (nineteen sixty-seven to nineteen eighty-three). It was the second collaboration between Woo Ping Yuen and Jackie Chan, following the huge success of Snake in the Eagle's Shadow, and it is even more refined, both in its fantastic fight choreography and in its measured doses of humor.

Veteran Simon Yuen, the director's father, plays the unforgettable sifu (master) who torments Chan during his training, and he became a star with the role only after a career of more than forty-five years, and shortly before his death. Jackie Chan got his first major role in the style he helped create, parodying the Chinese hero Wong Fei Hung, and he has an incredible fight scene at the end of the film.

The influence of Drunken Master was (and still is) so great that dozens of other productions (even Hollywood ones) drew on characters or scenes from the film, such as Bloodsport, from nineteen eighty-eight.

## [10] Se Ying Diu Sau  (1169 car., ~1.2 min)

But in nineteen seventy-eight, Chan teamed up with action choreographer Yuen Woo Ping on Yuen's first film as director, Se Ying Diu Sau (Snake in the Eagle's Shadow, nineteen seventy-eight). The resulting mix of physical comedy and kung fu gave Chan his first hit and the rudiments of what would become his signature style. Chan's next film with Yuen, Zui quan (Drunken Master, nineteen seventy-eight), and his directorial debut, Xiao Quan Guai Zhao (Fearless Hyena, nineteen seventy-nine), were also big hits and cemented his popularity.

Although these films were not the first kung fu comedies, they launched a trend that helped revitalize the kung fu genre. Particularly notable in this respect were two of Chan's childhood classmates at the Peking Opera school, Sammo Hung (born in nineteen fifty-two) and Yuen Biao (born in nineteen fifty-seven), who also built careers in this specialty, sometimes in the same films as Chan. Hung, remarkable for the apparent paradox of his overweight body and physical agility, also became known early on as a director and action choreographer, with films such as Fei Lung Gwoh Gong (Enter the Fat Dragon, nineteen seventy-eight).
