## [01] Fifth Generation  (1437 car., ~1.4 min)

In nineteen seventy-six, the Cultural Revolution came to an end with the death of Chairman Mao. Few could have predicted that by the end of the nineteen eighties Chinese cinema would be producing some of the most original and acclaimed films in the world. The reopening of the Beijing Film Academy paved the way, and when the first class graduated in nineteen eighty-two, the so-called Fifth Generation of filmmakers was ready to lead a revolution in Chinese cinema.

Meanwhile, the older directors of the Fourth Generation were already reassessing China's troubled past. Xie Jin (nineteen twenty-three to two thousand eight) used melodrama to cast a look at the political persecutions of the Cultural Revolution in Tian Yun Shan Chuan Qi (Legend of Tianyun Mountain, nineteen eighty). They also adopted a more innovative way of filmmaking that resulted in a new realism.

Zhang Nuanxin (nineteen forty-one to nineteen ninety-five), for example, opted for a semi-documentary style in Sha Ou (The Drive to Win, nineteen eighty-one). The Fourth Generation dealt with issues of contemporary relevance. In Xiangnu xiaoxiao (A Girl from Hunan, nineteen eighty-six), Xie Fei (born in nineteen forty-two) showed the persistence of marriage traditions in rural regions.

## [02] Chen Kaige  (881 car., ~0.9 min)

Moreover, as teachers at the Beijing Film Academy, the Fourth Generation directors would influence the next generation of filmmakers. In the words of Zhang Yingjin, "Like their predecessors, the Fifth Generation favored a non-dramatic, depoliticized narrative structure, but went further with sparse dialogue and music, as well as an abundance of ambiguities in characterization and narrative. They insisted on showing their films rather than telling what happened."

Chen Kaige (born in nineteen fifty-two) made a visually stunning debut with Huang tu di (Yellow Earth, nineteen eighty-four) at the remote Guangxi studio, near the border with Vietnam. Zhang Yimou (born in nineteen fifty-one), who had worked as cinematographer on Chen's films, made his own debut with Hong Gao Liang (Red Sorghum, nineteen eighty-eight), a harrowing account of Chinese history before the civil war.

## [03] Hou Hsiao-hsien  (1649 car., ~1.6 min)

Here, as in his later films, Zhang benefited from an intense performance by his star, Gong Li, and made great use of the complex visual symbolism of red - a color linked to fire and blood, but with traditional associations with happiness in China.

Communism, however, did not dominate the entire Chinese-speaking world. Across the strait, the capitalist island of Taiwan was also experiencing a cinematic renaissance with the emergence of a creative and distinctive art cinema. The leading figures of this renaissance included Hou Hsiao-hsien (born in nineteen forty-seven), whose films are characterized by long sequences and long takes reminiscent of classic Japanese cinema. His most important work is, arguably, the Taiwan trilogy, which begins with Bei Qing Cheng Shi (A City of Sadness, nineteen eighty-nine). The other notable director of the Taiwanese New Cinema was Edward Yang (nineteen forty-seven to two thousand seven), whose film Kong bu fen zi (Terrorizers, nineteen eighty-six) strikingly explores the theme of urban angst.

Elsewhere in the Chinese-speaking world, John Woo (born in nineteen forty-six) changed the face of Hong Kong cinema and profoundly influenced international cinema with the action thriller Ying Hung Boon Sik (A Better Tomorrow, nineteen eighty-six). This slick, stylized story of two rival brothers - a gangster and a cop - breathed new life into Chow Yun-fat's seemingly stalled career and broke box office records in Hong Kong.
