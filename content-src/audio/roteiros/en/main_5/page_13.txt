## [00] Opening  (220 car., ~0.2 min)

Part zero five: From nineteen seventy to nineteen eighty-nine. Chapter thirteen: Vietnam on Film.

The legacy of Vietnam in the cinema of the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties was enormous, inspiring landmark films.

## [01] The Green Berets  (1959 car., ~2.0 min)

The Vietnam War was the first war of the television age and produced striking images of the harsh reality of the battlefield. In contrast to World War Two, however, Hollywood showed a distinct lack of enthusiasm for producing patriotic films. Usually cited as the only pro-war feature produced during the conflict, The Green Berets, from nineteen sixty-eight, was savaged by critics for its simplistic stance.

Hollywood films of the late nineteen sixties and early nineteen seventies tended to offer less direct reflections on the war. The insolent, anti-authoritarian tone of M*A*S*H, from nineteen seventy, had more to do with Vietnam than with its supposed setting, the Korean War. Ulzana's Raid, from nineteen seventy-two, a fascinating Western by Robert Aldrich (nineteen eighteen to nineteen eighty-three), uses the story of a cavalry troop with the unhappy mission of recapturing a band of Apaches as a metaphor for a war that cannot be won.

Authentic dramatizations of the Vietnam War had to wait until the fighting was over. In nineteen seventy-eight, Coming Home, from nineteen seventy-eight, by Hal Ashby (nineteen twenty-eight to nineteen eighty-eight), criticized the war through the misadventures of a disabled veteran. Released the same year, The Deer Hunter, from nineteen seventy-eight, by Michael Cimino (born in nineteen thirty-nine), was more ambiguous in its attitude. Although described as an antiwar film, it was harshly criticized for presenting a brutal and one-sided image of the North Vietnamese.

The Russian roulette scene in particular stirred controversy, as there was no evidence whatsoever that the North Vietnamese had used the deadly game. It was Apocalypse Now, from nineteen seventy-nine, made by Francis Ford Coppola (born in nineteen thirty-nine) the following year, that provided the most powerful and enduring creation of the Vietnam experience and set a standard by which all other films on the subject came to be judged.

## [02] Learn more: Platoon  (1681 car., ~1.7 min)

Learn more: Platoon.

Writer-director Oliver Stone (born in nineteen forty-six) was the first Vietnam veteran to get Hollywood backing to make an autobiographical film about the conflict. The Deer Hunter, from nineteen seventy-eight, and Apocalypse Now, from nineteen seventy-nine, had turned the war into subject matter for movies, but it was Platoon that showed the reality of Vietnam to generations who only came of age after the conflict was over.

Chris Taylor (Charlie Sheen), who stands in for Stone, is an idealistic young man who volunteered to serve with the infantry in Vietnam. He feels torn between two "fathers": the gentle, sensitive super-soldier Sergeant Elias (Willem Dafoe), who smokes pot and listens to soul with the Black soldiers, and the sadistic, scar-faced Sergeant Barnes (Tom Berenger), who drinks and listens to country and western with the rednecks. Chris follows the path of other screen soldiers, "becoming a man", but whereas the recruits whipped into shape by John Wayne always ended up saluting the flag, Chris ends up losing faith in his country amid the blood and confusion.

Although it is overly schematic in pitting the two sergeants against each other and contrasting their philosophies, Platoon is a powerful film. Terrifying as he is, Barnes teaches Chris how to survive rather than become a martyr - the climax, unthinkable in earlier films, has Chris shooting one of his own superiors rather than facing the enemy. Harrowing and suspenseful as it is, the film is not devoid of nostalgia - the soundtrack is made up of big nineteen sixties hits and, in one scene, the soldiers sing "The Tracks of My Tears" by Smokey Robinson and The Miracles.

## [03] Oliver Stone  (1241 car., ~1.2 min)

Films dealing with America's emotional landscape in the wake of Vietnam provided an oblique commentary on the war. Who'll Stop the Rain, from nineteen seventy-six, shows a veteran and his best friend's wife on the trail of a shipment of Vietnamese heroin in California. The madness of the war is encapsulated in one of the film's lines of dialogue: "You see, in a world where elephants are pursued by flying men, people naturally want to get high."

Oliver Stone (born in nineteen forty-six) drew on his experience in Vietnam to make Platoon, from nineteen eighty-six, the first of a trilogy depicting different aspects of the war. The second, Born on the Fourth of July, from nineteen eighty-nine, was based on the memoir of a disabled war veteran, and the third, Heaven and Earth, from nineteen ninety-three, is the true story of a young Vietnamese woman who marries a traumatized soldier.

Stanley Kubrick (nineteen twenty-eight to nineteen ninety-nine) created an intense vision of Vietnam when he rebuilt the battlefields of Southeast Asia in the marshes east of London to shoot Full Metal Jacket, from nineteen eighty-seven. The conflict in Vietnam spilled across the borders into Cambodia when the Americans left in nineteen seventy-five.

## [04] The Killing Fields  (1413 car., ~1.4 min)

More than three million Cambodians were killed by the Khmer Rouge regime, as dramatized in the British film The Killing Fields, from nineteen eighty-four. It chronicles the barbaric events of Year Zero from the perspective of two journalists - one Cambodian, Dith Pran (Haing S. Ngor), and the other from New York, Sydney Schanberg (Sam Waterston) - including Pran's struggle to survive and his escape from one of the tortuous forced labor camps.

In the twenty-first century, attitudes toward Vietnam were reflected in a new political context. Made in the first year of the Iraq War, We Were Soldiers, from two thousand two, starring Mel Gibson (born in nineteen fifty-six), offers a sympathetic view of American intervention.

How can a shoot planned for sixteen weeks end up lasting fifteen months? In the case of Apocalypse Now, the real feat is that (almost) every member of the crew managed to finish the job alive. Its director, Francis Ford Coppola, ended up following his protagonist, Captain Willard, in his plunge into madness: if the soldier's mission was to hunt down Colonel Kurtz, Coppola's was to finish a film he had started without a screenplay and without an ending. He himself would admit to having contemplated suicide on three occasions over the four years of production, when everything that could go wrong went wrong. And everything nobody had even imagined could happen turned out even worse.

## [05] Charles Chaplin  (820 car., ~0.8 min)

No Hollywood studio wanted to hear about a film on Vietnam just months after the United States' defeat in the most controversial war in its history. Coppola secured the backing of the distributor United Artists, founded by Charles Chaplin (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen seventy-seven) in nineteen thirty so that artists would not have to depend on the commercial studios, but he was forced to negotiate personally with investors and back every loan with all his properties and the revenue still being generated by The Godfather, from nineteen seventy-two, and its sequel. In the seventies, Hollywood studios had not yet been absorbed by multinationals, so every dollar had to be negotiated. And shoots, thanks to the fact that executives were cinephiles rather than economists, could run long if the film deserved it.

## [06] Vittorio Storaro  (1772 car., ~1.8 min)

Apocalypse Now (released in the summer of nineteen seventy-nine) was, according to cinematographer Vittorio Storaro (born in nineteen forty), "a picture of the imposition of one culture on another and of the Americans' desire to turn everything into a spectacle": if real soldiers played rock and roll while bombing Vietnamese villages, the ones in the film listened to Wagner's Ride of the Valkyries. If the army devastated Vietnam with napalm explosions, Coppola would film the biggest explosion ever produced outside a war. With a budget of eleven million dollars (the same amount as Star Wars, from nineteen seventy-seven), Apocalypse Now would be the first art-house blockbuster.

Steve McQueen (nineteen thirty to nineteen eighty) turned down the lead role, as did Al Pacino (born in nineteen forty), Robert Redford (born in nineteen thirty-six) and Jack Nicholson (born in nineteen thirty-seven). Frustration led Coppola to throw his five Oscars out the window and, after putting them back on the shelf, to hire Harvey Keitel (born in nineteen thirty-nine). After three weeks of shooting, however, he realized that the actor's acting style did not suit a character who was supposed to function as a passive spectator on a journey to the end of the world and of the human soul. The replacement was Martin Sheen (born in nineteen forty), who landed in the Philippines in the middle of his own battle with his demons: he drank nonstop, smoked three packs a day and, in one of the first scenes, collapsed on the floor screaming through tears. When he looked in the mirror and punched his reflection, his arm was covered in blood, but Coppola ordered the cameras to keep rolling while the actor screamed. Apocalypse Now had only just begun. The horror had not yet arrived.

## [07] Part 6  (1264 car., ~1.3 min)

Instead of working from a screenplay, Coppola carried everywhere a copy of Heart of Darkness (the unadaptable Joseph Conrad novel on which the film is based) that he had underlined. And he wrote each scene the night before. The production was made in the Philippines because the country's president, the dictator Ferdinand Marcos, made everything easier: in exchange for thousands of dollars a day, the crew could use Philippine army helicopters and pilots and bomb as many hectares of forest with napalm as it needed. But on several occasions the helicopters, cameras still running, abandoned the scene because they had to go fight the Filipino rebel guerrillas.

Coppola and his nine hundred workers had no choice but to wait, arms folded, for the pilots to wipe out the enemy and decide to return to the set. Often, the airmen who took part in the rehearsals were not the same ones who later showed up for the shoot, so everything had to start from scratch each morning. Like the Vietnam War itself, this shoot was the imposition of one culture on another (the sets were built by locals, exploited at one dollar a day, and one of them died buried under a block). And, as also happened with the Viet Cong, the invasion was not as easy as the Americans had thought.

## [08] Marlon Brando  (2019 car., ~2.0 min)

Typhoon Olga ravaged the Philippines in May nineteen seventy-six. Coppola tried to incorporate the rain into the film (monsoons battered Vietnam during the war), but the plan proved impractical when the storm destroyed several of the film's sets. On hearing this, the director reacted by cooking a nice plate of pasta while listening to Puccini's La Bohème. After dinner, he decided to halt shooting for two months. When he resumed, he ran straight into another force of nature: Marlon Brando (nineteen twenty-four to two thousand four).

Brando showed up weighing one hundred thirty kilos (even though the screenplay described Kurtz as a mythological, slender and athletic creature), without having read the screenplay and with no intention of sharing a scene with Dennis Hopper (nineteen thirty-six to twenty ten) - who, to build his character, had asked for twenty-five grams of cocaine, paid for out of the production budget. But Brando had every intention of collecting his salary of three million dollars for three weeks.

We managed to get hold of those weapons and ammunition, and we went to a remote location where we could record and make original recordings with all the right perspectives.

Coppola had to postpone the shoot yet another week to read Brando's dialogue aloud and prepare the scenes with him. The filmmaker let the star improvise philosophical, martial and philanthropic reflections in an eighteen-minute monologue shot in shadow at the actor's request, as he did not want his physical bulk to distract viewers. He even put an earpiece in Brando's ear to feed him his lines. One day, Brando told Coppola that he had already used him enough and that, if he wanted more scenes, he should hire someone else. He got up from his chair, left and never appeared on the set again.

## [09] Part 8  (1823 car., ~1.8 min)

While waiting for Brando to be ready, producer Gray Frederickson began to smell something rotten on the set of Kurtz's compound. "You have to get rid of the dead rats," he told production designer Dean Tavoularis, who explained that the rodents were there on purpose to create atmosphere. Suddenly, a prop man walking by exclaimed: "Just wait until you find the human corpses." To the producer's astonishment, they took him to a tent full of dead bodies, stored until Coppola was ready to shoot Willard's arrival at the compound (where there would be corpses hanging from the trees and strewn across the ground). "It's going to look really authentic," the designer promised.

The man who supplied the corpses did not work at a morgue, as he had claimed; he had stolen the bodies from graves. The police then shut down the production for several days to question each of the workers and make sure they were not murderers. Since it was impossible to return the unidentified bodies to their graves (and United Artists refused to pay for burials), nobody knows, or nobody has wanted to tell, what was done with them.

On March fifth, nineteen seventy-seven, four days after the shoot passed the one-year mark, Martin Sheen woke up at two in the morning with unbearable chest pain. The actor left his tent and dragged himself along the road, in agony, for a kilometer until he found help. He was having a heart attack. When he heard, Coppola suffered an epileptic seizure, but he tried to hide the incident from United Artists. "Even if Martin dies, he's not dead until I say so," the director warned. Coppola had run up a debt equivalent to one hundred thirty-five million reais that would have left his wife Eleanor and his three children (Gio, twelve; Roman, ten; and Sofia, four) destitute. Suicide was no longer even an option.

## [10] Venom  (2132 car., ~2.1 min)

Apocalypse Now, with a budget that today would be comparable to that of Venom, from twenty eighteen, or San Andreas, from twenty fifteen, had surpassed Cleopatra, from nineteen sixty-three, as the most expensive film in history up to that point. During the six weeks Sheen was out, Coppola shot scenes that were not in the screenplay, sent a telegram to his friend (and the project's original director) George Lucas (born in nineteen forty-four) congratulating him on the success of Star Wars, and, while he was at it, asking for money, and kept stalling on finishing the film. As with war, Coppola knew when and how to start it (though never why), but had no idea how or when he would end it. And no matter how long he dragged it out, the ending would be there, waiting for him.

The final stage of the shoot was led by a Francis Ford Coppola who was fifty kilos lighter and insisted on pressing ahead despite the warning signs. The workers had dysentery almost daily, the actor who played the surfer Lance (Sam Bottoms) was always high on speed, marijuana or LSD because the whole crew partied until dawn, wild animals prowled around the camp tents at night, animal rights groups had denounced the slaughter of a water buffalo for the filming of the final scene, and United Artists intended to reduce the value of Coppola's life insurance. His life was no longer worth as much as at the start of the Apocalypse Now venture, but the film had to be finished even if it was (literally) the last thing he did. Only then would the investment be justified to the creditors. By this point, Coppola was already convinced the film would be astonishing.

When he presented it at the Cannes Film Festival, where it would go on to win the Palme d'Or despite being unfinished, Coppola drew a parallel between the shoot and the war it depicted: "We were guys with access to too much money and too much equipment, and little by little we went insane. My film is not about Vietnam; my film is Vietnam."

## [11] One from the Heart  (1332 car., ~1.3 min)

Apocalypse Now grossed five times its budget, which saved Coppola from bankruptcy, although his definitive ruin would come with One from the Heart, from nineteen eighty-two. Today, he says all the money he has is thanks to his vineyard in Napa, California. "The film isn't so strange anymore, seen today," the director reflected in twenty nineteen. "The same thing happened to it that happens to those avant-garde paintings that, over time, turn into wallpaper patterns."

Marlon Brando, until the last day of his life (he died in two thousand four), complained that Coppola was "a fat bastard" who owed him two million euros (nine million reais). Apocalypse Now took so long to shoot that, in nineteen seventy-eight, The Deer Hunter stole its honor of being the first Hollywood film about Vietnam. Before handing the Oscar to the director of The Deer Hunter, Michael Cimino (who would ruin his career two years later, also bringing about the closure of United Artists, with Heaven's Gate, from nineteen eighty), Coppola took the opportunity to issue a warning about Hollywood that was met with scorn: the press ridiculed him, concluding that he had gone definitively mad because of the Apocalypse Now shoot. What was the aberration Coppola dared to prophesy? "Get ready, because digital technology is about to change cinema forever."
