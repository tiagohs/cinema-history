Part zero five: From nineteen seventy to nineteen eighty-nine. Chapter seventeen: Canadian Cinema.

The new era of Canadian film.

## [01] David Cronenberg  (1264 car., ~1.3 min)

Canadians have to spend a lot of time patiently explaining that they are not American. Likewise, films made in Canada - with Toronto standing in for New York - are often hard to tell apart from those produced in the United States. This may be why certain Canadian directors make a point of creating works that set them apart from Hollywood's mainstream output.

Even those whose careers have every so often taken them to the Los Angeles studios, such as David Cronenberg (born in nineteen forty-three), resisted assimilation and continue to make unmistakably individual films, while the output of Canadian filmmakers such as Denys Arcand (born in nineteen forty-one), Guy Maddin (born in nineteen fifty-six) and Atom Egoyan (born in nineteen sixty) remains proudly and determinedly idiosyncratic.

David Cronenberg started out making ultra-low-budget underground films, but carried his recurring theme of "body horror" (body horror) into the sensationalist mainstream in productions such as his breakthrough film Shivers, from nineteen seventy-five. In it, as in the surreal Videodrome, from nineteen eighty-three, the theme of parasites and other elements that can invade the human body and turn against it becomes a metaphor for psychological and social dissolution.

## [02] The Fly  (1426 car., ~1.4 min)

During the nineteen eighties, his budgets grew, and with them the technical and narrative sophistication of his films, leading to his masterful reworking of the "teleportation gone wrong" story, The Fly, from nineteen eighty-six, - a rare example of a remake that completely surpassed the original - and Dead Ringers, from nineteen eighty-eight, featuring the best dual performance of Jeremy Irons's career as twin gynecologists with increasingly bizarre obsessions.

Atom Egoyan (born in nineteen sixty) likewise started small and moved progressively toward the mainstream. Drawing on his own Armenian-Canadian background and his family's turbulent history, he made a series of films in the nineteen eighties, among them Peep Show, from nineteen eighty-one, and Family Viewing, from nineteen eighty-seven, - that dealt insistently with problems of ethnic identity, dysfunctional homes, broken homes, alienation, loss and death. Alongside these themes lies an uneasy fascination with the role of visual media in the modern world. His characters obsessively use film and video to reconstruct reality so that it fits their desires - identity takes the form of a charade. Egoyan himself commented: "My films are designed to make viewers self-conscious. I take pride in that."

## [03] Tales from the Gimli Hospital  (865 car., ~0.9 min)

Unlike Cronenberg and Egoyan, Guy Maddin shows little interest in making films for the mainstream circuit. His debut feature, Tales from the Gimli Hospital, from nineteen eighty-eight, was so strange it could almost be seen as an act of contempt, a retro pastiche of silent-film stylization, with minimal dialogue, full of incoherences that provoke the viewer with a crazy logic.

Quebec filmmakers are naturally distinguished from their American colleagues by language, while at the same time remaining distant from and different to their European cousins so as not to be absorbed into the French-language circuit. Denys Arcand, the best-known French-Canadian director on the international scene, started out making short documentaries for the National Film Board of Canada, but his radical political positions and independent nature created tension with his bosses.

## [04] Gina  (1456 car., ~1.5 min)

His first three features, including Gina, from nineteen seventy-five, used the thriller to explore political themes, but his views aroused official suspicion, and for some years he was given few resources to work with. Arcand bounced back in grand style with Le déclin de l'empire américain (The Decline of the American Empire, nineteen eighty-six), which was nominated for an Oscar, as was his next film, Jésus de Montréal (Jesus of Montreal, nineteen eighty-nine), an intensely ironic fable about an actor who plays Christ in the city's annual Passion play and discovers that the role is taking control of his life.

[CITAÇÃO] Despite the fact that Videodrome came and went faster than Superman and his bullet, it is still an excellent film. It is a high-caliber genre film that raises a number of important questions.

said Christopher John on Videodrome, nineteen eighty-three.

The saddest decline in Canadian cinema was surely that of Claude Jutra (nineteen thirty to nineteen eighty-six). In nineteen seventy-one, he directed Mon oncle Antoine, from nineteen seventy-one, which received a shower of awards and was voted the best Canadian film of all time in nineteen eighty-four. But his subsequent work was disappointing. In the mid-nineteen eighties, he was diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease, and he died in nineteen eighty-six after disappearing from his home. When the St. Lawrence River thawed in April nineteen eighty-seven, his body was found in its waters.
