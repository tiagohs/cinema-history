Part zero five: From nineteen seventy to nineteen eighty-nine. Chapter two: New German Cinema.

In the nineteen seventies, the cinema of the Federal Republic of Germany saw its most exciting renewal since the nineteen twenties.

## [01] New German Cinema  (836 car., ~0.8 min)

The origins of this exciting era lay in nineteen sixty-two, when the movement known as New German Cinema emerged, following the Oberhausen Manifesto signed that year by directors such as Edgar Reitz (born in nineteen thirty-two) and Alexander Kluge (born in nineteen thirty-two).

By the late nineteen seventies, a group of German directors - among them Rainer Werner Fassbinder (nineteen forty-five to nineteen eighty-two), Wim Wenders (born in nineteen forty-five), Volker Schlöndorff (born in nineteen thirty-nine), Werner [[pron: Herzog|Hair-tsog]] (born in nineteen forty-two), Hans-Jürgen Syberberg (born in nineteen thirty-five) - had won international acclaim and fame and established New German Cinema (a term first used during the New York Film Festival in nineteen sixty-seven) as one of the most dynamic and influential forces on the world stage.

## [02] Learn more: Oberhausen Manifesto  (826 car., ~0.8 min)

Learn more: Oberhausen Manifesto.

The Oberhausen Manifesto was a declaration signed by a group of twenty-six young German filmmakers during the International Short Film Festival Oberhausen, North Rhine-Westphalia, on February twenty-eighth, nineteen sixty-two. The manifesto was a call to lay the foundations of a "new German cinema." It was initiated by Haro Senft, and among the signatories were the filmmakers Alexander Kluge and Edgar Reitz. The manifesto became associated with the motto "Papas Kino ist tot" ("Daddy's cinema is dead"), although the phrase does not appear in the manifesto.

The signatories became known as the Oberhausen group and are considered the forerunners of New German Cinema, which would begin later that same decade. The Oberhausen group was awarded the Deutscher Filmpreis in nineteen eighty-two.

## [03] Aguirre, der Zorn Gottes  (1356 car., ~1.4 min)

Critics generally attribute four essential characteristics to New German Cinema. Many of its leading names were young and had little film experience during the nineteen seventies. They followed the post-French New Wave movement in its emphasis on the director's complete control over the film and in its adoption of new, more portable technologies and leaner film crews.

They were influenced, both positively and negatively, by American cinema, classic and new alike. Finally, the radical cinema this movement represented was a reaction against the old, discredited, Nazi-compromised and commercial works of the previous generation of filmmakers (called le cinéma de papa) - typically nostalgic "Heimat" films, bland Westerns, crime thrillers in the style of Edgar Wallace, escapist romances, comedies and softcore pornography.

This supposed rebelliousness of nineteen seventies New German Cinema has been much debated by critics, who cite the apolitical and metaphysical nature of [[pron: Herzog|Hair-tsog]]'s films, such as Aguirre, der Zorn Gottes (Aguirre, the Wrath of God, nineteen seventy-two), the critique of a media outlet in Die verlorene Ehre der Katharina Blum (The Lost Honor of Katharina Blum, nineteen seventy-five), or the avoidance of direct condemnation of Nazi collaborators in films such as Deutschland bleiche Mutter (Germany Pale Mother, nineteen eighty).

## [04] Summer in the City  (1506 car., ~1.5 min)

In the early nineteen seventies, West German films still betrayed the influence of the previous decade, when filmmakers had been forced to adapt to the precarious, intellectually moribund conditions of the German film industry. This industry was still recovering from World War Two and from a reconstruction carried out under the influence and on behalf of the commercial interests of the Allies, dominated by the United States. It was also weakened by a historic low (reached in nineteen sixty-three) in attendance at the country's movie theaters and by the early popularity of television in West Germany.

At least until the Film and Television Agreement, from nineteen seventy-four, many young filmmakers worked on student productions or short films. The films were produced by the artists themselves or received federal and state subsidies; they had small budgets, were independent, mostly in black and white, and often involved formal experimentation. Among these works is Wim Wenders's first collaboration with cinematographer Robby Müller, his graduation project at the Munich Film School, Summer in the City, from nineteen seventy-two, a seductive road movie dominated by rock and pop music (The Kinks, The Lovin' Spoonful). With its existential ramblings, sense of displacement, taste for the work of American filmmakers and deeply felt evocations of time and place, it anticipated the thematic obsessions of his later work, such as Alice in den Städten (Alice in the Cities, nineteen seventy-four).

## [05] Auch Zwerge haben klein angefangen  (1238 car., ~1.2 min)

Werner [[pron: Herzog|Hair-tsog]]'s first self-produced films included the anarchic depiction of a rebellion in a community of dwarfs in Auch Zwerge haben klein angefangen (Even Dwarfs Started Small, nineteen seventy-one) and Land des Schweigens und der Dunkelheit (Land of Silence and Darkness, nineteen seventy-one), a moving documentary about Fini Straubinger, a fifty-six-year-old deaf and blind woman.

This industry was widely criticized, among other things, for its restriction of authorial control and growing censorship, a reaction that the various bodies providing subsidies after nineteen sixty-eight saw as an unacceptable anti-establishment stance on the part of filmmakers, which would reach its peak in the late nineteen seventies. In response, Wenders, [[pron: Herzog|Hair-tsog]], Fassbinder, Kluge and others formed their own production, distribution and marketing organization, the Filmverlag der Autoren, in nineteen seventy-one. Many of their best films were made under this banner, among them Aguirre, der Zorn Gottes (Aguirre, the Wrath of God, nineteen seventy-two), Jeder für sich und Gott gegen alle (The Enigma of Kaspar Hauser, nineteen seventy-four) and Die bitteren Tränen der Petra von Kant (The Bitter Tears of Petra von Kant, nineteen seventy-two).

## [06] Angst essen Seele auf  (1143 car., ~1.1 min)

As the nineteen seventies progressed, the tasks taken on by West German filmmakers - describing the realities faced by Germans in the contemporary world - became more focused or took on a more internationalist dimension. The plight of foreign workers, for example, was addressed in a series of Fassbinder films, notably and compassionately in Angst essen Seele auf (Ali: Fear Eats the Soul, nineteen seventy-four). The role of women is examined in films such as Die allseitig reduzierte Persönlichkeit (The All-Around Reduced Personality: Redupers, nineteen seventy-eight), by Helke Sander (born in nineteen thirty-seven), and Das zweite Erwachen der Christa Klages (The Second Awakening of Christa Klages, nineteen seventy-eight), by Margarethe von Trotta (born in nineteen forty-two).

## [07] Golden Bear  (1000 car., ~1.0 min)

The year nineteen seventy-seven was pivotal both for the West German state and for New German Cinema. It witnessed the kidnapping and death of industrialist Hanns-Martin Schleyer, the suicide of Gudrun Ensslin and the deaths of other members of the Red Army Faction/Baader-Meinhof group in Stammheim prison - events generally considered the end of the so-called decade of terror in Germany. In the sweeping Deutschland im Herbst (Germany in Autumn, nineteen seventy-eight), directors such as Fassbinder, Reitz and Schlöndorff offered an immediate response to these events.

New German Cinema reached its highest degree of recognition in this period. Palermo oder Wolfsburg (Palermo or Wolfsburg, nineteen eighty), by Werner Schroeter (nineteen forty-five to twenty ten), won the Golden Bear at the Berlin Film Festival. Die Blechtrommel (The Tin Drum, nineteen seventy-nine), Schlöndorff's adaptation of the work of Günter Grass, won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film in nineteen eighty.

## [08] Das Boot  (1440 car., ~1.4 min)

The following year, an adaptation of Lothar Buchheim's submarine novel, Das Boot, from nineteen eighty-one, directed by Wolfgang Petersen (born in nineteen forty-one), achieved national and international success. However, this happened precisely when the critical and dissenting voices that had so recently helped define New German Cinema found themselves marginalized, with formal experimentation halted and its filmmakers encouraged or forced to take on acceptable projects capable of enhancing or "culturally legitimizing" the international image of German cinema.

Some gave in to the demands of foreign investors. Others, like Wenders and [[pron: Herzog|Hair-tsog]], went to other countries to keep making films. Still, the continuity of the rich and persistent collaboration between film and television in Germany deserves mention. In nineteen eighty, Fassbinder, undoubtedly the most original, prolific and influential director of New German Cinema, made his masterpiece: a moving screen adaptation of his great passion - Alfred Döblin's finest novel, Berlin Alexanderplatz, from nineteen twenty-nine, shot in Bavaria to be broadcast on the public television channel ARD. Fassbinder died in nineteen eighty-two, a date that can serve as a marker, perhaps a somewhat belated one, of the end of a period that included the bold flowering of German cinema in the nineteen seventies and the decline of the country's film production in the period that followed.
