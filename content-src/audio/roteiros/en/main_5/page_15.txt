In one of the first scenes of the nineteen eighty-eight film Who Framed Roger Rabbit, the human protagonist, detective Eddie Valiant, played by Bob Hoskins (born in nineteen forty-two), is drinking at a bar when he is approached by a local jerk. The guy starts needling Valiant about his choice of clients, asking who he was working for that week: "Goofy? Or Screwy Squirrel?". Valiant kicks his tormentor's stool out from under him. Gripping the man's head in his hands and shaking with rage, he growls: "Get this, meatball: I - don't - work - for - toons!".

But, as you probably know, not only does Valiant work for toons, but the success of Who Framed Roger Rabbit soon made the rest of Hollywood want to work for them too. When that film cast human actors alongside a bunch of rubbery, spring-loaded, violent cartoon characters, it was taking a huge risk: the film went about twenty-eight million dollars over budget, attempting technical feats of animation that had never been done before to make the cartoons and humans look as if they were really shooting and grabbing each other. But it paid off, grossing one hundred fifty-six million dollars and winning three Oscars.

Roger Rabbit was far from the first film to feature cartoons and humans crossing the, well, color line. Cab Calloway (nineteen oh-seven to nineteen ninety-four) made appearances in Betty Boop cartoons such as Minnie the Moocher, from nineteen thirty-two, some of which opened with live-action footage of the Cotton Club bandleader dancing before revealing a version of him rotoscoped into an animated ghost. By the nineteen forties, animation technology was good enough for humans to share the screen with cartoons, so Gene Kelly could tap-dance with Jerry the mouse in Anchors Aweigh, from nineteen forty-five. But Roger Rabbit broke new ground, even beyond Jessica Rabbit's curves: it proved to film studios not only that audiences loved human-cartoon mash-ups, but that cartoons in general could make a lot of money.

Imitators followed, and joint cartoon-human productions became a fruitful genre between the late nineteen eighties and the mid-nineteen nineties. Like Roger Rabbit, these subsequent productions were tinged with racial messages, the tension (sexual or otherwise) between flesh and blood / pen and ink flickering with the danger and thrill of miscegenation. In the nineteen sixties, teen movies had Frankie Avalon (born in nineteen forty), Annette Funicello (born in nineteen forty-two), lots of sand (and no sex) and upbeat music, while in the nineteen seventies being a teenager essentially meant getting slaughtered in your bed in a string of horror films. But in nineteen eighty-four, writer-director John Hughes revolutionized the genre with Sixteen Candles, which he followed the next year with The Breakfast Club, a film whose great achievement was finally showing teenagers as they really talk and think.

No other film summed up the period better, or became more of a cult favorite among teenagers, than The Breakfast Club. It made Hughes a name to watch closely - he went on to make the excellent Ferris Bueller's Day Off, from nineteen eighty-six, and the hugely successful Home Alone, from nineteen ninety, - and it brought together a talented group of young actors (just as Francis Ford Coppola's The Outsiders had done two years earlier) whom the media dubbed the "Brat Pack".

Five very different teenagers are suspended and must serve Saturday detention for various reasons. Despite their differences when the day begins, they end up bonding with one another. We have the school jock (Emilio Estevez), the weirdo with too much eyeliner (Ally Sheedy), the stuck-up princess (Molly Ringwald, teen idol and Hughes's muse ever since she starred in Sixteen Candles), the nerd (Anthony Michael Hall) and the rebel (Judd Nelson), all trying to keep themselves entertained under the watchful eye of the complex teacher Mr. Vernon (Paul Gleason).

This film, which relies on a screenplay of witty one-liners rather than on action, works largely thanks to the cast's characterizations as truths are revealed, resentments are voiced and each of them gets a turn to shine. Although copied in films and TV series, it may well be the definitive teen movie, whether for its perfect timing, its sharp performances, its quotable lines or its time-capsule-worthy nineteen eighties soundtrack, which includes the classic "Don't You Forget About Me" by Simple Minds, written for the film.
