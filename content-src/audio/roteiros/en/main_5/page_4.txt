Part zero five: From nineteen seventy to nineteen eighty-nine. Chapter four: seventies Crime Films.

The nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties were troubled years in American life, with serial killers and the end of an unpopular war. And that was reflected in the crime films released during the period.

## [01] Francis Ford Coppola  (903 car., ~0.9 min)

The nineteen seventies began with the trial of Charles Manson for the murders that shook the Hollywood elite. The Watergate break-in permanently stained the highest office in the land, and the unpopular Vietnam War ended with the humiliating evacuation of American forces from Saigon. Like the era to which they belonged, crime films were characterized by disillusionment, distrust of authority, and paranoia.

Three of the era's classics denounce the nation's moral bankruptcy through the lens of history. Francis Ford Coppola (born in nineteen thirty-nine) gave the world The Godfather, from nineteen seventy-two, and its sequel The Godfather Part Two, from nineteen seventy-four, and, in doing so, reinvented the gangster film as grand opera. Both films place the Mafia squarely at the center of American life and, coincidentally, gave new meaning to one of the most American of concepts, "the family."

## [02] Learn more: Building the Myth of the Mobster with The Godfather (1)  (1603 car., ~1.6 min)

Learn more: Building the Myth of the Mobster with The Godfather.

The youngest son, Michael Corleone (Al Pacino), returns from World War Two outside the family business: organized crime. When his father, Don Corleone (Marlon Brando), is gunned down, however, Michael is driven to carry out a vendetta, a retaliatory killing, bound by blood and "honor" to a violent chain of events of power and survival in the underworld. Ultimately, Michael inherits the role of head of the family, shutting the door on his uncomprehending non-Italian wife (Diane Keaton) as he receives homage as the new "Godfather," or boss.

The film's dialogue and characters immediately entered the collective imagination of moviegoers. Al Pacino (born in nineteen forty) and James Caan (born in nineteen forty) (the hotheaded older brother Sonny) became stars, and the film won the Oscars for Best Picture and Best Screenplay, as well as Best Actor for Brando, in a triumphant comeback. In adapting Mario Puzo's bestseller, Francis Ford Coppola made a gangster opera in the pulp fiction style, an epic about patriarchy, family and America itself. Protests of defamation from Italian Americans were swept aside by the film's immense popularity. Every descendant of immigrants looked on with nostalgia at the Corleone clan making pasta, celebrating and grieving together. The anecdotes, footnotes and postscripts are now part of the folklore - Brando didn't stuff his cheeks with cotton but wore resin pads attached to his molars, and he sent a fake Native American, "Sacheen Littlefeather," to the Oscars ceremony to decline his award.

## [03] Learn more: Building the Myth of the Mobster with The Godfather (2)  (981 car., ~1.0 min)

Coppola set many of the rules of the game for nineteen seventies cinema with his technical mastery. The audacious, visceral and grandiose set pieces are now legendary - the horse's head in the bed, the slaughter of Sonny, the scenes cutting between a sunny garden wedding party and Don Corleone's entourage inside the house, and the stunning finale with murders being carried out during the baptism of the new Corleone (in effect, the rites of consecration for Michael as he assumes the role of the new boss). The film's best qualities reveal Coppola's fluency in the classics, in pulp, film noir and social dramas, but a persistent criticism of The Godfather is that it glorifies the Mafia. Pacino's Michael is the film's hero, and Michael is not a good guy. Even so, its mythic exploration of family ties, even when they are cursed by blood and ambition, keeps drawing viewers in with the idea that having a family (even a Mafia one) is still better than having no family at all.

## [04] Chinatown  (1281 car., ~1.3 min)

Chinatown, from nineteen seventy-four, directed by Roman Polanski (born in nineteen thirty-three), also investigates the criminal foundations of American society, with a story of murder and incest that unfolds against a backdrop of endemic corruption during the growth of Los Angeles.

Lack of faith in the law is the theme of many crime films of the era. Dirty Harry, from nineteen seventy-one, by director Don Siegel (nineteen twelve to nineteen ninety-one), features detective Harry Callahan, played by Clint Eastwood (born in nineteen thirty), doing as he pleases on the streets of San Francisco while hunting a sadistic serial killer.

This film is not an endorsement of vigilante justice (as its liberal critics claim). Its theme is the destructive synergy between the cop and the psychopath. In The French Connection, from nineteen seventy-one, director William Friedkin (born in nineteen thirty-five) presents the law in the form of Popeye Doyle, a charmless, foul-mouthed cop who contrasts with the polished, aristocratic kingpin he is trying to bring down. Based on true events, it shows the police and the criminals operating on a basis of moral equivalence.

## [05] The Long Goodbye  (1277 car., ~1.3 min)

In The Long Goodbye, from nineteen seventy-three, by Robert Altman (nineteen twenty-five to two thousand six), private detective Philip Marlowe, a kind of knight-errant created by Raymond Chandler, is given a makeover for the nineteen seventies. Elliott Gould's Marlowe is a man out of his time, chain-smoking in a Hollywood where a gangster's preferred method of extracting information is to stage an impromptu nudist encounter. Screenwriter Leigh Brackett (nineteen fifteen to nineteen seventy-eight) had worked on an earlier adaptation of Chandler's work, The Big Sleep, from nineteen forty-six, and her screenplay for Altman captures the cadences of the nineteen seventies cult of individualism.

In his essay The 'Me' Decade and the Third Great Awakening, from nineteen seventy-six, Tom Wolfe defined the solipsism of the era. He could just as well have been writing about the Los Angeles of The Long Goodbye.

Clint Eastwood made his directorial debut with Play Misty for Me, from nineteen seventy-one, a film attuned to the dictates of the "Me" decade, transforming his screen persona: instead of an action hero, he plays a smug late-night radio disc jockey. A smooth-jazz-fueled seducer, he is stunned when the fan he carelessly takes to bed turns into a homicidal stalker.

## [06] Learn more: The Revival of Mafia Films (1)  (2306 car., ~2.3 min)

Learn more: The Revival of Mafia Films.

In the nineteen seventies, there was a resurgence of mafia films, most notably The Godfather. It was followed by two sequels: The Godfather Part Two, from nineteen seventy-four, and The Godfather Part Three, from nineteen ninety, and it also inspired other mafia films, such as The Valachi Papers, from nineteen seventy-two.

The first Godfather film portrayed a dichotomy of values between the first-generation Italian-American Vito Corleone, who still follows the traditional mafioso code of honor, and his Americanized son Michael, who over the course of the films loses all his moral principles as greed consumes him. The Godfather films also offered a perverted version of the famous "American Dream," as the Corleones rose to power and wealth through entirely illegal and brutal means. In the late nineteen sixties and early nineteen seventies, events such as the Vietnam War and various social protest movements caused many Americans to lose faith in traditional American values, and so the distorted, dark version of the theme held particular appeal when the film was released in nineteen seventy-two. Inspired by the films' worldwide success in the nineteen seventies, Italian films portrayed mafiosi as antiheroes, men who lived by crime but possessed a certain nobility and greatness of character.

The nineteen eighty-three remake of Scarface, Scarface, from nineteen eighty-three, was not particularly well received at the time of its release, but over time it came to be seen as a classic of mafia films, serving as inspiration for films such as King of New York, from nineteen ninety. Sergio Leone (nineteen twenty-nine to nineteen eighty-nine) filmed an epic crime drama, Once Upon a Time in America, from nineteen eighty-four, starring Robert De Niro (born in nineteen forty-three) and James Woods (born in nineteen forty-seven). Although the release of the "US Cut" was a commercial and critical failure, the releases of the "European Cut" and "Director's Cut" were both critical successes and restored its publicity and reputation. The Untouchables, from nineteen eighty-seven, directed by Brian De Palma (born in nineteen forty), presented a largely fictionalized version of Eliot Ness's efforts to enforce the law and bring down Al Capone's "Outfit."

## [07] Learn more: The Revival of Mafia Films (2)  (1287 car., ~1.3 min)

The nineteen eighties saw La Mattanza ("The Slaughter") in Italy, as the Corleonesi family wiped out its rivals, leading to more than one thousand gang murders in nineteen eighty-one to nineteen eighty-three, and the Mafia also murdered magistrates, police officers, and journalists with impunity. In the nineteen eighties and nineteen nineties, the general feeling in Italy was that the Mafia was out of control. As a consequence, Italian cinema in the nineteen eighties and nineteen nineties turned to civil cinema, a series of realistic and courageous films that portrayed the Mafia as an unstoppable force that had completely corrupted almost everyone and murdered the few honest people willing to stand up to it. Films and TV series such as La piovra, from nineteen eighty-four, La scorta, from nineteen ninety-three, and Palermo-Milano sola andata, from nineteen ninety-five, had as their heroes a lone police officer or magistrate, the only one to cling to his honesty and integrity, who faced not only the Mafia's killers but also his corrupt superiors and the prevailing apathy of Italian society.

The following decade would continue with critically acclaimed mafia film productions, with Martin Scorsese leading most of the big hits, among them GoodFellas, from nineteen ninety.

## [08] Blaxploitation  (803 car., ~0.8 min)

The rise of Black consciousness in the nineteen seventies was reflected in the blaxploitation phenomenon (exploitation of Black stereotypes), with films aimed at Black audiences. The tone for the genre was set by Shaft, from nineteen seventy-one, - directed by African American filmmaker Gordon Parks (nineteen twelve to two thousand six) and starring a charismatic detective - and by the more ambiguous Super Fly, from nineteen seventy-two, directed by Parks's son, Gordon Parks Jr. (nineteen thirty-four to nineteen seventy-nine), with a ghetto drug dealer as its hero. Both films feature revolutionary soundtracks, as successful as the films themselves - Shaft, by Isaac Hayes (nineteen forty-two to two thousand eight), and Super Fly, by Curtis Mayfield (nineteen forty-two to nineteen ninety-nine).

## [09] Race with the Devil  (1096 car., ~1.1 min)

In a decade that began with the trial of Charles Manson and witnessed the mass suicide of Jim Jones's cult, it was possible to believe the devil was on the loose, a mood well captured by the occult-tinged road movie Race with the Devil, from nineteen seventy-five. In this exercise in post-Manson paranoia, two couples on vacation in an RV witness a human sacrifice and are pursued by members of a devil-worshipping cult. It shows the American Southwest in the grip of a satanic conspiracy.

Although both the nineteen sixties and the nineteen eighties produced excellent crime films, the profusion of original works capturing the mood of the times made the nineteen seventies the golden age of the genre. Despite the recent trend toward remaking classics, the DNA of nineteen seventies crime thrillers has occasionally been used to create original works of superior quality.
