Part zero five: From nineteen seventy to nineteen eighty-nine. Chapter twelve: African Cinema.

The boom of African cinema in the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties.

## [01] Ousmane Sembène  (1014 car., ~1.0 min)

African cinema in the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties witnessed an explosion of invention and creativity that rivaled any other cinematic renaissance the world has seen before or since. Directors were energized by the opportunities, the energy and the politics of the postcolonial era to create increasingly singular and ambitious films. Yet, compared with the films of the French New Wave, they seem underrated today.

Often referred to as "the father of African film," Senegalese writer and director Ousmane Sembène (nineteen twenty-three to two thousand seven) embodied the new urgency of postcolonial Africa. In nineteen forty-seven, Sembène moved to France, where he worked as a docker in Marseille, joined the Communist Party and became a militant trade unionist. His cinema showed the same political and social engagement he embraced in his life, and his inspired debut came with La noire de... (Black Girl, nineteen sixty-six), the first feature by a Black African to gain international recognition.

## [02] Fespaco  (1744 car., ~1.7 min)

Unique among the emerging directors of the nineteen seventies was Senegal's Djibril Diop Mambéty (nineteen forty-five to nineteen ninety-eight), whose debut feature, Touki Bouki, from nineteen seventy-three, captured the voice of African youth and brought a dazzling, experimental touch to African cinema. Writer Manthia Diawara wrote that it "tears through the screen with fantasies of African modernity as never seen before in cinema," and critic Mark Cousins described it as "the African equivalent of Easy Rider, from nineteen sixty-nine, or À bout de souffle (Breathless, nineteen sixty)." The film also has a political edge, following a young couple who dream of leaving Senegal and embark on a series of adventures to get the money they need, but who decide, at the last minute, that they cannot leave their homeland.

Inspired by the example of Sembene and also by the creation of two major African film festivals - Carthage, in Tunisia, in nineteen sixty-six, and Fespaco (the Panafrican Film and Television Festival), in Burkina Faso, in nineteen sixty-nine - there was a palpable sense of promise surrounding African cinema at the dawn of the nineteen seventies. Electrified by writers such as Frantz Fanon, who argued in The Wretched of the Earth that national liberation would only be possible after the psychological liberation of individuals, the early nineteen seventies saw pioneering directors such as Med Hondo (born in nineteen thirty-six), from Mauritania and based in Paris, go beyond simply imitating Western films to arrive at a cinema with a distinctly African voice that addressed the issues affecting its people.

## [03] Mirt sost shi amit  (890 car., ~0.9 min)

In East Africa, Mirt sost shi amit (Harvest: three thousand Years, nineteen seventy-six) by Ethiopian Haile Gerima (born in nineteen forty-six) was a landmark, a furious and formally radical denunciation of the oppression suffered by Ethiopia's peasants at the hands of landowners, with characters who symbolized the relationship between Africa and the West. At the same time, in North Africa, filmmakers such as Tunisian Abdellatif Ben Ammar (born in nineteen forty-three) were also producing challenging films, such as the award-winning Aziza, from nineteen eighty. Chronique des années de braise (Chronicle of the Years of Fire, nineteen seventy-five), by Mohammed Lakhdar Hamina (born in nineteen thirty-four), portrayed Algeria's struggle to free itself from French colonial rule. The film created a new awareness of Algerian cinema, winning the Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival.

## [04] Soleil O  (1446 car., ~1.4 min)

If Western and Asian cinema have their roots in literature and theater, African directors found inspiration by looking to the African tradition of oral storytelling. Films such as Hondo's documentary Soleil O, from nineteen seventy-three, about immigrants in France, and Emitaï, from nineteen seventy-one, about the conflict between the French army and Senegalese villagers at the end of World War Two, employed key devices of that tradition, such as flashbacks and out-of-sequence narratives.

Following in the footsteps of Sembene and Hondo, directors emerged from all over sub-Saharan Africa, people like Ababacar Samb-Makharam (nineteen thirty-four to nineteen eighty-seven), director of Kodou, from nineteen seventy, or Safi Faye (born in nineteen forty-three), also from Senegal, who became the first woman film director in sub-Saharan Africa with the feature Kaddu Beykat (Letter from My Village, nineteen seventy-five), a docufiction told through a letter narrated by Faye herself. The film is set in the village where Faye was born in Senegal and focuses on the daily life and customs of its inhabitants, while also telling the story of a young couple who want to marry but cannot - because a drought has ruined his crops, the man cannot pay his bride's dowry. With independence still recent and postcolonial governments across Africa sinking into corruption, the focus of nineteen seventies African cinema tended to remain on the present.

## [05] Xala  (2282 car., ~2.3 min)

The films of this period covered a range of controversial and topical subjects. Sembène's Xala, from nineteen seventy-five, was cut by Senegalese censors because of its scathing commentary on the new post-independence elites, who had simply replaced the abuses of the colonial rulers with abuses of their own. The ambitious Ceddo, from nineteen seventy-seven, although set in the nineteenth century, had a clear message to convey about contemporary Africa by telling the story of African traditions under threat from outside forces.

The lyrical Muna Moto, from nineteen seventy-five, by Jean-Pierre Dikongué-Pipa (born in nineteen forty) of Cameroon, followed Kaddu Beykat in criticizing the system of putting a price on brides. And an important new voice emerged with director Souleymane Cissé (born in nineteen forty) of Mali, who, like Sembène, had studied in Moscow. Cissé's second feature, Baara (Work, nineteen seventy-eight), explored the daily lives of workers at a textile factory in Bamako and revealed Cissé's Marxist background in its portrayal of an engineer who challenges the factory owners. The film won the top prize at Fespaco, as did his next one, Finyé (The Wind, nineteen eighty-two), which dealt with class and generational conflict and showed students who dared to challenge the authority of the military government.

If contemporary reality and its themes dominated African cinema in the nineteen seventies, the following decade saw its directors turn their gaze to the precolonial past in search of inspiration. This shift gave rise to a slower-paced cinema, rooted more specifically in African myths than in the furious statements of the previous decade. The conditions for this came from Burkina Faso, a small West African country. As host of the Fespaco festival, it had already had an impact on African cinema out of all proportion to its status as one of the poorest nations on the continent.

## [06] Wend Kuuni  (866 car., ~0.9 min)

However, in the early nineteen eighties, with the support of the nation's young and charismatic leader, Thomas Sankara, the country began producing films that had a crucial influence on the development of African cinema. Wend Kuuni (God's Gift, nineteen eighty-two), by Gaston Kaboré (born in nineteen fifty-one), was the great landmark. Set in the precolonial era, it revolves around a mute boy found alone in the bush and adopted by villagers. Kaboré's next film, Zan Boko, from nineteen eighty-eight, implicitly criticized the political situation in Burkina Faso at the time. Kaboré inspired other directors, among them his fellow countryman Idrissa Ouedraogo (born in nineteen fifty-one), who made his own "village" films, including the acclaimed Yaaba, from nineteen eighty-nine, in which a ten-year-old boy befriends an elderly woman who has become an outcast.

## [07] Yeelen  (1648 car., ~1.6 min)

The most celebrated African film of the nineteen eighties also follows the example of Wend Kuuni. With the extraordinary Yeelen, from nineteen eighty-seven, Souleymane Cissé left behind the contemporary realism of Baara and Finyé and told a story of myth, magic and ritual set in eleventh-century Mali, based on tales passed down through oral tradition. The film has some of the most beautiful cinematography in world cinema. Other notable African films of the period include Med Hondo's epic Sarraounia, from nineteen eighty-six. In this historical drama, Hondo immortalized one of the few African tribal leaders to oppose French expansionism - Sarraounia, of the Azna - who in eighteen ninety-nine fought the better-equipped colonial military forces with guerrilla tactics.

Hondo's film was acclaimed by critics and won the top prize at Fespaco. The nineteen eighties also saw the arrival of a new generation from sub-Saharan Africa, among them the great Malian director Cheick Oumar Sissoko (born in nineteen forty-five), whose first feature, Nyamanton (The Garbage Boys, nineteen eighty-six), portrays the lives of street children in Bamako. In Burkina Faso, S. Pierre Yaméogo (born in nineteen fifty-five) also emerged as a name to watch.

These talents ensured that African cinema remained a vital presence on the international scene during the nineteen nineties, a period that also saw the flourishing of a direct-to-video film industry in the former British colonies of Ghana and Nigeria - countries where cinema had until then been hampered by a lack of financial and structural support similar to what France had offered its former colonies.
