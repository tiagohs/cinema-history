## [01] French New Wave  (1621 car., ~1.6 min)

The seventies were a fertile era for horror. Subversive, nihilistic, shockingly immediate with its camera vérité, George A. Romero's (born in nineteen forty) apocalyptic zombie film Night of the Living Dead, from nineteen sixty-eight, suggested that something was very wrong in the United States, and a new generation of filmmakers didn't exactly want to continue the earlier work, preferring to scorch the whole territory.

"You watch these films and you know you're in the hands of a maniac," said John Landis (born in nineteen fifty), referring to the dangerous and singular output of Wes Craven (born in nineteen thirty-nine), David Cronenberg (born in nineteen forty-three), Brian De Palma (born in nineteen forty), David Lynch (born in nineteen forty-six), and John Carpenter (born in nineteen forty-eight). This was a new breed of creators, influenced by films such as Psycho, from nineteen sixty, and Rosemary's Baby, from nineteen sixty-eight, as well as by the French New Wave and the sociopolitical and economic situation of the time.

## [02] Learn more: The Resurrection of Monster Movies with Alien  (2615 car., ~2.6 min)

"In space no one can hear you scream." The tagline on the Alien poster was an obvious come-on, but there was plenty of screaming in theaters. Defying the popularity of Star Wars, from nineteen seventy-seven, Ridley Scott (born in nineteen thirty-seven) resurrected the cheap genre of scary monsters from outer space, paired it with a meticulous, big-budget look and created a paralyzing science fiction horror film capable of leaving nerves completely frayed.

Dan O'Bannon's sophisticated screenplay owes a great deal to the ingenious nineteen fifties B movie Giant from the Unknown, from nineteen fifty-eight, and follows the "haunted house" formula of The Cat and the Canary, from nineteen thirty-nine, or Ten Little Indians, from nineteen sixty-five, but set aboard a spaceship and with a much greater gross-out factor.

The Nostromo is a gigantic cargo ship operating in deep space, with a minimal (and far from conventional) crew led by Captain Dallas (Tom Skerritt) that includes the smart Lieutenant Ripley (Sigourney Weaver) and Jones the cat. Responding to a distress call, they discover an abandoned alien ship and strange eggs. As they examine the eggs, a parasite leaps out of one and latches onto Kane's (John Hurt) helmet, infecting him and, a few days later, wreaking terrifying havoc when a baby alien bursts out of his chest during breakfast. The other actors supposedly didn't know what was going to happen, which made their shock and revulsion genuine.

Scott creates a frisson and uses the dark corridors dramatically as the dwindling crew - who apparently never watched a horror film as teenagers - walks straight into the monster's pair of extendable jaws. What raised the anguish to an art form, through Oscar-winning visual effects, was the unparalleled art direction and design, blending organic and metallic patterns, as well as the (only glimpsed) alien designed by the artist H. R. Giger, whose terrible beauty was given an eerie grace by the statuesque Maasai dancer Bolaji Badejo.

Weaver became a star and an icon overnight as the brave survivor Ripley, facing her final battle in nothing but a T-shirt and underwear. She was supposed to end the film naked, to emphasize human fragility in the face of the perfect killing machine, but Twentieth Century Fox, eager to avoid an "R" rating (restricted for those under seventeen) in the United States, vetoed the idea. Unusually for a horror franchise, the sequels were handed to directors famous for their distinctive visual styles - James Cameron, David Fincher and Jean-Pierre Jeunet.

## [04] Learn more: The Horror Film Phenomenon  (1890 car., ~1.9 min)

Learn more: The Horror Film Phenomenon.

This film, the first blockbuster in the history of horror cinema, had an enormous influence on the future of the genre. Never before had a horror film been the object of so much attention prior to its release, so much gossip about conflicts during post-production, so much speculation about why people of all ages stood in line for hours to see a work supposedly capable of inducing fits of vomiting, fainting, and even temporary psychosis. It is hard to convey the true cultural impact of The Exorcist. The film stole headlines from the Watergate scandal, at least for a while caused a considerable rise in the number of "real" cases of demonic possession and, as one critic wrote, "established grossness as a form of mass entertainment".

Inspired by newspaper reports about a thirteen-year-old Maryland boy whose body had supposedly been possessed by demonic forces, novelist William Peter Blatty turned the possessed boy into a girl. Blatty embellished many details, adding heavy doses of philosophical and theological speculation about the nature of evil, and the result was the nineteen seventy-one book The Exorcist. Anticipating a bestseller, Warner Bros. bought the film rights, and after several revisions of the original screenplay, Blatty finally produced a version of The Exorcist that lived up to Friedkin's exacting standards.

In an era of student protests, experimental drug use, and the questioning of authority, The Exorcist let viewers revel in the horrific punishments inflicted on the rebellious Regan. But by making demon-Regan so fascinating to watch, so full of indecent surprises, The Exorcist also gave the audience the pleasure of rebellion. The film not only spawned a series of inferior sequels, imitations, and variations on the theme of possession, but also made children with evil powers a dominant theme in horror cinema.

## [05] Slasher  (1677 car., ~1.7 min)

Brian De Palma (born in nineteen forty) created a landmark (and one of the best adaptations to date of a Stephen King novel) with Carrie, from nineteen seventy-six. It was high-end horror for audiences who would rather have eaten their own foot than watch repulsive cannibal films or ghastly low-budget Italian productions.

Horror blockbusters kept appearing throughout the nineteen eighties, as studios softened the scares and added humor, special effects and fun. The result was family-friendly works such as Poltergeist, from nineteen eighty-two, and Ghostbusters, from nineteen eighty-four, which proved to be a good alternative to the repetitive, gory films that followed Halloween, from nineteen seventy-eight.

In other cases, the advances in transformation effects achieved by Rick Baker (born in nineteen fifty) in An American Werewolf in London, from nineteen eighty-one, and by Rob Bottin (born in nineteen fifty-nine) in The Thing, from nineteen eighty-two, showed that the splatter still mattered. A handful of smart, subversive horror films also appeared, among them The Fly, from nineteen eighty-six, seen by many as a commentary on the fear of the AIDS epidemic, and Clive Barker's (born in nineteen fifty-two) sadomasochistic fantasy Hellraiser, from nineteen eighty-seven.

Behind her came the killer with a bloody machete and a mask covering his face. She was just one more victim of one more psychopath in one more slasher film that left its mark on the horror genre.

## [06] Le manoir du diable  (888 car., ~0.9 min)

Since its inception, cinema has always flirted with the fantastic and with fear. The first horror film is called Le manoir du diable, from eighteen ninety-six, and was directed by Georges [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] (eighteen sixty-one to nineteen thirty-eight); already at this early stage one notices a strong influence of the fantastic and the personification of an evil figure that tends to have the upper hand over the heroes. Along with the use of a menacing villain, it also became a rule to build a setting that did nothing to make the protagonists' lives easier, as can be seen in the aforementioned [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] work and also in the Italian film L'inferno (Dante's Inferno, nineteen eleven), whose adaptation of Dante Alighieri's journey through the circles of hell featured a set design unique for its time, making skillful use of entirely new camera shots as well as illusionism and lighting techniques.

## [07] Louis Feuillade  (944 car., ~0.9 min)

However, in the early twentieth century, some filmmakers realized that the newborn horror genre didn't need to be tied to the fantastic. Just as small pamphlets about crimes were popular on English streets, German and French filmmakers experimented with adapting urban violence for the screen. From nineteen fifteen to nineteen sixteen, Louis Feuillade's (eighteen seventy-three to nineteen twenty-five) film serial Les Vampires (The Vampires or, The Arch Criminals of Paris, nineteen fifteen) set out to tell the story of a gang of house burglars in Paris who dressed as bats and stole jewelry, even committing a few murders.

The Italian subgenre known as giallo, in the nineteen sixties and seventies, changed the entire aesthetic of what was understood by films involving murders. For the first time, a series of separate films established that there would be rules in a world of killings, rules that would become the basis for slasher films.

Director Mario Bava (nineteen fourteen to nineteen eighty) released in nineteen sixty-three the first example of the genre, La ragazza che sapeva troppo (The Girl Who Knew Too Much, nineteen sixty-three), a thriller in which a young American woman visits a friend in Rome and becomes an eyewitness to a murder, which culminates in an investigation she undertakes herself in search of the killer. The following year, Bava released Sei donne per l'assassino (Blood and Black Lace, nineteen sixty-four) and once again invoked the rule of a mysterious killer who piles up bodies over the course of the film.

It was then clear that the ground was ready for a new subgenre to emerge. The year nineteen seventy-four became special for one filmmaker in particular. Tobe Hooper (nineteen forty-three to twenty seventeen) had been an active member of the hippie movement in the sixties, and his first feature, Eggshells, from nineteen seventy-one, was well received for exploring the relationship of the youth of the time with drug use and sex, being regarded as a tribute to the end of the movement.

## [09] Learn more: Halloween – The Landmark of Slasher Films  (2462 car., ~2.5 min)

Learn more: Halloween, The Landmark of Slasher Films.

No director since Alfred Hitchcock (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty) has captured the delicious voyeurism of horror as well as John Carpenter does in Halloween, a film so full of our most primal anxieties that it still defines the genre twenty years later. Indeed, the comparison between Hitchcock and Carpenter is no exaggeration: Halloween is full of Carpenter's tributes to Hitchcock, from the characters' names - Sam Loomis and Tom Doyle, from Psycho, from nineteen sixty, and Rear Window, from nineteen fifty-four, respectively - to the casting of Jamie Lee Curtis (born in nineteen fifty-eight), daughter of Janet Leigh (nineteen twenty-seven to two thousand four), the unfortunate shower victim of Psycho.

Halloween's plot is quite simple: a masked psychopath, Michael Myers, stabs and strangles his way through a group of teenage friends as they drink beer and chase after sex, until he finally comes up against the film's great pure force, the virginal babysitter Laurie Strode (Curtis). Despite its minimalism, the evident social anxieties of good versus evil in Halloween's plot are left unresolved at the end, a strategy repeated consistently in other slashers ranging from Friday the thirteenth, from nineteen eighty, to Scream, from nineteen ninety-six. The intensity of Carpenter and of Debra Hill's (nineteen fifty, two thousand five) screenplay lies in placing horror in the placid setting of suburban American life, where young people are presumed (or at least were once presumed) to be safe.

Deconstructing the notion of the home as a protected place, Halloween's camerawork invades the fictional town of Haddonfield with a roaming point-of-view shot. The film opens with a brilliant shot showing everything through the eyes of the killer, then six years old. Carpenter builds a purely aesthetic fear, setting Halloween apart from its own tiresomely expository offspring. Throughout the film, someone is always watching, whether predator or prey. As the film unfolds, we find ourselves lurking around corners, behind shoulders, through windows and through cracks in closet doors, waiting for what we believe to be certain death.

The brilliant pacing and excruciatingly long takes keep the adrenaline high as Michael slinks through the shadows, cheating death several times to stalk the smart Laurie. When the film ends, we are left anxiously wondering what separates fact from fiction.

## [10] US$300,000  (1348 car., ~1.4 min)

In 'seventy-four, he released The Texas Chain Saw Massacre, from nineteen seventy-four, on a budget of three hundred thousand dollars, earning a return of thirty million eight hundred fifty-nine thousand dollars, establishing not only an excellent financial return but also the rules for what would become the slasher, rules that were in turn inherited, with some modifications, from Mario Bava's giallo.

The Texas Chain Saw Massacre defined the following rules: the serial killer would keep his identity secret. His methods of murder would not be limited to simply stabbing. The victims would be mostly young people constantly using drugs or having sex (the symbolism of lost purity and the punishment represented by the killer are very strong concepts in the early drafts of the Massacre screenplay), and the location of the murders would vary between isolated spaces, such as forests and farms, or urban ones, such as houses or schools. One of the victims would always survive at the end.

It was during the seventies that some of the best examples of the genre were produced, generating revenue and, consequently, more productions. Halloween, from nineteen seventy-eight, by John Carpenter (born in nineteen forty-eight), would become established as one of the best horror films of the subgenre, with Michael Myers piling up bodies in Haddonfield.

## [11] Heyday of the slasher subgenre  (1218 car., ~1.2 min)

Alice, Sweet Alice, from nineteen seventy-six, would feature one of the forerunners among cinema's serial killers to wear a stylized outfit (despite the obscurity into which the film fell), sporting a yellow raincoat and a translucent Halloween mask. Black Christmas, from nineteen seventy-four, would break some of the rules set by Massacre by setting its story inside a college sorority house, where a maniac who has escaped from an asylum hides inside the house, shattering the sense of everyday safety. When a Stranger Calls, from nineteen seventy-nine, would bring back this concept in one of the most frightening opening sequences in history, which has also fallen into obscurity.

Then came the eighties, and with them the heyday of the slasher subgenre, packing movie theaters and video stores with all kinds of stories that, despite oozing creativity in their kills and sometimes in their violence, increasingly lacked budget and therefore relied on weak casts and ever more amateurish productions.

## [12] Jason Voorhees  (867 car., ~0.9 min)

The release of Friday the thirteenth, from nineteen eighty, introduced Jason Voorhees into the pantheon of great movie villains, followed to the letter the rules established by Massacre, and featured the special effects of Tom Savini (born in nineteen forty-six), an absolute benchmark of the genre. In the wake of Friday the thirteenth came a flood of works that sought to copy its resounding success.

My Bloody Valentine, from nineteen eighty-one, brought the fearsome miner killing teenagers in every possible way with his pickaxe. Sleepaway Camp, from nineteen eighty-three, would offer a story along the lines of the Jason saga by setting a series of murders at a summer camp, but with the added bonus of one of the scariest endings in film history. Prom Night, from nineteen eighty, had as its premise a serial killer who hunts teenage victims at a school dance.

## [13] Freddy Krueger  (1765 car., ~1.8 min)

A Nightmare on Elm Street, from nineteen eighty-four, by Wes Craven, would mark the debut of Freddy Krueger and his techniques for killing teenagers in their dreams. The glut of productions (and sequels to those productions) would lead to a resulting exhaustion of the subgenre, similar to what happened to Westerns in the middle of the twentieth century.

[CITAÇÃO] I really think A Nightmare on Elm Street is one of the greatest fairy tales of any decade, because Craven understands the roots of those myths.

said Guillermo del Toro on A Nightmare on Elm Street, nineteen eighty-four.

The nineties, however, held one last ace up the sleeve for the slasher when director Wes Craven (born in nineteen thirty-nine) and screenwriter Kevin Williamson (born in nineteen sixty-five) teamed up and launched the Scream saga with Scream, from nineteen ninety-six. With the goal of rekindling audience interest in killer movies, Scream was born with the premise of not being a copy of formulas that had worked in the past and had worn these films out, but rather a non-comedic parody of the rules established by Mario Bava and Tobe Hooper.

In other words, the story would not be comedic and would carry its own suspense, but it would constantly play with the conventions of the subgenre, such as the final girl, the killer's creative methods, stylized costumes, sexual morality, and so on. For a while, the success of Scream spurred the emergence of another franchise, I Know What You Did Last Summer, from nineteen ninety-seven, which, lacking any originality, failed to replicate the success of Craven and Williamson's saga. Scream can also be considered the last great example of the slasher, which, despite sporadic works, has been losing ground to another subgenre, the haunting film.
