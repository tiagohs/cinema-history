## [01] Bullitt  (2034 car., ~2.0 min)

With the disenchantment of the late nineteen sixties and early nineteen seventies, cinema tended to scorn the thrills of chases, cowboy movies or secret agent films. Bullitt, from nineteen sixty-eight, and The French Connection, from nineteen seventy-one, may have offered elaborate car chases, but their context involved cynicism, political scheming and heroes who challenged the system.

Dirty Harry, from nineteen seventy-one, turned cowboy Clint Eastwood (born in nineteen thirty) into Harry Callahan, the archetypal cop of urban action films. Traditional Westerns and war films were replaced by sermons on the evils of violence, graphically presented in films by Sam Peckinpah (nineteen twenty-five to nineteen eighty-four) such as The Wild Bunch, from nineteen sixty-nine, and Straw Dogs, from nineteen seventy-one.

said Dr. Marcus Brody (Denholm Elliott) in Raiders of the Lost Ark.

Martial arts action films made an explosive entrance with mainstream Western audiences in the early nineteen seventies, thanks to the iconic Bruce Lee (nineteen forty to nineteen seventy-three). Enter the Dragon, from nineteen seventy-three, was the first Chinese martial arts film produced by a major Hollywood studio. Although Lee did not live to see the final result, his work influenced martial arts films around the world for many decades.

By the late nineteen seventies, the climate had changed again. Directed by George Lucas (born in nineteen forty-four), Star Wars, from nineteen seventy-seven, is a return to the comic-strip era of the nineteen thirties, with heroes like Flash Gordon, in which space battles are antiseptic - planets are blown up without any real sense of suffering - and bombings, a massacre at a farm and sword duels are not felt episodes but references to scenes from earlier films. Lucas and his friend Steven Spielberg (born in nineteen forty-six) continued to show their love for old-fashioned adventure with Raiders of the Lost Ark, from nineteen eighty-one.

## [02] Learn more: The Science Fiction Landmark in Cinema (1)  (2437 car., ~2.4 min)

Learn more: The Science Fiction Landmark in Cinema.

This potential contender for a Golden Turkey Award in the "kitsch" category ended up becoming one of the big hits of nineteen eighty-four, and its enduring popularity has enshrined it as a classic of the genre. The Terminator is a triumph of style over plot, of cleverness over intelligence, and a combination of elements that adds up to more than the sum of its parts. Its circular narrative about the future's attempt to control the past in order to take control of itself, thereby changing the premises of its own state, is familiar to every fan of postwar science fiction literature (Harlan Ellison, Philip K. Dick, and others) and of postwar television series (such as The Outer Limits and Star Trek).

Shortly after completing low-budget special effects for Roger Corman (born in nineteen twenty-six) and John Carpenter (born in nineteen forty-eight), writer-director James Cameron (born in nineteen fifty-four) adopted his mentors' disarming humor to shield himself from objections about the many plot holes. The vision of a bleak, dark, apocalyptic future was fashionable at the time, as attested by Escape from New York, from nineteen eighty-one, by John Carpenter, Mad Max, from nineteen seventy-nine, by George Miller (born in nineteen forty-five), Le dernier combat (The Last Battle, nineteen eighty-three), by Luc Besson (born in nineteen fifty-nine), and Forbrydelsens Element (The Element of Crime, nineteen eighty-four), by Lars von Trier (born in nineteen fifty-six).

Cameron's assured style (tech noir, as it was called after a nightclub in the film) and his kinetic energy combined with narrative economy keep the viewer distracted from the absurdity of the whole thing. Even Arnold Schwarzenegger, the epitome of bad acting with his cold facial expression, heavy Austrian accent, and body language learned at the same school as Frankenstein's monster, becomes a major triumph for the film. The casting, Cameron admitted to Wired magazine, "shouldn't have worked. The guy is supposed to be an infiltrator, and it's impossible not to spot him immediately in a crowd, unless everyone looked like Arnold". Nevertheless, everyone who has seen this unlikely phenomenon knows most of the film's lines by heart, including Schwarzenegger's peculiar pronunciation. "I'll be back" is probably the most quoted line of all, often used as a catchphrase on radio and television shows.

## [03] Learn more: The Science Fiction Landmark in Cinema (2)  (896 car., ~0.9 min)

If The Terminator's ability to engage its audience recalls the fan cult of The Rocky Horror Picture Show, from nineteen seventy-five, its insistent irony points ahead to the jokes of the Scream series (nineteen ninety-nine to twenty eleven), built on generic clichés. The same can be said of the sadistic violence and the heroine's turnaround at the end. Beneath the surface of this cut-and-paste of science fiction elements lies an even less original slasher movie, featuring a (literally) mechanical killer and a tough heroine (Linda Hamilton) who discovers her capacity to fight back and combat evil. The film's appeal, as with all great films, does not come from its supposed originality. It is the way Cameron brings together familiar elements and breathes new life into them that results in such a memorable experience, one that ultimately changed science fiction films in theaters forever.

## [04] Richard Donner  (1813 car., ~1.8 min)

Directed by Richard Donner (born in nineteen thirty), Superman, from nineteen seventy-eight, made audiences "believe a man can fly" - and that Clark Kent (Christopher Reeve) was not an outdated character. Alex Raymond's comic-strip hero also got a reinterpretation in Flash Gordon, from nineteen eighty, but despite its camp style and Queen soundtrack, the film did poorly at the box office. Batman, from nineteen eighty-nine, by Tim Burton (born in nineteen fifty-eight), was more successful in nineteen eighty-nine, offering a darker, more gothic superhero in the introspective Bruce Wayne (Michael Keaton).

In the nineteen eighties, action and adventure films featured ever more muscular heroes. Arnold Schwarzenegger (born in nineteen forty-seven) flexed his pecs and grunted a few words as Conan in Conan the Barbarian, from nineteen eighty-two, and as the Terminator in The Terminator, from nineteen eighty-four. Sylvester Stallone (born in nineteen forty-six) turned Rambo in First Blood, from nineteen eighty-two, from a traumatized Vietnam veteran at war with his own country into a superhero ready to settle the score ("Do we get to win this time?"), and Rocky from a washed-up bulldog into a champion of the United States.

Bruce Willis (born in nineteen fifty-five), then best known as Cybill Shepherd's (born in nineteen fifty) irreverent partner on the TV series Moonlighting, took on fake terrorists in a one-man crusade in Die Hard, from nineteen eighty-eight, and its popular sequels. These films were full of montages in which macho men work out their bodies, displaying a sweaty perfection, or gather a ridiculous number of firearms in a scene of gun fetishism.

## [05] Commando  (1264 car., ~1.3 min)

Commando, from nineteen eighty-five, is perhaps the most delirious and sado-erotic of these spectacles of brutes taking on brutes, but Predator, from nineteen eighty-seven, comes a close second, pitting Arnie against an alien. Dip huet seung hung (The Killer, nineteen eighty-nine), by Hong Kong filmmaker John Woo (born in nineteen forty-six), inspired a series of similar (and violent) films, such as Léon: The Professional, from nineteen ninety-four. Meanwhile, Lethal Weapon, from nineteen eighty-seven, with Mel Gibson (born in nineteen fifty-six) and Danny Glover (born in nineteen forty-six), was just one of many mismatched cop pairings in buddy cop movies, such as Stakeout, from nineteen eighty-seven.

The Bond franchise returned with Timothy Dalton (born in nineteen forty-six) in two more violent installments that foreshadowed later entries with Pierce Brosnan (born in nineteen fifty-three) and Daniel Craig (born in nineteen fifty-six). Meanwhile, in Aliens, from nineteen eighty-six, James Cameron (born in nineteen fifty-four) followed up Alien, from nineteen seventy-nine, with more straight-ahead action than horror - "This time it's war" - and with Sigourney Weaver (born in nineteen fifty-nine) returning as the tough, independent Ellen Ripley.
