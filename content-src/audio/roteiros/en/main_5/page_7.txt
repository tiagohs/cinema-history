## [01] Steven Spielberg  (1893 car., ~1.9 min)

The first critics and commentators to take popular cinema seriously tended to value the so-called "golden age" of the Hollywood studios in the nineteen thirties and nineteen forties, somewhat arbitrarily labeling nineteen thirty-nine as "the greatest year in film." This orthodoxy was set aside by the argument that the nineteen seventies were the best years of American cinema - although a simplistic version holds that the industry's domination by important and revolutionary works ended with the rise of the popcorn blockbusters of Steven Spielberg (born in nineteen forty-six) and George Lucas (born in nineteen forty-four).

At the start of the decade, a protagonist would likely be as complex as Robert Eroica Dupea (Jack Nicholson) in Five Easy Pieces, from nineteen seventy. By the end, he would be more two-dimensional, like Clark Kent (Christopher Reeve) in Superman, from nineteen seventy-eight.

But it is worth remembering that, even before Spielberg's Jaws, from nineteen seventy-five, and Lucas's Star Wars, from nineteen seventy-seven, the major studios were happy to ride any profitable wave. Critical hits and award winners such as The French Connection, from nineteen seventy-one, by William Friedkin (born in nineteen thirty-five), and The Sting, from nineteen seventy-three, by George Roy Hill (nineteen twenty-one to two thousand two), inspired films in which tough cops took part in car chases and nostalgic comedy-dramas about male friendship.

Freebie and the Bean, from nineteen seventy-four, combined the two formulas and was part of a wave of mismatched cop pairs in film and television (Black/white, man/woman, gay/straight, human/alien) that would continue through the nineteen eighties and beyond. Paper Moon, from nineteen seventy-three, offered another mismatched pair, the smart-aleck orphan (Tatum O'Neal) and the con man who becomes a father figure (Ryan O'Neal).

"Someday a real rain will come and wash all this scum off the streets". So mutters tormented cab driver Travis Bickle, played with almost furious intensity by Robert De Niro, in the first of his acclaimed leading roles in Martin Scorsese's films. Bickle's job, crisscrossing New York from one end to the other ("anytime, anywhere"), gives him a sleepless view of the city's underbelly, everything that happens in the dark alleys that most people never witness. He ends up so accustomed to the world around him, however, that he feels numb, invisible and, ultimately, powerless.

Taxi Driver's portraits of urban disorder and malaise are as dark and claustrophobic as they come. The film has some elements of film noir (Bickle's voice-over narration, the jazzy score by Bernard Herrmann (nineteen eleven to nineteen seventy-five)) but departs radically from the pattern when it comes to storytelling. Taxi Driver unfolds like a noir told from the perspective of an anonymous stranger standing in front of a murder scene, peering over the police cordon to see the covered body in the street. What is this person thinking? How would they react when confronted with such vivid exposure to violence?

Scorsese, Schrader and De Niro seem to be asking viewers the same thing. Throughout the film, we are forced to observe the city from Bickle's relentlessly isolated perspective, with only a few glimmers of hope carrying us outside his maladjusted mind. He is Dostoevsky's "underground man", who emerges with a gun and a death wish, an antihero who takes justice into his own hands, taking a hands-on approach to cleaning up the city. "Here is a man who won't take it anymore," he announces triumphantly. "A man who stood up against the scum, the sluts, the dogs, the filth, the shit. Here is a man who stood up."

Is this what we want? In an ironic plot twist (of the "the end justifies the means" kind), Bickle ends up hailed as a hero in his own crusade, and it is hard to say whether Bickle's inadvertent triumph is not, in reality, a tragedy. Because the film has so skillfully knocked our moral compass off its axis, we are left struggling desperately in search of impossible answers.

## [04] Bonnie and Clyde  (884 car., ~0.9 min)

Bonnie and Clyde, from nineteen sixty-seven, The Graduate, from nineteen sixty-seven, Night of the Living Dead, from nineteen sixty-eight, Easy Rider, from nineteen sixty-nine, The Wild Bunch, from nineteen sixty-nine, and Medium Cool, from nineteen sixty-nine, were made at the end of the nineteen sixties, but it was in the following decade that their artistic and commercial lessons were put into practice.

The enormous flowering of creativity may simply be due to the fact that audiences turned into hits films that middle-aged or geriatric studio heads could not understand, and so they greenlit projects that baffled them, imagining that perhaps audiences wanted something as surprising as the bone that turns into a spaceship in two thousand one: A Space Odyssey, from nineteen sixty-eight, or David Hemmings making photographic enlargements in Blow-Up, from nineteen sixty-six.

## [05] Two-Lane Blacktop  (1272 car., ~1.3 min)

This gave rise to many films that came to be considered classics: not only films that flopped on release but later became cult masterpieces, such as Two-Lane Blacktop, from nineteen seventy-one, or Willy Wonka and the Chocolate Factory, from nineteen seventy-one, but also immediate hits and Oscar winners. Making The Godfather, from nineteen seventy-two, in the nineteen seventies was not a particularly radical decision, since gangster films had been part of Hollywood's repertoire since the nineteen thirties. However, entrusting the project to an inexperienced director whose last studio job had been Finian's Rainbow, from nineteen sixty-eight, just because he was Italian-American, was a decision born of inspired desperation.

One complaint heard from the upper echelons of US politics and the armed forces after the defeat in Vietnam was that - leaving aside The Green Berets, from nineteen sixty-eight, - Hollywood had let them down by not mobilizing the propaganda machine the way it had during World War Two. Instead of Yankee Doodle Dandy, from nineteen forty-two, young people went to see Woodstock, from nineteen seventy. Instead of Betty Grable showing her legs and the Duke taking Iwo Jima, Jane Fonda supported the enemy and Donald Sutherland looked stoned.

## [07] The Parallax View  (1689 car., ~1.7 min)

In the nineteen seventies, the major Hollywood studios backed The Parallax View, from nineteen seventy-four, and All the President's Men, from nineteen seventy-six, paranoid visions of a government conspiracy. When Hollywood began making films about Vietnam, few would have pleased John Wayne: starting with Coming Home, from nineteen seventy-eight, The Deer Hunter, from nineteen seventy-eight, and Apocalypse Now, from nineteen seventy-nine.

Perhaps the most important American filmmaker of the decade was a man who directed almost all of his films before nineteen seventy-one (afterward, he mostly made quick, opportunistic productions): Roger Corman (born in nineteen twenty-six). While the studios were still trying to follow in the footsteps of The Sound of Music, from nineteen sixty-five, Corman was employing Francis Ford Coppola (born in nineteen thirty-nine), Peter Bogdanovich (born in nineteen thirty-nine), Martin Scorsese, Robert Towne (born in nineteen thirty-four), Jonathan Demme (born in nineteen forty-four), Joe Dante (born in nineteen forty-six), Robert De Niro, Jack Nicholson, Monte Hellman (born in nineteen thirty-two), and Paul Bartel (nineteen thirty-eight to two thousand).

Without Corman's approach - keep the camera moving, include nudity, season the clichés with humor, use music that isn't fashionable yet (i.e., cheap) but is about to be, and make sure there's a crumb of social commentary for the college kids - there would be no films like American Graffiti, from nineteen seventy-three, Mean Streets, from nineteen seventy-three, The Conversation, from nineteen seventy-four, Chinatown, from nineteen seventy-four, or Shampoo, from nineteen seventy-five.

## [08] Learn more: One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest  (1899 car., ~1.9 min)

Learn more: One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest.

A project that actor Kirk Douglas (nineteen sixteen to twenty twenty) had wanted to make for years (he owned the rights, but by the time the film finally went into production, he felt he was too old to star in it), One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest earned his son, Michael Douglas (born in nineteen forty-four), his first Oscar, not as an actor but as a producer.

Based on the bestseller by Ken Kesey, written after his experience working at a veterans' hospital in California, the film is set in a state mental institution where the wily nonconformist Randle P. McMurphy (Jack Nicholson) is committed for rehabilitation. Inside the institution, he is kept under the watchful eye of the sadistic Nurse Ratched (Louise Fletcher, in a role turned down by several major Hollywood actresses, including Jane Fonda, Anne Bancroft, Ellen Burstyn, and Faye Dunaway), and he soon stages a rebellion against her with the help of other patients.

A groundbreaking film that Kesey reportedly never wanted to see (he was supposedly irritated that the story was not told from the point of view of Chief Bromden (Will Sampson), as in his book), One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest became part of Oscar history when it became only the second film up to that time to win all five major awards. Each one was deserved: Czech director Milos Forman (nineteen thirty-two to twenty eighteen) created a hypnotic, humanistic film full of eccentric characters (among the actors who made their film debuts are Brad Dourif (born in nineteen fifty) and Christopher Lloyd (born in nineteen thirty-eight)), and drew one of the best performances of Fletcher's career. Nicholson, naturally, is captivating as McMurphy, imprisoned yet witty and full of life, and the scenes in which he faces off with the despicable Nurse Ratched are the most electrifying in this modern classic of American cinema.

## [09] John Carpenter  (1498 car., ~1.5 min)

Corman's business plan also impressed other independents who were making films far from studio interference. Drive-ins and college cinemas in the nineteen seventies were full of oddities and films that embraced a multitude of genres. There were action films with Black casts, softcore / hardcore porn, and road movies. John Carpenter (born in nineteen forty-eight) created a few films, and Clint Eastwood (born in nineteen thirty) went from star to star director. Terrence Malick crafted experimental, symbolic narratives such as Days of Heaven, from nineteen seventy-eight.

It was also the era of the "early, funny" films of Woody Allen (born in nineteen thirty-five), which led to Annie Hall, from nineteen seventy-seven, - the moment he decided that just being funny was not enough - and the romantic comedy and declaration of love for New York, Manhattan, from nineteen seventy-nine. There was still room for an intense, emotional drama like Kramer versus Kramer, from nineteen seventy-nine, - which won five Academy Awards.

From a group of young filmmakers came the summer blockbuster - Jaws. The film had its antecedents: at first, Hollywood saw Spielberg's film simply as a follow-up to disaster movies such as The Poseidon Adventure, from nineteen seventy-two, or The Towering Inferno, from nineteen seventy-four. It was only when Jaws became as successful as The Godfather that people realized how significant the film was, and from then on it came to be considered a "high-end" horror film.

In the long run, it may be that the effects of Star Wars brought an end to the opportunities that benefited Hollywood in the nineteen seventies. From Jaws to Star Wars - as well as in the films of John Carpenter, Walter Hill (born in nineteen forty-two), and others - came a sense of rediscovery of the Hollywood classics, which led American filmmakers to draw inspiration as much from Alfred Hitchcock (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty), Howard Hawks (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen seventy-seven), and John Ford (eighteen ninety-four to nineteen seventy-three) as from Jean-Luc [[pron: Godard|Go-dar]] (born in nineteen thirty), Michelangelo Antonioni (nineteen twelve to two thousand seven), and Ingmar Bergman (nineteen eighteen to two thousand seven).

The boxing film about a hero who comes from nowhere can also be seen from this angle, although Rocky, from nineteen seventy-six, is far more interesting than its many sequels. It brought energy and a sense of fun back to a cinema that had run the risk of producing an excess of slow, sad films, and it drew a huge audience that had largely stayed away from productions that would later be considered important.
