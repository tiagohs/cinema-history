## [01] Fred Schepisi  (592 car., ~0.6 min)

Australian cinema in the nineteen seventies and early nineteen eighties was an industry sharply divided in two. On one side, the prestige productions of Fred Schepisi (born in nineteen thirty-nine), Peter Weir (born in nineteen forty-four) and Gillian Armstrong (born in nineteen fifty) told quintessentially Australian stories: elaborate, lovingly crafted, rich in artistic integrity and quiet grace.

On the other side, the booming B-movie industry offered a parade of crude sex comedies, punch-heavy action-adventure films and inventive gory horror films, collectively known as Ozploitation.

"Ozploitation" films are a type of horror, comedy and action film, usually low-budget, made in Australia after the introduction of the R rating in nineteen seventy-one.

Ozploitation is often considered a minor wave within the Australian New Wave. ## [03] Australian New Wave  (1347 car., ~1.4 min)

In the late nineteen sixties, aside from a few historical dramas, in many cases financed from abroad, the Australian film industry had created very few productions of its own. In the early nineteen seventies, however, a confluence of events changed that picture. A new artistic counterculture emerged; the increasingly popular national television industry trained directors, actors and producers; and a series of government initiatives and tax incentives made filmmaking a far more attractive business. The result was the country's creative wave in cinema, dubbed the "Australian New Wave" by the press.

The first hits were ocker comedies (a term used for crude, uncultivated Australians), films about suburban life and sexual frustration that reflected the new cultural freedom while both celebrating and mocking Australian notions of masculinity and culture.

## [04] Picnic at Hanging Rock  (1734 car., ~1.7 min)

His next film, Picnic at Hanging Rock, from nineteen seventy-five, however, came to define everything that quality Australian cinema stood for: stunning landscapes, carefully reconstructed period settings, beauty, silence and a sense of something terrible lurking. The film both celebrates and questions aspects of Australian culture, specifically the powerful influence of the British, the quiet reliance on a class system, and an Aboriginal culture trampled by colonialism.

Taking their cue from Picnic at Hanging Rock, Australia's prestige filmmakers used cinema to revisit aspects of the country's colonial past. The Chant of Jimmie Blacksmith, from nineteen seventy-eight, evoked the savagery of the nation's birth, portraying the colonial forces as murderous thugs waging a war for dominance and crushing the native population in their path. Meanwhile, Gillian Armstrong's (born in nineteen fifty) My Brilliant Career, from nineteen seventy-nine, and Weir's Gallipoli, from nineteen eighty-one, explored the country's attempts to survive the decline of the British Empire.

B-movie production was also finding its footing. The biker film Stone, from nineteen seventy-four, took the template of Roger Corman's epic motorcycle-rebel movies and added explicit sex, violence and a shrewd awareness of Australian identity, paving the way for the country's biggest hit, Mad Max, from nineteen seventy-nine, a worldwide success. The latter introduced a new international star: Mel Gibson (born in nineteen fifty-six) had the looks and swagger of a classic movie icon, but with an added toughness and emotional instability. His hugely successful decision to leave Australia for Hollywood set a precedent for countless actors and directors.

## [05] Learn more: Mad Max: The New Australian Cinema Goes Global  (1810 car., ~1.8 min)

Learn more: Mad Max: The New Australian Cinema Goes Global.

Although its sequel usually gets most of the critical praise, it all began with the striking Mad Max, released two years earlier. It was here that Miller first brought to the screen his hellish vision of an inhospitable, gang-ruled Australia, with the help of a newcomer actor named Mel Gibson.

Gibson was only twenty-three when he landed the role of Max Rockatansky (legend has it that Gibson auditioned for the part the day after getting into a bar fight, and his bruised face stuck in the casting director's mind). Gibson was so unknown that when Mad Max was about to be released in the United States, he didn't even appear in the trailers, which showed only explosions and car crashes. In hindsight, of course, the leather-jacketed antihero Gibson created is a fundamental element of the film.

In a decaying, futuristic Australia, Rockatansky is a motorcycle cop trying to keep order in a rapidly disintegrating society. Gangs roam the abandoned highways, raping and robbing at will, and one of these groups ends up at the house where Max's wife and child are staying. When they are murdered, Max seeks revenge and unleashes a wave of violence involving spectacular car chases, brutal fights and nonstop action.

Most impressive is the fact that the whole film cost just four hundred thousand dollars, a budget so tight that Miller reportedly had to wreck his own car for one scene. In the end, though, it grossed more than one hundred million dollars, making it one of the most profitable films of all time. Mad Max deservedly became a cult hit around the world (curiously, it was dubbed into "American English" in the United States because it was thought audiences wouldn't understand the Australian accent) and remains a memorable film to this day.

## [06] Crocodile Dundee  (1531 car., ~1.5 min)

Australian B movies often outdid their better-funded American counterparts. The work of American-born screenwriter Everett De Roche, in films such as the eco-satire Long Weekend, from nineteen seventy-eight, fused the typical thrills of a low-budget B movie with smart dialogue and sharp ideas about psychology, ecology and landscape, filtered through an outsider's affectionate perspective on his adopted country.

Both the prestige strand and the B-movie strand began to falter in the mid-nineteen eighties, as government funding shrank and many leading directors, such as Peter Weir, headed for Hollywood. (Fred Schepisi was so disillusioned by the poor reception of The Chant of Jimmie Blacksmith at home that he left Australia and took ten years to shoot there again.)

Australian film culture today is little different from that of any small Western country: it produces a large number of modest dramas, a steady stream of talented screenwriters, actors and directors with their eyes on Hollywood, and the occasional worldwide hit such as Crocodile Dundee, from nineteen eighty-six. Starring and written by comedian Paul Hogan (born in nineteen thirty-nine), that film (and its sequels) was initially aimed at the large American audience, but managed to win over international audiences. Although it seems unlikely that filmmakers will recapture the spirit of daring and experimentation that defined the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties, Australian cinema remains synonymous with popular, intelligent and well-made films.
