## [01] That's Entertainment!  (1312 car., ~1.3 min)

Hollywood musicals are deeply associated with the golden age of American cinema, but they did not disappear with the end of the studio system. Instead, they changed shape. The traditional musical could still be found often, slightly reworked to suit a more modern audience.

However, the genre was taken into new areas, exploring contemporary issues and fads, as a platform for new musical genres or to reassess the past. The first two installments, That's Entertainment!, from nineteen seventy-four, and That's Entertainment, Part Two, from nineteen seventy-six, recalled the heyday of the Hollywood musical.

There were also contemporary releases that differed little from nineteen fifties productions: Scrooge, from nineteen seventy, Willy Wonka and the Chocolate Factory, from nineteen seventy-one, Fiddler on the Roof, from nineteen seventy-one, Funny Lady, from nineteen seventy-five, and Annie, from nineteen eighty-two, adopted a classic style and offered a perfect escape from reality.

Nevertheless, Norman Jewison (born in nineteen twenty-six) gave an inventive spin to Jesus Christ Superstar, from nineteen seventy-three, which featured songs by the new wunderkinds of musical theater, Andrew Lloyd Webber and Tim Rice. That same year, another reinterpretation of a biblical story, Godspell, also appeared.

## [02] Learn more: Cabaret: The Political, Modern Musical  (1656 car., ~1.7 min)

Learn more: Cabaret: The Political, Modern Musical.

Bob Fosse's masterpiece is a cynical critique of the last decadent days of the Weimar Republic, on the eve of the rise of National Socialism, with frivolous, catchy songs that mask the troubling events unfolding in Europe.

Brian (Michael York) arrives in Berlin in nineteen thirty-one. He meets Sally Bowles (Liza Minnelli) and the two become lovers. She works at the Kit Kat Klub (note the three Ks), an underground cabaret cut off from the outside world. Sally and Brian's fleeting affair is put into perspective by the violent turmoil surrounding them, which begins to destroy the freedoms they cherish so much.

Glimpses of Nazi brutality go unnoticed by those enjoying themselves at the cabaret. The songs bring the plot and characters to life, driving the narrative while also commenting on the unfolding events. "Maybe This Time" expresses Sally's high hopes; "If You Could See Her" is a terrifying allegory of the intense persecution of Berlin's Jewish population. The songs are performed on the stage of the Kit Kat Klub and, apart from the expressionist angles revealing morally gray spectators, the numbers are staged realistically. Fosse shows the performances from a distance, using the glare of the lights to emphasize the small stage and the silhouettes of heads in the audience - conspirators in this dark world.

The final frames repeat the opening scene, with the smeared glass reflecting those who have finally taken over the audience, identifiable only by their swastika-adorned armbands. A mirror is held up to society, and the figures in it are hazy, distorted, sharing a cruel worldview.

## [03] Oh! Calcutta!  (1344 car., ~1.3 min)

The counterculture born in the nineteen sixties was equally represented by adaptations of stage shows: Oh! Calcutta!, from nineteen seventy-two, and Hair, from nineteen seventy-nine. Although they adopted a conventional narrative structure, these films - like the shows they were based on - were controversial for breaking taboos regarding nudity, sex and politics.

Bob Fosse (nineteen twenty-seven to nineteen eighty-nine) was the dominant force in the American musical from the late nineteen sixties onward. A choreographer by training, Fosse created a singular style that used every limb to accentuate a dancer's movement. His Cabaret, from nineteen seventy-two, won eight Oscars, while All That Jazz, from nineteen seventy-nine, a frank, semi-autobiographical account of a director on the verge of collapse amid preparations for his next show, was awarded the Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival.

Rock dominated most musicals from the nineteen seventies onward. Alan Parker (born in nineteen forty-four), who also directed Bugsy Malone, from nineteen seventy-six, and Fame, from nineteen eighty, directed Pink Floyd: The Wall, from nineteen eighty-two. A more traditional style was evident in Grease, from nineteen seventy-eight, which casts an affectionate eye on teenage rebellion and romance and became the decade's biggest musical hit.

## [04] The Rocky Horror Picture Show  (1221 car., ~1.2 min)

The Rocky Horror Picture Show, from nineteen seventy-five, combines the sexual standards that prevailed after the nineteen sixties with a salute to the horror films of the nineteen forties, all driven by a series of skillfully produced musical numbers. Even the punk movement was represented - The Great Rock 'n' Roll Swindle, from nineteen eighty, introduced the Sex Pistols to a wider world.

The success of Saturday Night Fever, from nineteen seventy-seven, practically ignited the disco phenomenon, thanks to the infectious soundtrack by the Bee Gees, and spurred a series of dance films that gained popularity during the nineteen eighties. Staying Alive, from nineteen eighty-three, saw John Travolta's (born in nineteen fifty-four) character, Tony Manero, attempt a career on Broadway, while in Flashdance, from nineteen eighty-three, the factory worker played by Jennifer Beals (born in nineteen sixty-three) dreams of getting into dance school.

## [05] Learn more: The Rocky Horror Picture Show  (2034 car., ~2.0 min)

Learn more: The Rocky Horror Picture Show.

Richard O'Brien's offbeat stage musical, adapted for the screen in nineteen seventy-five, was a flop on its release. However, when a New York theater began showing it at midnight screenings, word quickly spread about the bizarre science fiction and horror spoof. The film became a cult hit and to this day holds the record for the longest theatrical run, having been shown at the same theater in Munich, Germany, every week for more than twenty-seven years.

A very young Susan Sarandon (born in nineteen forty-six) and Barry Bostwick (born in nineteen forty-five) star as Janet and Brad, an innocent couple whose car breaks down on a stormy night, forcing them to seek shelter in a nearby castle, unaware that it belongs to the stockings-and-garters-clad transvestite Frank N. Furter (Tim Curry, reprising his stage role) and his friends from Transylvania, including the sinister Riff Raff (Richard O'Brien) and Magenta (Patricia Quinn). Also living in the mansion are the biker Eddie (Meat Loaf), a failed creation (in the style of Frankenstein's monster) of Frank N. Furter, and Rocky (Peter Hinwood), his perfectly tanned replacement/upgrade.

Narrated by Dr. Everett Scott (Jonathan Adams) and driven by glam rock, the film is a celebration of sexuality: Frank N. Furter and his troupe try to seduce the virginal Brad and Janet to the tune of memorable songs such as "Touch-a Touch-a Touch Me", "Sweet Transvestite" and, of course, "Time Warp". The mix of brazen sexuality, ironic quips, outrageous costumes, and double entendres is unlike anything ever done in cinema. It is easy to see why the catchy songs and highly quotable dialogue became such a huge hit with fans, the most enthusiastic of whom dress up as the characters, act out parts of the film during screenings, and bring props to use at specific moments in the film, for example, throwing rice during the wedding scene. The Rocky Horror Picture Show may not be a film for the whole family, but it is still fantastic kitsch fun.

## [06] Footloose  (309 car., ~0.3 min)

Conservatism stood against dancing in two of the decade's most popular musicals, Footloose, from nineteen eighty-four, and Dirty Dancing, from nineteen eighty-seven. As in all nineteen eighties musicals, the music was prerecorded and generally included tracks familiar to audiences, boosting their popularity.
