During the economic turmoil of the Great Depression, movies offered an affordable window onto the world for the price of a ticket.

## [01] I Am a Fugitive from a Georgia Chain Gang  (757 car., ~0.8 min)

For the price of a ticket, any moviegoer could witness sophistication and glamour in films like Grand Hotel, from nineteen thirty-two, and Trouble in Paradise, from nineteen thirty-two, or learn about the lives of great men such as Louis Pasteur and Emile Zola through biopics. With Citizen Kane, from nineteen forty-one, audiences could step into the world of a contemporary celebrity, press baron William Randolph Hearst.

Every so often, the major studios felt ready to educate audiences about some of the darker aspects of American society. In nineteen thirty-one, Warner Bros. bought an autobiographical article by Robert Elliott Burns that had run in True Detective magazine and was later published as the book I Am a Fugitive from a Georgia Chain Gang.

## [02] Learn more: Inventing modern cinema by ignoring conventions  (1925 car., ~1.9 min)

Learn more: Inventing modern cinema by ignoring conventions.

Citizen Kane, the directorial debut of Orson Welles (nineteen fifteen to nineteen eighty-five), has always been considered "the greatest film of all time", which makes it hard to assess clearly. Myths have formed around it: that Welles pioneered a series of innovative filmmaking techniques, and that the film was dismissed on its release for being considered too pretentious.

Welles was not the first to use techniques such as greater depth of field, sets with visible ceilings, overlapping sound, jump cuts, "naturalistic" dialogue in which lines trample over one another, chiaroscuro lighting, or a narrative with multiple points of view - although no other filmmaker had dared to combine them with as much fervor and aesthetic creativity as he did.

When Citizen Kane opened, most critics received it enthusiastically, but its release was sabotaged. Press baron William Randolph Hearst had no trouble at all recognizing himself in the character of Charles Foster Kane. In the blink of an eye, his entire powerful public relations machine went into action against Welles's film, making sure it never got a decent release.

For such a revolutionary work, the message behind the film is surprisingly conventional: the rich are not to be envied for their wealth, because they have lost the simple pleasures of true love and happiness. Even so, it deserves its top status, first, because Welles's signature is so captivating, stamped so indelibly on every frame, and second, because today viewers can recognize much of Welles in the protagonist himself.

Citizen Kane is still a hugely important film, but amid its visual grandiloquence, its baroque showmanship and its ostentatious techniques - however fascinating they may be - we can hear, like a terrible premonition, the howl of frustration and creative blockage that the future held in store for the brilliant Welles.

## [03] I Am a Fugitive from a Chain Gang  (1885 car., ~1.9 min)

Despite doubts about the subject matter, one director dismissed it on the grounds that it was unsuitable at a time when people in general were so depressed that many were "jumping out of windows", the studio persisted, and Burns, though technically still a fugitive from justice, was secretly hired to work on a first draft of the screenplay. The success of I Am a Fugitive from a Chain Gang, from nineteen thirty-two, directed by Mervyn LeRoy (nineteen hundred to nineteen eighty-seven) and starring Paul Muni (eighteen ninety-five to nineteen sixty-seven) as the fugitive James Allen, incisively exposed the cruelty of the penal system in the American South.

Fritz Lang (eighteen ninety to nineteen seventy-six) delivered a powerful indictment of another sordid American secret. Fury, from nineteen thirty-six, was inspired by the true story of a double lynching in San Jose three years earlier. Spencer Tracy (nineteen hundred to nineteen sixty-seven) plays Joe Wilson, a man wrongly accused of kidnapping a child. When a crazed mob sets fire to the jail, he manages to escape, but pretends to be dead so that those responsible will be charged with murder.

In fact, nearly all the people who were lynched at the time were Black, a fact no studio was interested in publicizing. The singular power of Fury lies in the way viewers find themselves uncomfortably drawn to sympathize with the lynchers, who in turn become victims of an injustice.

Frank Capra (eighteen ninety-seven to nineteen ninety-one) offered a more optimistic view of the anxieties of the common man. Historian Robert Sklar attributes the secret of Capra's success to "his ability to convince viewers that they were seeing their own strengths and weaknesses, their own dreams and values, in short, seeing themselves represented on the screen."

## [04] Darryl Zanuck  (931 car., ~0.9 min)

Capra's heroes may be victims of corrupt politicians and of capitalism, but they are not outsiders. Portraying the lives of the dispossessed on screen was more problematic. When Darryl Zanuck (nineteen oh-two to nineteen seventy-nine) acquired the rights to John Steinbeck's (nineteen oh-two to nineteen sixty-eight) novel The Grapes of Wrath, from nineteen thirty-nine, the plight of the refugees of the Dust Bowl, a series of violent dust storms that devastated the southern United States in the nineteen thirties, was a reality that many were unwilling to face.

John Ford's (eighteen ninety-four to nineteen seventy-three) sensitive screen version touched the collective conscience of nineteen forties moviegoers. Singer Woody Guthrie, who retold the story in song, summed up its importance: "Go see The Grapes of Wrath, pardner... you are the star of that picture. Go and see yourself and hear your own words and your own song."
