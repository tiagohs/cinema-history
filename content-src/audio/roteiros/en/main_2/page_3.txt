## [00] Opening  (256 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero two: From nineteen thirty to nineteen thirty-nine. Chapter three: Color in Film.

In the early days of cinema, films were dominated by black and white. It was a long road from frame-by-frame hand painting to the refinement seen in The Wizard of Oz.

## [01] Pathé’s Pathécolor system  (954 car., ~0.9 min)

Photographic color came to the movies at roughly the same time as sound, although, as with sound, various color effects had been used in films since the invention of the medium. Georges [[pron: Méliès|May-lee-ess]] (eighteen sixty-one to nineteen thirty-eight), for example, employed twenty-one women at his studio in Montreuil to color his films frame by frame, but hand painting was not cost-effective unless the films were very short.

In the mid-nineteen hundreds, as films began to approach one reel in length and more prints of each film were sold, mechanized stencil processes were introduced. In [[pron: Pathé|Pa-tay]]’s Pathécolor system, for example, a stencil was cut for each desired color (up to six) and aligned with the print; the color was then applied through the stencil frame by frame at high speed. With the advent of the feature and the industry's conversion to mass production during the nineteen tens, frame-by-frame painting was replaced by mechanized tinting and toning.

## [02] Part 2  (812 car., ~0.8 min)

Tinting colored all the light areas of an image and was achieved by immersing a black-and-white print in dye or by using a colored film base for printing. The toning process involved chemically treating the film emulsion to color the dark areas of the print. Each process produced monochromatic images, whose color was usually chosen to match the mood or setting of the scene. Occasionally the two processes were combined to produce elaborate two-color effects.

By the early nineteen twenties, nearly every American feature included at least one color sequence; but after nineteen twenty-seven, when it was discovered that tinted or toned film stock interfered with the transmission of the optical soundtrack, both practices were temporarily abandoned, leaving the market open to new systems of color photography.

Created in twenty twelve, the Timeline of Historical Film Colors was developed and curated by Barbara Flueckiger, a professor in the Department of Film Studies at the University of Zurich, to provide comprehensive information on the historical film color processes invented since the late nineteenth century, including the specific color technologies of still photography that were their conceptual predecessors.

## [04] Kinemacolor  (1047 car., ~1.1 min)

Photographic color can be produced in film using either an additive or a subtractive process. The first systems to be developed and used were all additive, such as Kinemacolor, from nineteen oh-six, and [[pron: Gaumont|Go-mon]] Chronochrome, from nineteen twelve. They achieved varying degrees of popularity, but none was entirely successful, mainly because all additive systems involve the use of special cameras and projectors, which makes them too complicated and expensive for widespread industrial use.

One of the first successful subtractive processes was a two-color system introduced by Herbert Kalmus in nineteen twenty-two: Technicolor. It used a special camera and a complex procedure to produce two separate positive prints that were then cemented together into a single print. The final print required careful handling, but it could be projected with ordinary equipment. This "cemented positive" process was used successfully in features such as The Toll of the Sea, from nineteen twenty-two, and Fairbanks's The Black Pirate, from nineteen twenty-six.

## [05] Technicolor  (2185 car., ~2.2 min)

In nineteen twenty-eight, Technicolor introduced an improved process in which two gelatin positives were used as relief matrices to "print" the color onto a single strip of film. Its introduction led to a significant increase in Technicolor production between nineteen twenty-nine and nineteen thirty-two. Color reproduction in the two-color Technicolor process was good, but still not completely natural. Its popularity began to fall sharply in nineteen thirty-two, and Technicolor replaced it with a three-color system that used the same basic principles but included all three primary colors.

For the next twenty-five years, nearly every color film made was produced with Technicolor's three-color system. Although the quality of the system was excellent, it had drawbacks. The bulk of the camera made location shooting difficult. Moreover, Technicolor's virtual monopoly gave it indirect control over the production companies, which were required to rent Technicolor's equipment, crews, consultants, and laboratory services, at high prices, every time they used the system. During the Depression, therefore, the conversion to color was slow and never truly complete.

After three-color Technicolor was used successfully in the Disney cartoon short Three Little Pigs, from nineteen thirty-three, the live-action short La Cucaracha, from nineteen thirty-four, and the feature film Becky Sharp, from nineteen thirty-five, directed by Rouben Mamoulian (eighteen ninety-seven to nineteen eighty-seven), it gradually made its way into feature production with The Garden of Allah, from nineteen thirty-six, Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, from nineteen thirty-seven, The Adventures of Robin Hood, from nineteen thirty-eight, The Wizard of Oz, from nineteen thirty-nine, and Gone with the Wind, from nineteen thirty-nine, although it remained strongly associated with fantasy and spectacle.

It has been said that when Dorothy first stepped out of her sepia-toned, tornado-ravaged house into the multicolored wonderland of Munchkin Country, an audible gasp swept through movie theaters all across America. In an instant, audiences were transported into a Technicolor world, never to look back.

## [06] Natalie Kalmus  (1431 car., ~1.4 min)

Hollywood, however, was slow to cross the threshold from grayscale to color, about thirty years, in fact. At first, color was seen merely as a new embellishment for a film, reserved for big dance numbers or dream sequences. It aimed at "sensual intensity" rather than realism. It was the visionary Natalie Kalmus (eighteen eighty-two to nineteen sixty-five) who rescued color from being understood as mere ornament. She wove it into the very structure of filmmaking.

When the revolutionary three-color Technicolor developed by her husband Herbert Kalmus (eighteen eighty-one to nineteen sixty-three) arrived in nineteen thirty-two, it had decades of baggage to overcome. Herbert's real challenge was convincing a skeptical industry, and, indeed, the moviegoing public, that color could be integrated into the basic vocabulary of cinematic storytelling.

The first Technicolor feature, Becky Sharp, from nineteen thirty-five, established Technicolor as the truest color ever seen on screen. Yet while it was a persuasive showcase for the technology, the film did not make a convincing case that color is essential to the art of storytelling. Its unrestrained use of color drew ire. One reviewer complained: "Your eyes are constantly being diverted from the people to the flowers." Another remarked: "When Rawdon casts Becky off, you don't see her so much as you see the dress she is wearing and the Aubusson carpet on which she sinks."

## [07] Technicolor Motion Picture Corporation  (985 car., ~1.0 min)

Both the studios and the public needed to be convinced that color could help and enhance a narrative rather than overwhelm it. Herbert Kalmus's invention was on shaky ground. As Life magazine's review of the film concluded, it remained "a question whether or not color can be kept in its place in the making of a feature picture." That's where Natalie came in.

Natalie Dunfee, born in eighteen eighty-two, met Herbert Kalmus when the two were studying color at the University of Zurich in Switzerland. In nineteen oh-two, Dunfee married Kalmus, and over the next ten years the Kalmuses moved constantly. Natalie studied art at various institutions in Europe and North America, and Herbert began experimenting in their basement with the groundbreaking notion of color film. Natalie, equally passionate about color, joined in. In nineteen fifteen, Herbert Kalmus, along with Daniel Comstock and Burton Westcott (all of them MIT alumni), founded the Technicolor Motion Picture Corporation.

## [08] 400 films  (2276 car., ~2.3 min)

Here, Natalie became the world's first Technicolor star. She acted in their test films and also served as developer, cutter, and camera owner. Finally, around nineteen twenty-seven, the small Technicolor company made its way to Hollywood. Technicolor would go on to defeat dozens of other competing corporations, including companies such as Kinemacolor and Kodachrome, to become Hollywood's reigning color company. Early Technicolor films tended to be garish and murky, not least because the studios, irked by the high cost, insisted on getting their money's worth in quantity, they wanted every color, all the time. Natalie, drawing on her years of study, set out to change that attitude. She wanted to push the industry from this blunt approach toward a sensitive 'color consciousness.' Kalmus believed that cinema had been "steadily tending toward a more complete realism" and that, after the addition of sound, color was the final step toward perfection, if used correctly.

said Natalie Kalmus.

Kalmus recommended that color be used with sensitivity, because the human nervous system "suffers a shock when it is forced to adapt itself to any degree of abnormality in the reception of external stimuli." Kalmus laid down some fundamental guidelines for the use of color in film. She believed that color should serve as a support for action and dialogue; it should be suggestive of mood rather than an equal partner in communication. She warned against a "superabundance of color" in favor of more natural, neutral color schemes. And she stressed "the law of emphasis," decreeing that insignificant details in an image should not be emphasized.

Natalie worked on more than four hundred films, but it was her work as color director on The Wizard of Oz that finally caught the world's attention. Cinema changed forever when the film went from sepia to color. From Dorothy's ruby slippers to the use of multicolored lights in the wizard's chambers, the film is an ode to the magic of Technicolor (by the way, the sepia wasn't a post-production trick. The house was actually painted sepia, and Judy Garland's double was wearing a sepia dress).

## [09] An American in Paris  (1673 car., ~1.7 min)

Kalmus did not have an easy life on film sets. Men bristled at having to defer to a woman. While directing An American in Paris, from nineteen fifty-one, Vincente Minnelli (nineteen oh-three to nineteen eighty-six) grumbled that he "couldn't do anything right in Mrs. Kalmus's eyes." In response to Kalmus's rule about clashing colors, Cecil DeMille (eighteen eighty-one to nineteen fifty-nine) fumed: "Well, it's a pity the good Lord up above didn't have a Technicolor consultant when he made apples and oranges!" On British film sets, where she faced resistance for both her gender and her nationality, she was often a target of scorn for her looks, clothes, and hats (apparently to discredit her aesthetic judgment). The general feeling toward Kalmus, nurtured by Hollywood's boys' club, was perhaps best summed up by Alan Dwan (eighteen eighty-five to nineteen eighty-one), one of the most important directors of the silent era. "Natalie Kalmus was a bitch," he said in a nineteen eighty interview.

Films have a seismic effect on our collective visual vocabulary. Those who create the grammar of film, how films are lit, framed, composed, shot, and, indeed, colored, are major influencers of visual culture. As the "high priestess of Technicolor," Kalmus needs no gender qualifier to be considered one of the greats of cinema of all time. That said, she was a woman who steered the film industry through one of its most difficult transitions, facing resistance and skepticism every step of the way. How did she do it? "If you apply yourself properly to a career of any kind, you will not need to seek advice about it," she said. "No one will be able to stop you anyway."
