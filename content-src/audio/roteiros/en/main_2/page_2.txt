## [00] Opening  (303 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero two: From nineteen thirty to nineteen thirty-nine. Chapter two: Gangster Films.

The early nineteen thirties, the so-called "golden age of turbulence", was a period when censorship was suspended. Mae West was at her peak, wildly surreal musicals were being made and armed gangsters were lurking.

## [01] James Cagney  (613 car., ~0.6 min)

The films of this period bring certain images to mind: James Cagney (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty-six) pushing a grapefruit into Mae Clarke's (nineteen ten to nineteen ninety-two) face in The Public Enemy, from nineteen thirty-one, Paul Muni (eighteen ninety-five to nineteen sixty-seven) brandishing his machine gun in Scarface, from nineteen thirty-two, and Edward G. Robinson (eighteen ninety-three to nineteen seventy-three) and his stocky, fedora-wearing mob boss in Little Caesar, from nineteen thirty-one. These three productions form what critics call the paradigm of the classic gangster film.

## [02] Volstead Act  (2089 car., ~2.1 min)

Although the nineteen thirties were an especially fertile period for gangster films, the one considered the first example of the genre was made almost twenty years earlier: D. W. Griffith's (eighteen seventy-five to nineteen forty-eight) The Musketeers of Pig Alley, from nineteen twelve, a sixteen-minute silent short film based on the real-life murder of gambler Herman Rosenthal. Another key gangster film of the late nineteen twenties was Josef von Sternberg's (eighteen ninety-four to nineteen sixty-nine) Underworld, from nineteen twenty-seven, written by former Chicago reporter Ben Hecht. "I had an idea. The trick was to skip the heroes and heroines and write a movie containing only villains and bawds," Hecht explained in a revealing passage of his autobiography. "As a newspaperman, I had learned that the good folk - the public - loved criminals, and were crazy to read about their love troubles and their sadism."

In the previous decade, gangster films were spurred on by an unlikely group - the U.S. Congress - when, in the fall of nineteen nineteen, the Volstead Act, which banned the sale of alcohol, was passed. Prohibition fostered the growth of criminal gangs, which seized the opportunity to offer Americans illegal access to liquor.

This was the era of crime bosses, bootleggers and speakeasies. It also represented a brave new world of opportunity for Hollywood. Some studios specialized in gangster films. Warner Bros., which liked to boast of making films "ripped from the headlines," was one of the most prominent. Little Caesar, directed by Mervyn LeRoy (nineteen hundred to nineteen eighty-seven) and starring Edward G. Robinson as a short, psychotic hoodlum; William Wellman's (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen seventy-five) The Public Enemy, from nineteen thirty-one, with Cagney as an enterprising gangster (both from Warner); and Scarface, from nineteen thirty-two, from United Artists, directed by Howard Hawks (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen seventy-seven), were released one after another and spawned a series of imitations.

## [03] Learn more: Prohibition in the US (1)  (2122 car., ~2.1 min)

Learn more: Prohibition in the US.

In short, Prohibition, also known as the Prohibition era (Prohibition in the United States), refers to the period from nineteen twenty to nineteen thirty-three during which the manufacture, transportation and sale of alcoholic beverages for consumption were banned nationwide, as stipulated by the Eighteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution.

By the late nineteen twenties, alcohol use had become a symbolic arena for a more general conflict within the American middle class. Prohibition, originally adopted with strong popular support, ended up making drinking a perfect symbol of generational revolt, "the symbol of a sacred cause," as Liebling recalled. Although Prohibition had reduced overall consumption by two-thirds at its most effective, and still by one-third around nineteen thirty, middle-class consumption levels as a whole were little affected.

In the years before Prohibition, the movies had been seen as a great support for the cause of "temperance," both by the "wets" and - more mistakenly - by the "drys." First of all, they were the most attractive substitutes ever devised for saloons and bars. At the time, Chicago police officials estimated that they had cut the saloon trade in half in a North Side district. Temperance melodramas, a specific genre that reached great popularity until the end of the nineteen twenties, aimed to combat alcohol consumption and support Prohibition. 'Dry propaganda' films such as Prohibition, from nineteen fifteen, directed by Hal Reid, Distilled Spirits, from nineteen fifteen, directed by Milton J. Fahrney, and The Curse of Drink, from nineteen twenty-two, directed by Harry O. Hoyt, are examples of this genre.

But this changed in the late nineteen twenties. Ten Nights in a Barroom was released in two versions at the time, in nineteen twenty-six and nineteen thirty, and the nineteen thirty version already presented the view of a population opposed to the law. In one scene, the town doctor looks back on the old days and tells his cronies, who are turning against Prohibition, about the evils of the old-time saloon.

## [04] Learn more: Prohibition in the US (2)  (762 car., ~0.8 min)

The effect of the law was the complete opposite of what was expected. Instead of ending alcohol consumption, social problems and other ills, the law led to the discrediting of the authorities, increased corruption, crime surges in several states and the enrichment of the mafias that controlled the bootlegging of alcoholic beverages. The meeting places for people who wanted to drink were clandestine underground bars, designed not to draw attention. Arguing that legalizing alcohol would create more jobs, boost the economy and increase tax revenue, opponents of then-U.S. president Franklin Roosevelt convinced him to ask the United States Congress to legalize beer. As a result, in nineteen thirty-three the Prohibition constitutional amendment was repealed.

## [05] Clark Gable  (1473 car., ~1.5 min)

Scarface, written by Hecht, was based on the story of crime boss Al Capone (played with flamboyant affectation by Muni). "I'd like to do the Capone family as if they were the Borgias set down in Chicago," Hawks told Hecht. Unlike earlier films in the genre, Scarface did not slip past the censors. "The American public and the conscientious State Boards of Censorship find gangsters and hoodlums repugnant. Gangsterism must not be mentioned in the cinema," the Hays Office informed Hawks on the eve of production. Hawks shot the film anyway, but afterward he and producer Howard Hughes faced a Herculean battle to secure its release.

The concessions demanded of Hawks and Hughes before Scarface could reach theaters proved a discouraging example for future producers of gangster films. The censors wanted the film retitled "The Shame of a Nation" and wanted the antihero to reveal himself as a coward in the final scenes and be hanged. Yet the censors could hardly accuse Hollywood of inventing its gangster stories at a time when such stories were constantly on the front pages of the newspapers. The line between real-life criminals and their celluloid counterparts often seemed thin. John Dillinger, one of the most famous gangsters of the period, was shot dead in nineteen thirty-four as he left a Chicago movie theater, where he had gone to see a gangster picture starring Clark Gable (nineteen oh-one to nineteen sixty), Manhattan Melodrama, from nineteen thirty-four.

## [06] Angels with Dirty Faces  (1386 car., ~1.4 min)

There is an essential tension in the gangster film that defines it as a genre. This tension is obvious from the very first of them, but it is especially prevalent in the nineteen thirties. On the one hand, its heroes are glamorous, charismatic figures with whom audiences easily identify. But, on the other, they invariably turn out to be cruel, hateful killers who leave a trail of physical and emotional destruction wherever they go.

That is why they must be punished - and why Cagney's gangster in Angels with Dirty Faces, from nineteen thirty-eight, goes so cowardly to the electric chair. Or at least he seems to. The film ends ambiguously: the viewer will never know whether Cagney is pretending to be afraid out of respect for his childhood friend Jerry Connolly (Pat O'Brien) - Connolly, now a priest, had asked him to die whimpering so that the kids he is trying to keep on the right side of the law won't idolize him - or whether he is truly terrified.

Gangster antiheroes hold a special fascination for those who wish to criticize the status quo. This was made clear in Robert Warshow's influential essay "The Gangster as Tragic Hero", from nineteen forty-eight, written just after the genre's golden age in the nineteen thirties and early nineteen forties. He argued that gangster films were "an expression of that part of the American psyche which (...) rejects Americanism".

## [07] Edwin S. Porter  (2181 car., ~2.2 min)

The United States as a political and social organization may have been committed to "a cheerful view of life," but Warshow believed that, deep down, this view was subverted by these films. He wrote about Hollywood's gangsters as if they were Shakespearean characters - heroes with fatal flaws. If gangster films created a new kind of Hollywood protagonist - the tough, urban, working-class hero - they also made America's obsession with violence abundantly clear.

Guns had been part of American films since Edwin S. Porter's (eighteen seventy to nineteen forty-one) The Great Train Robbery, from nineteen oh-three, but the boom of the early nineteen thirties came just as Hollywood was making the transition to talkies - and only sound films could do justice to the "rat-a-tat-tat" of machine-gun fire from both FBI agents and gangsters. The gangsters - and especially those played by Cagney - used dialogue in an equally reckless and aggressive way. The best films of the genre also had the same vulgarity and realistic characterization found in the slangy short stories written by Damon Runyon (eighteen eighty to nineteen forty-six) and Ring Lardner (eighteen eighty-five to nineteen thirty-three) during the period.

Worried about the growing fame of criminals such as John Dillinger, the Hays Office censors banned gangster films in nineteen thirty-five. Hollywood's response was to move the bandit, or at least the actor who played him, to the side of the law. 'G' Men, from nineteen thirty-five, starred Cagney, one of the archetypal screen gangsters, now turned government agent as a lawyer working for the FBI.

In Hollywood, the brooding self-awareness of film noir only began to replace the genre in the nineteen forties. In this respect, tracing Humphrey Bogart's career is quite instructive. In The Petrified Forest, from nineteen thirty-six, he was Duke Mantee, a world-famous killer. In Dead End, from nineteen thirty-seven, Angels with Dirty Faces, from nineteen thirty-eight, and The Roaring Twenties, from nineteen thirty-nine, he was still on the side of the bad guys.

## [08] The Big Heat  (1125 car., ~1.1 min)

However, his screen persona gradually changed. He began playing private detectives instead of criminals. As he metamorphosed, so did the genre. Productions such as Scarface and Little Caesar gave way to films in which detectives, not gangsters, were the heroes. Still, the bandits of Hollywood films of the nineteen twenties and nineteen thirties were also businessmen of a sort. Prohibition offered them a market gap that they exploited with the power of the gun. The Depression created an intimidating economic environment in which the criminal lifestyle seemed attractive.

By the nineteen fifties, Prohibition was no longer in effect and the United States was going through a more prosperous time. Gangsters became the scum of society. The level of sadistic violence began to rise: in The Big Heat, from nineteen fifty-three, Lee Marvin throws a cup of scalding coffee in Gloria Grahame's face. In nineteen seventy-three, in The Long Goodbye, from nineteen seventy-three, Mark Rydell smashes a bottle into his girlfriend's face - not because she did anything wrong, but just to warn Philip Marlowe, played by Elliott Gould.
