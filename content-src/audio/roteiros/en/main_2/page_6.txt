## [00] Opening  (302 car., ~0.3 min)

Part zero two: From nineteen thirty to nineteen thirty-nine. Chapter six: Hollywood in the Shadow of War.

Even before the United States entered the war, Hollywood had been turning out a series of films with propagandistic leanings and, weeks before Pearl Harbor, was already making patriotic war movies.

## [01] All Quiet on the Western Front  (2331 car., ~2.3 min)

All Quiet on the Western Front, from nineteen thirty, a humanitarian drama about the First World War directed by Lewis Milestone (eighteen ninety-five to nineteen eighty) and based on the best-selling novel by Erich Maria Remarque, reflected the feelings of a generation of Americans who agreed with the lyrics of the song "I Didn't Raise My Boy to Be a Soldier." The film shows the conflict from the German point of view, and its villains are the politicians and civilians who use patriotism as an excuse to send young men to the front to die for nothing. The film also struck a deep chord with those who remembered the horrors of "the war to end all wars."

The star Lew Ayres (nineteen oh-eight to nineteen ninety-six) became so personally committed to pacifism that his career would later be damaged by the revelation that he remained opposed to World War Two even when the country already thought otherwise. For much of the nineteen thirties, despite, or because of, the news coming from Europe and Asia, films like The Man Who Reclaimed His Head, from nineteen thirty-four, portrayed world war as an evil promoted by dishonest politicians, corrupt arms dealers, and narrow-minded fanatics.

The rise of Nazism unsettled many people in Hollywood. Ironically, the studio heads, nearly all of them Jewish, had a personal style that might have made them more inclined to admire fascism, Harry Cohn of Columbia modeled his office on that of Benito Mussolini, were it not for the antisemitism that came with it. Producers such as Cohn, Walt Disney (a rare non-Jew in the industry), and Louis B. Mayer would have been far happier, as they were in the nineteen fifties, fighting the communists, who seemed to them a more serious personal threat (through the spread of labor unions) than the fascists. Hollywood also wanted to ensure the commercial success of its films in Europe.

The intellectual and inflamed anti-Hollywood rhetoric of both the left and the right in the nineteen thirties was also marked by antisemitism, so most of the studio heads preferred to keep quiet on political and religious matters. When Warner Bros. boldly produced The Life of Emile Zola, from nineteen thirty-seven, which recounts the French writer's involvement in the Dreyfus affair, the fact that the defendant was Jewish is barely mentioned in the film.

## [02] Learn more: Chaplin's inevitable parody  (1801 car., ~1.8 min)

Learn more: Chaplin's inevitable parody.

It seemed inevitable that at some point Charlie Chaplin would play Hitler on screen; the two had been born only days apart and shared a certain distinctive feature on their faces. As critic and filmmaker Basil Wright put it: "Hitler's mistake of wearing the same mustache as Charlie left the director no choice: he had to seize that marvelous opportunity." The Great Dictator has not aged as well as other anti-Nazi films of the period, such as Pastor Hall, from nineteen forty, and The Mortal Storm, from nineteen forty, but the mere fact of its existence and the stills of Chaplin in costume hold a fascination that goes far beyond anything achieved by the film itself.

The film cost Chaplin two million dollars of his own money and two years of work, owing to threatening letters and pressure from the Hays Office and United Artists to drop the project. Its premiere, on October fifteenth, nineteen forty, was a major event, as it was the first talkie by one of the few film artists considered capable of standing up to Hitler at the time. The standout in the cast is Henry Daniell as the arrogant Garbitsch (a parody of Goebbels). Despite (or perhaps because of) his disagreements with Chaplin on set, his scenes carry an air of menace absent from the rest of the film, as when he declares: "You must stir up the people's anger. At this moment, violence against the Jews might make them stop thinking about their own stomachs." Chaplin later admitted: "Had I known of the actual horrors of the German concentration camps, I could not have made The Great Dictator; I could not have made fun of the homicidal insanity of the Nazis." Naturally, the film was banned in Germany, but Hitler himself watched it twice, alone. There is no record of his opinion of it.

## [03] The Road Back  (1654 car., ~1.6 min)

Ironically, however, the popularity of All Quiet on the Western Front pushed Hollywood to make more films dealing with the situation in Germany at the time, in the hope of repeating the original's success. The Road Back, from nineteen thirty-seven, Three Comrades, from nineteen thirty-eight, and The Mortal Storm, from nineteen forty, are unofficial sequels to All Quiet on the Western Front and show postwar Germany taking a violent political turn to the right.

Warner Bros. was the most anti-Nazi studio of all, even closing its German branch, allegedly because antisemitic thugs had murdered its representative in the country. In nineteen thirty-nine, the studio produced the incendiary Confessions of a Nazi Spy, from nineteen thirty-nine, an exposé based on true events of German espionage in the United States that criticizes the Nazi leanings of the German American Bund, an American organization of sympathizers of German ultranationalism.

With another world war looking inevitable, the film industry responded by producing more films on the subject: Clarence Brown directed Idiot's Delight, from nineteen thirty-nine, based on the play Idiot's Delight by Robert Sherwood (eighteen ninety-six to nineteen fifty-five), which tells the story of a group of Americans stranded in Europe when war breaks out, and it was already in theaters just a few months after the situation it portrays had taken place. What it implies is that the United States could not avoid getting involved in the conflict.

## [04] Charlie Chaplin  (733 car., ~0.7 min)

American isolationists harshly criticized the way Hollywood seemed to lean toward the British side at the start of the war, especially when it came to English filmmakers such as Charlie Chaplin (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen seventy-seven) with The Great Dictator, from nineteen forty, and Alfred Hitchcock (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty) with Foreign Correspondent, from nineteen forty.

By nineteen forty-one, Hollywood was perhaps better prepared to face a war than the American Army or any national defense agency. Resigned to the loss of the European market and aligned with President Roosevelt's "Good Neighbor" policy, the industry invested in all kinds of attempts to expand, such as the ill-fated, unfinished one.
