## [00] Opening  (379 car., ~0.4 min)

Part zero two: From nineteen thirty to nineteen thirty-nine. Chapter four: The First Animated Films.

In nineteen oh-eight, Fantasmagorie, by Émile Cohl (eighteen fifty-seven to nineteen thirty-eight), fascinated audiences by showing what animation could do. Twenty-nine years later, the first animated feature film was released, opening the doors of imagination on the big screen.

## [01] Disney  (917 car., ~0.9 min)

German shadow-theater animator Lotte Reiniger (eighteen ninety-nine to nineteen eighty-one) achieved great success with the oldest surviving fully animated feature film: Die Abenteuer des Prinzen Achmed (The Adventures of Prince Achmed, nineteen twenty-six). Twenty years later, animated film's most famous son was born. He is an icon known throughout the world and has appeared in hundreds of short and feature films.

Mickey Mouse first appeared in nineteen twenty-eight, and the most famous of his cartoons from that period is Steamboat Willie, from nineteen twenty-eight, from that same year. The magic of the Disney studio's animation is evident in this early example of the form, with its catchy music and anthropomorphic characters. For decades, all the studio's artists had to do was put a dress on Minnie and a bow on her head to convince viewers that, despite being nearly identical to Mickey, she was a girl.

## [02] Nine Old Men  (1448 car., ~1.4 min)

In Disney's golden age, Walt Disney (nineteen oh-one to nineteen sixty-six) and his "Nine Old Men" (the studio's lead animators) worked tirelessly to produce cartoons for the masses and to create what would become the standard for animated feature films in the West.

Between nineteen thirty-seven and nineteen forty-two, they released Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, from nineteen thirty-seven, Pinocchio, from nineteen forty, Fantasia, from nineteen forty, Dumbo, from nineteen forty-one, and Bambi, from nineteen forty-two. Each of these films is a cultural landmark and, in its own way, a pioneer, as with Fantasia's blend of animation and stereophonic sound recording, or the balance between deeply emotional moments and dark passages in Pinocchio and Bambi.

At the same time, the Fleischer studio, MGM, and Warner Bros. were producing their own excellent animation. The Fleischer brothers created the legendary Betty Boop, whose mix of innocence and sensuality stirred controversy in shorts such as Betty Boop in Snow White, from nineteen thirty-three. Ever since, her look and her gangster-movie-moll persona have been endlessly parodied.

If Betty was the queen of the Fleischer studio, then Popeye was its king. Making his debut in Popeye the Sailor, from nineteen thirty-three, the squinty-eyed man of supernatural strength has a girlfriend who adores him, sports manly tattoos, and spends all his time smoking a pipe and getting into fights.

MGM established its legacy with Tom and Jerry's first appearance in the nineteen forty cartoon Puss Gets the Boot. Warner Bros., for its part, had its own line of animated films, with the Looney Tunes and Merrie Melodies series. These anarchic shorts were light-years away from Disney's sentimental style and meticulous, selective production.

Animators Chuck Jones (nineteen twelve to two thousand two) and Friz Freleng (nineteen oh-six to nineteen ninety-five) had the freedom to experiment, and, voiced by the incomparable Mel Blanc (nineteen oh-eight to nineteen eighty-nine), zany characters like Bugs Bunny and Daffy Duck offered an alternative to more conventional heroes like Mickey Mouse and Popeye. The only limit of animated film is the imagination, and for more than a century children and adults alike have been fascinated and entertained by animation.

## [04] Learn more: That's all, folks! The art of Warner Bros. (1)  (2169 car., ~2.2 min)

Learn more: That's all, folks! The art of Warner Bros..

Some of the most iconic animated characters in film history, such as Bugs Bunny, Daffy Duck, and Tweety, were created by animators who worked at Warner Bros. Besides entertaining several generations of children and adults, these animated shorts won several Academy Awards and have been incredibly influential on the animation industry in film and TV.

Warner Bros. Animation began when producer Leon Schlesinger (eighteen eighty-four to nineteen forty-nine), who was distantly related to the Warner Brothers who founded the studio bearing their name, hired two animators, Hugh Harman (nineteen oh-three to nineteen eighty-two) and Rudolf Ising (nineteen oh-three to nineteen ninety-two), veterans of Walt Disney's cartoon studio in Kansas City. Harman and Ising created the character Bosko, who starred in the studio's first theatrical animated short: Sinkin' in the Bathtub, from nineteen thirty.

Since Walt Disney Productions had launched its Silly Symphony series of cartoon shorts the year before, Schlesinger dubbed his shorts "Looney Tunes," since these shorts initially revolved around music. That short included a Warner Bros. character saying "That's all, folks!" to close the short, which later became the trademark of the studio's Porky Pig.

The following year, Warner Bros. launched a second series titled Merrie Melodies, which still revolved around music but featured songs from earlier Warner Bros. films to help sell recordings of those songs. After producing more than sixty cartoons for Warner Bros., the Harman and Ising team split from Schlesinger over salary disputes in nineteen thirty-three, taking their characters with them. The new star of Looney Tunes was initially Buddy, a Bosko look-alike. Buddy was replaced as the series' main star by another Bosko clone named Beans the Cat, who first appeared in nineteen thirty-five in the Technicolor short I Haven't Got a Hat, from nineteen thirty-five. However, a much more important character also appeared in that short: Porky Pig. Although Porky was much fatter than in later years, in his first appearance he already had his trademark stutter.

## [05] Learn more: That's all, folks! The art of Warner Bros. (2)  (2326 car., ~2.3 min)

One of the main reasons for Porky's success was the influence of animation director Tex Avery (nineteen oh-eight to nineteen eighty), who joined Leon Schlesinger Productions in nineteen thirty-five, and his team of animators, which included Bob Clampett (nineteen thirteen to nineteen eighty-four) and Chuck Jones. Under Avery's direction, Porky was redesigned to be "softer" as opposed to fat, and Avery encouraged his animators to develop distinct personalities for the characters that would allow them to stand on their own. He was also a great champion of including rapid-fire gags in every short, a style that would define Warner Bros. cartoons for decades.

Avery's team created other great Warner Bros. cartoon stars in the years that followed, including Daffy Duck, in Porky's Duck Hunt, from nineteen thirty-seven, Egghead (who would later become Elmer Fudd), in Egghead Rides Again, from nineteen thirty-seven, and, after introducing several rabbit characters in earlier shorts, Bugs Bunny, in A Wild Hare, from nineteen forty. Adding to their appeal was voice actor Mel Blanc, "The Man of a Thousand Voices," who gave Porky, Daffy, Elmer Fudd, Bugs, and countless other Warner Bros. cartoon characters their distinctive voices.

Avery left Warner Bros. for MGM in nineteen forty-two, and as the studio's cartoons grew in popularity in the early nineteen forties, Warner Bros. bought Schlesinger's studio outright and renamed it "Warner Bros. Cartoons, Inc." in nineteen forty-four. Chuck Jones became the studio's most influential animator, and his cartoons introduced several major characters, including Pepé Le Pew, Marvin the Martian, Wile E. Coyote, and the Road Runner. Other important characters introduced during this period include Tweety (in nineteen forty-two's A Tale of Two Kitties), Sylvester (in nineteen forty-five's Life with Feathers), Yosemite Sam (in nineteen forty-five), and Foghorn Leghorn (in nineteen forty-six's Walky Talky Hawky). Chuck Jones and other great animators delivered Warner Bros.' greatest classics between the forties and the sixties, such as What's Opera, Doc?, from nineteen fifty-seven, Duck Amuck, from nineteen fifty-three, Duck Dodgers in the 24½th Century, from nineteen fifty-three, One Froggy Evening, from nineteen fifty-five, and Rabbit of Seville, from nineteen fifty.

## [06] The Rescuers  (849 car., ~0.8 min)

As critic C. A. Lejeune observed in her review of Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, written at the time of its release: "To be frank, it is at times rather badly drawn. But I do not believe any film of its generation will bring so much pleasure, however simple, to so many people."

The "Nine Old Men," as Walt Disney himself called them, were the lead animators of Walt Disney Productions during its Golden Age, some of whom later became directors, and they created some of Disney's most famous animated films, from the first feature in history, Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, to The Rescuers, from nineteen seventy-seven. Disney delegated more and more tasks to them in the animation department in the early nineteen fifties, as his interests expanded and diversified in scope. All are recognized as legends at Disney and in the world of animation.

## [07] The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation  (1838 car., ~1.8 min)

The work done by these nine animators is still used today as a reference in modern animation, and based on the studies they carried out in the thirties and forties, a template was developed and explained in the book The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation, written by Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston, two of the members of the Nine Old Men. The book presents the twelve basic principles of animation: Squash and stretch, Anticipation, Staging, Straight Ahead, Action and Pose to Pose, Follow Through and Overlapping, Action Slow In and Slow Out, Arcs, Secondary Action, Timing, Exaggeration, Solid Drawing and Appeal.

Les Clark (nineteen oh-seven to nineteen seventy-nine): There's no doubt that Les Clark's talent earned him a job at the Disney studio, but his nerve also played an important part. As a high school student, Clark worked at a lunch counter where Walt and Roy Disney (eighteen ninety-three to nineteen seventy-one) often ate. While waiting on them one day, Clark asked Walt for a job; Disney was surprisingly receptive, asking to see some of his work. "So I copied some drawings and showed them to Walt. He said I had a good line and why don't I come to work on Monday," Clark recalled. Clark graduated that Thursday and went to work for Walt Disney four days later. He assisted animator Ub Iwerks (nineteen oh-one to nineteen seventy-one) on the first Mickey Mouse shorts, Plane Crazy, from nineteen twenty-eight, and Steamboat Willie, from nineteen twenty-eight.

Marc Davis (nineteen thirteen to two thousand): In nineteen thirty-five, Davis was looking for work as a newspaper cartoonist when he noticed that Disney Studios was hiring. Although he had no animation experience, Disney was impressed by his sketches of anatomy and animal movement and hired Davis as an apprentice animator on Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs.

## [08] Ollie Johnston (1912-2008)  (718 car., ~0.7 min)

Ollie Johnston (nineteen twelve to two thousand eight): Joining the Disney Studios as a newcomer in nineteen thirty-five alongside Marc Davis was Ollie Johnston, who was also hired as an apprentice animator. One of his first projects was a Silly Symphonies short called The Tortoise and the Hare, from nineteen thirty-five, which won the Oscar for Best Short. Like Kimball, Johnston's lifelong hobby was live steam trains. Starting in nineteen forty-nine, he built a railroad in his backyard, with three one/twelve-scale locomotives (later owned by his sons). That railroad was one of the inspirations for Walt Disney to build his own backyard railroad, which later inspired the building of the railroad at Disneyland.

## [09] Milt Kahl (1909 -1987)  (757 car., ~0.8 min)

Milt Kahl (nineteen oh-nine to nineteen eighty-seven): Kahl is considered one of the greatest masters in the history of animation, and was the mentor of Richard Williams, author of the book The Animator's Survival Kit and responsible for the animation of Who Framed Roger Rabbit, from nineteen eighty-eight. Like Marc Davis, Kahl hoped to find work as a cartoonist. He dropped out of high school to work at the Oakland Post-Enquirer and the San Francisco Bulletin, but after Kahl saw the short Three Little Pigs at a local theater, he decided to try his hand at animation. He was hired by Disney in nineteen thirty-four as an assistant animator on shorts such as Lonesome Ghosts, from nineteen thirty-seven, and The Ugly Duckling, from nineteen thirty-nine.

## [10] Ward Kimball (1914 -2002)  (2304 car., ~2.3 min)

Ward Kimball (nineteen fourteen to two thousand two): Kimball, like Kahl, was inspired to become an animator by Disney's short Three Little Pigs. After graduating from the Santa Barbara School of the Arts, he found a job at Disney Studios in nineteen thirty-four and worked on most of its animated films until his retirement in nineteen seventy-two. Kimball was famous for his sense of humor. He enjoyed perpetuating the rumor that Walt Disney had been frozen: "I always tell people that if anybody was going to be frozen, it would be Walt. Knowing Walt, he was always interested in experiments, always interested in science. He was always interested in 'new' things. He had that kind of personality..."

Wolfgang Reitherman (nineteen oh-nine to nineteen eighty-five): Wolfgang "Woolie" Reitherman was set on a career as an aircraft engineer when he suddenly became enchanted with watercolors. He transferred from Pasadena Junior College to the Chouinard Art Institute in LA, where he met an instructor who happened to also teach at the Walt Disney Studio. Reitherman landed a job there in nineteen thirty-three.

Frank Thomas (nineteen twelve to two thousand four): When Frank Thomas was just nine years old, he asked his father how he could make money drawing pictures. Whether he was set on the career from a very young age or just uncannily prophetic, Thomas graduated from Stanford and then went on to study at Chouinard. A fellow student told Thomas that Disney was hiring, and on September twenty-fourth, nineteen thirty-four, he started work on a short called Mickey's Elephant, from nineteen thirty-six.

Eric Larson (nineteen oh-five to nineteen eighty-eight): Eric studied journalism at the University of Utah and, as he became more involved with the campus magazine, he also grew interested in sketching. After freelancing for a year after graduation, Larson decided, on a friend's advice, to send some of his sketches to Disney. When he retired in nineteen eighty-six, he had been with the company for fifty-two years, one of the longest tenures at Disney. His training program at Disney included names such as John Lasseter (one of the founders of Pixar), Brad Bird (director of The Incredibles), Don Bluth, Tim Burton, Glen Keane, Henry Selick, Andreas Deja, Ron Clements, and many, many others.

## [11] John Lounsbery (1911-1977)  (488 car., ~0.5 min)

John Lounsbery (nineteen eleven to nineteen seventy-seven): Lounsbery's story is very similar to those of his colleagues: he graduated from an art institute (in Denver) and then went to Los Angeles looking for work. While he was taking some illustration courses at the Art Center School of Design, an instructor there thought he would be a good fit for Disney and pointed him toward the studio, which was hiring. Lounsbery joined in nineteen thirty-five, specializing in the Pluto shorts.
