Part zero two: From nineteen thirty to nineteen thirty-nine. Chapter one: Gothic Horror.

The first decades of film history brought many attempts to adapt Gothic novels for the screen, and works such as Frankenstein and Dracula still shape the popular imagination today.

## [01] The Castle of Otranto (1764)  (985 car., ~1.0 min)

In its early decades, the history of horror cinema is directly tied to the Gothic horror novel. The Castle of Otranto, from seventeen sixty-four, and The Mysteries of Udolpho, from seventeen ninety-four, developed a genre of literature that became popular for the way it blended romance and horror. Its popularity peaked with Mary Shelley's masterpiece, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus, from eighteen eighteen.

Later manifestations include the work of Edgar Allan Poe and the music of Hector Berlioz, which combined allusions to exoticism, heresy and the supernatural with an atmosphere of pessimism and foreboding. Other typical elements in depictions of madness and a certain distrust of new technologies. By eighteen ninety-seven, when Bram Stoker's Dracula was published, the stylistic and thematic features of Gothic horror had already influenced many kinds of narratives, among them Robert Louis Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, from eighteen eighty-six.

## [02] Fritz Lang  (1392 car., ~1.4 min)

In its early days, cinema brought us several attempts to adapt Gothic novels for the screen, but it was only in the nineteen twenties that a tradition of Gothic-inspired horror films was established, with films that owed a great deal to the genre's books written in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and to the dark, gloomy aura of German Expressionist films, such as Fritz Lang's (eighteen ninety to nineteen seventy-six) Der müde Tod (Destiny, nineteen twenty-one) and F. W. Murnau's (eighteen eighty-eight to nineteen thirty-one) Nosferatu, from nineteen twenty-two.

Hollywood's forays into Gothic horror followed the example of these films. Immigrant directors such as Paul Leni (eighteen eighty-five to nineteen twenty-nine), with The Cat and the Canary, from nineteen twenty-seven, and Benjamin Christensen (eighteen seventy-nine to nineteen fifty-nine), with Seven Footprints to Satan, from nineteen twenty-nine, introduced the German style to Hollywood. Advances in makeup effects and set design are most evident in the films of actor Lon Chaney, among them The Hunchback of Notre Dame, from nineteen twenty-three, one of the first cinematic examples of the blend of Grand Guignol, Gothic romance, freak show, pantomime and vaudeville.

## [03] The Phantom of the Opera  (765 car., ~0.8 min)

Chaney's portrayals of disfigured and deranged characters (for which he created his own makeup) - often in settings of hair-raising Gothic architecture, as in The Phantom of the Opera, from nineteen twenty-five, and London After Midnight, from nineteen twenty-seven, - caused a sensation, with audiences literally cowering in fear before the latest frightening creation of the "man of a thousand faces."

Before the rise of the Hays Office, the censorship body created by the Hollywood studios themselves, put a stop to experiments with suggestive themes and explicit violence, the early sound era was a prosperous period for horror cinema, including films such as Paramount's Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde, from nineteen thirty-one, famous for its strong sexual overtones.

## [04] Universal  (1291 car., ~1.3 min)

Most of the genre's films at the time were produced by Universal, and the studio released two films in nineteen thirty-one: Dracula, from nineteen thirty-one, and Frankenstein, from nineteen thirty-one, which decisively established the themes, style and inspirations of Gothic horror. Dracula was directed by Tod Browning (eighteen eighty to nineteen sixty-two), who had worked with Chaney on several films before the actor's untimely death. It was an adaptation of both Stoker's book and the Broadway play that had made its star, Bela Lugosi, famous in nineteen twenty-seven. However, the most famous and iconic Gothic horror film of all is Frankenstein, directed by James Whale (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen fifty-seven).

The story of a scientist, Henry Frankenstein, who successfully carries out the experiment of reanimating the assembled parts of several corpses, focuses on the Monster, played by Boris Karloff (eighteen eighty-seven to nineteen sixty-nine) - a gentle but clumsy creature who accidentally kills a little girl and is then hunted down by the inhabitants of a village. While Dracula summed up everything horror cinema could do, Frankenstein added an important new element to the genre: a feeling of pity for the figure of the monster, portrayed as a victim of society.

## [05] Murders in the Rue Morgue  (1043 car., ~1.0 min)

The success of Dracula and Frankenstein set off a flood of films that tried to capitalize on their storytelling technique and style. Universal's eagerness to repeat its hits crystallized the conventions and iconography of the horror film in the public imagination. Films such as Murders in the Rue Morgue, from nineteen thirty-two, starring Bela Lugosi (eighteen eighty-two to nineteen fifty-six), The Mummy, from nineteen thirty-two, and Bride of Frankenstein, from nineteen thirty-five, both with Karloff, offer slight variations on the model but remain within the Gothic archetype in their plots and in their atmosphere, heightened by exotic locations, macabre props and bizarre supporting characters.

One of the genre's most significant innovations was the deepening of female roles. Productions came up, ever more creatively, with objects of impure desire around which the stories revolved, which made the men - at least for most viewers - seem to exude sexual repression, indecency, anxiety and hysteria on the part of both men and women.

## [06] Fay Wray  (1337 car., ~1.3 min)

The visceral, piercing scream of the helpless victim or heroine was first let loose by Fay Wray (nineteen oh-seven to two thousand four) as the intrepid blonde captive Ann Darrow facing Kong, a giant gorilla, in King Kong, from nineteen thirty-three, produced by RKO and directed by Merian C. Cooper (eighteen ninety-three to nineteen seventy-three) and Ernest B Schoedsack (eighteen ninety-three to nineteen seventy-nine).

She was encouraged to rehearse her scream, which became the perfect example of the sexual innuendo evoked by this kind of film. Because of it, Wray became known as "the scream queen." King Kong is also famous for its advances in special effects, having used stop-motion technology to animate the dinosaurs and the giant Kong. The fact that Universal drew inspiration from Gothic works made the horror genre a kind of "adult" cinema.

However, not all Gothic horror films were produced under the aegis of Universal. Productions as diverse as White Zombie, from nineteen thirty-two, Freaks, from nineteen thirty-two, The Ghoul, from nineteen thirty-three, Cat People, from nineteen forty-two, and I Walked with a Zombie, from nineteen forty-three, were made by other studios, usually independent ones, and accused of being "Frankenstein knockoffs" trying to outdo the original. Freaks, in particular, gained notoriety.

## [07] Learn more: A Controversial Masterpiece  (1725 car., ~1.7 min)

Learn more: A Controversial Masterpiece.

Tod Browning's Freaks, one of the most shocking and bizarre films ever produced by a major Hollywood studio, fires in two radically different directions at once. It is both an openly exploitative film (advertised as "the most startling horror story of the abnormal and unwanted") and a work that treats its deformed protagonists with considerable sensitivity. MGM's intention was clear from the start: "I want something that out-horrors Frankenstein," studio executive Irving Thalberg reportedly told the filmmakers, aware of the success Universal had just had with its film.

Browning had directed Dracula for Universal and, although he had a morbid and strange sensibility, he had a strong, clear bond of empathy with his characters. MGM's biggest stars wanted nothing to do with a film like this. Myrna Loy (nineteen oh-five to nineteen eighty-three), originally cast as Cleopatra, the trapeze artist, begged the studio bosses to release her from the role. She was replaced by Olga Baclanova (eighteen ninety-three to nineteen seventy-four).

The action takes place in a traveling circus full of clowns, acrobats and "freaks". Hans (Harry Earles), an elegant little person, has just inherited a fortune. Cleopatra tries to ruin his relationship with his fiancée Frieda (Daisy Earles), while having an affair with Hercules (Henry Victor), the troupe's strongman. She tries to poison Hans, but is caught in the act - and the freaks carry out a terrible revenge.

After a few disastrous preview screenings, it was heavily edited. Even so, it was received with hostility. It was only with the arrival of the nineteen sixties and the counterculture era that it reached its current cult status.

## [08] The Bat  (1032 car., ~1.0 min)

I Walked with a Zombie also stood out, but for different reasons. As part of a cycle of horror films produced by Val Lewton (nineteen oh-four to nineteen fifty-one) and distributed by RKO, it was promoted like any exploitative franchise. However, the film features no "marketable" monster and has almost no explicit violence, although it is full of sexual overtones. It internalizes much of the horror that earlier films in the genre showed explicitly - and this has the effect of heightening any anxiety it generates.

With the end of World War One, during the nineteen twenties, horror took over theater stages across the United States. Productions such as The Bat, The Monster and The Cat and The Canary began to be staged and became huge hits until, in nineteen twenty-seven, a certain adaptation of a book by the Irishman Abraham Stoker opened on Broadway, frightening and captivating audiences once and for all. Dracula, produced by Horace Liveright, premiered, and the play grossed around two million dollars at the box office.

## [09] Universal Monsters  (1489 car., ~1.5 min)

The success of the adaptation, combined with the strong influence of German Expressionism and the postwar economic crisis, and multiplied by audiences' avid desire for horror as an escape valve or as a metaphorical portrayal of nightmares of repressed violence and anguish, produced the perfect equation to encourage a new generation of producers to take a chance on a new trend in Hollywood: the horror film.

It was in this setting that Carl Laemmle Jr. (nineteen oh-eight to nineteen seventy-nine), son and heir of the man who founded Universal Pictures, who took over the studio in nineteen twenty-eight, decided to bring Dracula from the stage to the screen in nineteen thirty-one - following in the footsteps of his father who, in the previous decade, had ventured during the silent era into adapting The Hunchback of Notre Dame, from nineteen twenty-three, and The Phantom of the Opera, from nineteen twenty-five, - and kicked off the cycle of the famous Universal Monsters, which produced thirty-three films released over two decades, with the Gill-man, the creature from Creature from the Black Lagoon, as its last representative.

The studio promoted a series of legendary franchises that established it as the bastion of Gothic horror, such as Dracula's Daughter, from nineteen thirty-six, starring Gloria Holden (nineteen oh-three to nineteen ninety-one) as Countess Marya Zaleska, the vampire's daughter - a character who, with her hints of lesbianism, drew on the vamp tradition.

## [10] Son of Frankenstein  (2174 car., ~2.2 min)

In later years, Universal's horror output took on many facets, as in Son of Frankenstein, from nineteen thirty-nine, House of Dracula, from nineteen forty-five, and even the comedy Bud Abbott and Lou Costello Meet Frankenstein, from nineteen forty-eight. These productions tended to dissociate their characters from the less Gothic and less menacing films. This also caused the original films to which they were initially tied - Dracula and Frankenstein - by extension, to drift away from their Gothic aura, in the concept of the vampire and the monster. Removing the characters from their habitat gave the studio's horror films the status of "elders of the tribe" - a deference illustrated by the unshakable dignity of the "Universal horror" label and by the admiration for original, groundbreaking actors such as Lugosi and Karloff.

said Mordaunt Hall on Frankenstein, nineteen thirty-one.

An essential element in the success of the studio's films was the team involved in these productions. In James Whale, a World War One veteran, Universal found a visionary filmmaker whose imagination and keen (and often peculiar) sense of humor added a great deal to the films he directed. Another figure no less crucial to the success of the monster films was the groundbreaking makeup artist Jack P. Pierce (eighteen eighty-nine to nineteen sixty-eight). It was he who created the Monster's look, with its square head and neck bolts, in addition to devising a series of different and innovative makeups for Bride of Frankenstein and The Wolf Man, from nineteen forty-one, among others.

In Bride of Frankenstein, Pierce modified the creature's original look to depict the effects of a fire, adding scars and shortening its hair; in addition, he gradually altered the makeup over the course of the film to indicate that its wounds were healing. The makeup sessions were usually long and laborious. On Bride of Frankenstein, Pierce worked seven hours a day over thirty-two days of shooting to create the two monsters. The result was spectacular and ensured that Universal's creatures became icons.
