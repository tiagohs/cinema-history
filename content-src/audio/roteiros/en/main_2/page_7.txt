Part zero two: From nineteen thirty to nineteen thirty-nine. Chapter seven: Depression-Era Musicals.

Musicals dominated movie theaters in cinema's early decades, and in the nineteen thirties they captured the recklessness and feverish energy people showed in the face of the Great Depression.

## [01] Wall Street Crash  (779 car., ~0.8 min)

The Wall Street Crash of October nineteen twenty-nine ushered in an era of despair and soaring unemployment in the United States. By nineteen thirty-three, an estimated fourteen million working-age Americans were out of work.

"The long lost dollar has come back to the fold / With silver you can turn your dreams to gold," went the lyrics of "We're in the Money" in the sequence choreographed by Busby Berkeley in Gold Diggers of nineteen thirty-three, from nineteen thirty-three. Money is celebrated precisely because so many people had so little of it. The cheer of the chorus girls dressed in coins seems forced, and the analogy between sex and money is obvious. Still, it would be a mistake to think that nineteen thirties musicals were only about opulence, sex, and escapism.

## [02] 42nd Street  (1041 car., ~1.0 min)

In such a bleak climate, dancing was an act of resistance. In Forty-second Street, from nineteen thirty-three, Berkeley's final sequence shows police officers, gangsters, and shopkeepers dancing, as if to keep the troubles of the day at bay. The backstage musicals of the period, among them Forty-second Street and Footlight Parade, from nineteen thirty-three, were built on the idea that rehearsals demanded "sacrifice, blood, and sweat."

There were two kinds of musicals in the nineteen thirties. On one side, the Warner Bros. productions with Berkeley's fabulously expressionistic choreography and, on the other, the films in which the stars themselves did the dancing. As Fred Astaire famously put it: "Either the camera will dance, or I will." In Astaire's films, the star usually stayed in the center of the frame in long takes, so that viewers could appreciate his moves.

## [03] Top Hat  (926 car., ~0.9 min)

In Berkeley's sequences, the dancers were part of extremely complex arrangements that the choreographer conceived for the screen. The camera swept around the dancers, above them, and sometimes even between their legs. Audiences had no way of knowing whether the performers could really dance. With Astaire, however, there was not the slightest doubt. His legs were reportedly insured for one million dollars, and the star even persuaded technicians to design new equipment to film him dancing.

Astaire gave the impression of performing his numbers effortlessly. In films like Top Hat, from nineteen thirty-five, he always wore a cheerful smile while Ginger Rogers (nineteen eleven to nineteen ninety-five) looked radiant, as if the pair were dancing spontaneously. Depression-era audiences could get swept up in musical sequences that conveyed the most complete lightheartedness, yet there was nothing easy about these numbers.

## [04] Learn more: The Actor Who Changed Cinema  (1391 car., ~1.4 min)

Learn more: The Actor Who Changed Cinema.

Considered the most influential dancer in film history, Fred Astaire was one of the most important figures of the golden age of musicals.

His career on stage and later in film and television spanned a total of seventy-six years. He starred in more than ten Broadway and London musicals, made thirty-one musical films and four television specials, and released countless songs. As a dancer, his most striking traits were his uncanny sense of rhythm, his perfectionism, and his innovation. His favorite and most memorable dance partnership was with Ginger Rogers (nineteen eleven to nineteen ninety-five), with whom he co-starred in a series of ten Hollywood musicals. The American Film Institute named Astaire the fifth greatest male star of classic Hollywood cinema in its "one hundred Years...one hundred Stars" list.

Gene Kelly (nineteen twelve to nineteen ninety-six), another renowned star of dance on film, said that "the history of dance on film begins with Astaire." He later stated that Astaire was "the only one of today's dancers who will be remembered." Beyond film and television, many dancers and choreographers, including Rudolf Nureyev, Sammy Davis Jr., Michael Jackson, Gregory Hines and Mikhail Baryshnikov, George Balanchine, Jerome Robbins, Madhuri Dixit, and Bob Fosse, who called Astaire an "idol," also acknowledged his influence.

## [05] Swing Time  (797 car., ~0.8 min)

Astaire was relentless with himself and quite demanding with Rogers. He is estimated to have worn out at least a thousand pairs of dancing shoes over the course of his career. While filming Swing Time, from nineteen thirty-six, this extreme perfectionist made Rogers rehearse a difficult sequence so many times that her feet reportedly bled in her silk slippers.

Another difference between Astaire's musicals and the films featuring Berkeley's army of chorus girls is that the former are rooted in individualism, while the latter celebrate the collective. Some theorists have suggested that Berkeley was either an ideologue of the New Deal, extolling the merits of the collective, or a "dirty old man" whose camera was a leering eye peeping at the voluptuous chorus girls from every possible angle.
